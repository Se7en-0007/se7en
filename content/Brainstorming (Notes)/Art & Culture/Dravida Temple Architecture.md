## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">INTRODUCTION</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Three Major Temple Styles of India</mark>

The major temple styles listed and described in the Vastu Shastra texts are:
1) **Nagara** - the North Indian style
2) **Dravida** - the South Indian style
3) **Vesara** - the mixed or hybrid Deccan style. 

Among these, the prime position in the texts is assigned to the Nagara style of north India as the leading style. Next in importance is the Dravida of South India. The Vesara is the mixed style of the Deccan and was still in an experimental stage when the 10th–11th century texts were composed and when temple architecture was at its climax. The Deccan was the main zone of the evolution of the Vesara form with variations based on sub-regions and their dynastic preferences.

The classification of the three styles shows that they are generally named after the various regional schools and classified according to their superstructures.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Dravidian Art - A Purely Indigenous Tradition</mark>

The Dravidian art of South India is of special interest as the art appears to be **wholly indigenous, untouched by foreign influence**. It comprises only architecture and sculpture, because no paintings worth the name have survived. It begins at the close of the sixth century under the rule of the Pallavas. Earlier works were executed mostly in wood and other perishable materials and have perished. But there is no doubt that they existed and that they served the useful purpose of training a school of artists who effected the transition from wood to stone. The magnificent temples and the remarkable sculptures on the rocks of Mamallapuram executed by the Pallavas could not have been the works of novices. Their very excellence indicates a mature skill acquired by generations of practice.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">ORIGIN AND EARLY PRECURSORS OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Gupta Period - Beginning of Structural Temple Architecture</mark>

The Gupta period marks the beginning of structural temple architecture in India. Several well-defined types of early temples may be identified:

1. **Flat-roofed, square temple** with a shallow pillared porch in front
2. **Flat-roofed, square temple** with a covered ambulatory around the sanctum and preceded by a pillared porch, sometimes with a second storey above
3. **Square temple with a low and squat sikhara** tower above
4. **Rectangular temple with an apsidal back** and a barrel-vaulted roof above
5. **Circular temple** with shallow rectangular projections at the four cardinal faces

The full unit of a structural temple does not appear anywhere in India before AD 550, and the Bhitargaon temple was the earliest such temple and also the most outstanding example.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Precursor of the Dravida: The Second Type of Gupta Temple</mark>

A temple of South India has the **sanctum cella situated invariably within an ambulatory hall** and a **pyramidal tower** formed by an accumulation of storey after storey in receding dimensions. These are to be regarded as the distinctive characteristics of a Dravida temple. The **second type of Gupta storeyed temple**, showing the beginnings of such a ground-plan and elevation, may be identified as its precursor. This type is seen in:

- The **Parvati temple at Nachna Kuthara** (Madhya Pradesh)
- The **Siva temple at Bhumara** (Madhya Pradesh)
- The **Lad Khan at Aihole** (Karnataka)

Each comprises a flat-roofed square sanctum cella inside a similarly roofed bigger square hall. The bigger hall, with a covered ambulatory (pradakshina) around the inner sanctum, is preceded by a slightly smaller rectangular porch. In both the Parvati temple at Nachna Kuthara and the Lad Khan at Aihole there is an upper storey vimana above the inner chamber. In the following centuries, this type supposedly underwent further improvements and crystallised to form the Dravida style in the South.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Nucleus of the Dravida Temple</mark>

The **nucleus of the Dravida temple** is the storeyed form of the Gupta temple. The combination of a stepped pyramid and dome with a cubic or prismatic base, even in its primitive form, was a novel and pleasing ensemble. It was to become the hallmark of south Indian temple architecture, symbolising the Shaivite aspect of the Hindu trinity which enjoyed great popularity in the South, just as the Vaishnava type enjoyed in the North.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Badami Temple - An Essential Link</mark>

The ruined temple at **Badami** forms an essential link in tracing the design development of the South Indian vimana. The designer of this unnamed temple reorganised the rather disparate elements of the Lad Khan into an integrated whole. The garbhagriha, which had been an incongruously partitioned space in the rear of the Lad Khan, is appropriately relocated at the core of the composition. The aisles so formed around the cella became an enclosed pradakshina path, roofed, like in its prototype, with massive sloping slabs of stone. The ground plan is completed by the addition of a small mandapa or ante-room preceding the garbhagriha, and an entrance portico similar to that of the Lad Khan.

The tower now takes the form of a **stepped pyramid**, every step defined by horizontal moulding of varying thickness. Each one, though, is rounded off, as if to consciously remind the viewer of its origins from the gentle overhang of the thatch roofs over bamboo huts. The apex is crowned by a domical form derived from a rural one - the shallow, almost semicircular contours of a bamboo canopy, built up over a square base. With minimal modifications this curvilinear form was hewn out of massive blocks of masonry and planted squat over the tower.

The pyramidal tower - popularly known as the **vimana** in South Indian architectural terminology - crowning the temple of Badami, though, was far from perfect. The inexperienced hands of the stone mason, working with large unwieldy blocks of stone, had given birth to a new concept, but refinements of detail and the fluidity of the profile of the vimana was to be achieved by another group of craftsmen elsewhere in India.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">FUNDAMENTAL DISTINGUISHING FEATURES OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Defining Characteristics - Contrast with Nagara</mark>

The North Indian temple is a square one with a number of graduated projections (rathakas) in the middle of each face, which gives it a cruciform shape in the exterior. In elevation it exhibits a tower (sikhara), gradually inclining inwards and capped by a spheroid slab with ribs round the edge (amalaka). The cruciform ground-plan and the curvilinear tower are the salient features of a Nagara temple.

In contrast, a **Dravida temple** has:
- The **sanctum cella situated invariably within an ambulatory hall**
- A **pyramidal tower** formed by an accumulation of storey after storey in receding dimensions

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Compound Wall and Gopuram</mark>

Dravida temples, in contrast to Nagara temples, are **enclosed within a compound wall**. The entrance wall boasts a **central gateway known as a gopuram**. This is one of the most easily identifiable features of the Dravida style.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Vimana</mark>

The **vimana** is the main temple tower in Tamil Nadu. It takes the form of a **geometrically rising stepped pyramid**, differing from the curving shikhara of North India. In South Indian architectural terminology, the vimana represents the sanctum with its tall pyramidal tower. The term **shikhara** in South India refers only to the **crowning element**, usually shaped like a small stupika or an **octagonal cupola** - equivalent to the amalaka and kalasha of North Indian temples. This is a critical distinction: in the Nagara style, the entire curvilinear tower is the shikhara, whereas in the Dravida style, shikhara refers only to the finial at the top of the vimana.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Terraced Pyramidal Tower</mark>

The Dravidian style of architecture, especially in relation to the construction of temples, is characterised by a **terraced pyramidal tower** over which the dome is called the Sikhara. This lofty tower over the shrine dominates the whole structure, producing a pleasant spectacular effect.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Gopuram - The Immense Gateway</mark>

**Gopurams** or huge gateways to the temple enclosure constitute another important feature of the Dravidian style. In the later Chola art, the central shrine was reduced to insignificance while endless labour was lavished on mighty Gopuram. The result is unsatisfying, although the beholder is impressed by the imposing vastness of the structure.

Unlike North Indian temples with clusters of shikharas, South Indian temples often have the **main temple with one of the smallest towers**, indicating its historical significance. Over time, as towns expanded, **new boundary walls with loftier gopurams** were constructed around the temple.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Entrance Sculptures - Dvarapalas</mark>

Entrance sculptures in Dravida temples typically depict **fierce dvarapalas** (door-keepers) guarding the temple. In contrast, Nagara temples feature river goddesses Ganga and Yamuna at the entrance of a garbhagriha.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temple Tanks</mark>

Temple complexes in the Dravida style often include **large water reservoirs or temple tanks** - an essential feature of the sacred complex.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Construction Material</mark>

The temples were usually built of very large, **closely jointed blocks of stone without any mortar**. This was a defining characteristic of the construction technique.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">CLASSIFICATION AND SHAPES OF DRAVIDA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Five Shapes of Dravida Temples</mark>

Dravida temples are categorised into five shapes based on their plan:

1. **Kuta or Caturasra** - Square
2. **Shala or Ayatasra** - Rectangular
3. **Gaja-prishta or Vrittayata** - Elliptical
4. **Vritta** - Circular
5. **Ashtasra** - Octagonal

The **choice of temple plan and vimana shape** is influenced by the iconographic nature of the consecrated deity.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE TWIN FUNDAMENTAL FEATURES OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

In the rock-cut rathas of Mahabalipuram, one may recognise the origin of the **twin fundamental features** of the Dravida temple:

1. **The Vimana** - representing the sanctum with its tall pyramidal tower
2. **The Gopuram** - the immense pile of the gateway leading to the temple enclosure

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">EVOLUTION OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE - CHRONOLOGICAL PROGRESSION</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Pallava Period - Phase I: Rock-Cut Architecture (7th Century AD)</mark>

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Mahendra Group - Rock-Cut Cave Temples</mark>

The development of temple architecture, particularly the Dravida style, under the Pallavas can be seen in four stages. The first is the **Mahendra Group**, characterised by the influence of the cave style of architecture. **Mahendravarman I** is credited with the introduction of rock-cut temples in the Pallava territory. He claims in his **Mandagappattu inscription** that his shrine to Brahma, Isvara and Vishnu was made without using traditional materials such as brick, timber, metal and mortar.

Mahendravarman's rock-cut temples are usually the **mandapa type** - with a pillared hall or the mandapa in front and a small shrine at the rear or sides. In Tamil Nadu, the cave style was introduced in the 7th century by Mahendravarman I. Here the shrine usually takes the shape of a shallow rectangular hall or mandapa, with one or more cells cut further deep on one or more sides of the hall.

Examples of the Mahendra Group include:
- Rock-cut temples at **Bhairavakonda** (North Arcot District)
- **Anantesvara temple at Undavalli** (Guntur District)

Though born a Jaina, Mahendravarman was converted to Shaivism quite early in his life. It was under his patronage that a novel and significant experiment in the art of temple building in the south was carried out.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">The Guilds of Masons and Cutters</mark>

By the time of the Pallava architectural experiments, two distinct guilds of building craftsmen had evolved - that of **stone masons** and **stone carvers**, each proud of its own tradition and eager to challenge the other. Aware of the aesthetic shortcomings of the efforts of the former at Aihole and Badami, the latter were now commissioned by the Pallavas.

During the period of wars between the Chalukyas and Pallavas, skilled craftsmen either transferred their allegiance voluntarily or were often forcibly carried away from the kingdom of the vanquished to build temples for the victorious king. During this period there was, therefore, a **parallel and interrelated growth of temple architecture** in the cities of the Chalukyas and Pallavas, since the masons and sculptors who were shunted back and forth belonged to the same school of craftsmanship.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Narasimha Group - The Monolithic Rathas at Mahabalipuram</mark>

The **rock-cut rathas of Mahabalipuram** (7th century AD) supply an interesting stage in the evolution of the Dravida style. The rock-cut method of the initial phase was replaced by the structural one during the reign of Narasimhavarman II (also known as Rajasimha).

**Mahabalipuram** (also known as Mamallapuram) was the coastal capital of the early Pallavas. Situated at the mouth of the Palar river, it stood on a 100 ft (33.3 m) high, and a half mile (800 m) long hill of granite gneiss rising out of the surrounding sands. **Narasimhavarman I**, also known as Mamalla, ascended the Pallava throne around 640 CE, expanding the empire and initiating building works at Mahabalipuram.

Instead of carving caves out of the living rock as his forebears had done for centuries, the sculptor-architect, with surprising aplomb, proceeded to chisel down the granite outcrop into **free-standing monolithic models of structural buildings** - much like the romantic gardener who pares thick hedges into shapes of animals and birds. A small granite outcrop, about 250 ft (75.2 m) long and 30 ft (9.1 m) high, south of the fortifications of Mahabalipuram became the site of the most unusual experiment in the techniques of building, resulting in a form of architecture that flourished into a mature consummation in India.

##### The Eight Rathas

The stone carvers' perseverance resulted in the famous **seven pagodas or rathas** (literally, chariots) of Mahabalipuram. Due to the disposition of the outcrop - a narrow and long whale-backed hump of none-too-great dimension - the seven virtual architectural models are of moderate size and aligned along a single axis. Though for some obscure historic reasons the work of finishing and polishing the models was abandoned, the artists managed to present to the king an array of distinctly comprehensible temple forms. Each of these eight was subsequently named after the heroes of the great Hindu epic, the Mahabharata.

The rathas, popularly called the **Seven Pagodas**, are actually eight in number:
1. **Dharmaraja Ratha** - Built over a square plan, it was found to do the greatest aesthetic justice to all the formal, cosmological and ritual requirements of a temple. The finial of this finely proportioned and meticulously carved pyramid is an elegantly contoured ribbed octagonal dome, poised over a squat cylindrical shaft. It was ultimately adopted as the most appropriate form of the vimana to crown the South Indian Hindu temple. Each receding level (bhumi) of the pyramidal roof is in itself a miniature model of the Buddhist vihara.
2. **Bhima Ratha** - Rectangular in plan with beautiful sculptures of Harihara, Brahma, Vishnu, Skanda, Ardhanarisvara and Siva as Gangadhara.
3. **Arjuna Ratha** - Contains artistically carved sculptures of Siva, Vishnu, mithuna and dvarapala.
4. **Sahadeva Ratha** - Mounts a three-tiered roof over an apsidal-ended plan, culminating in the barrel-vaulted chaitya format at the apex.
5. **Draupadi Ratha** - The simplest of the so-called rathas, is an unpretentious version of the rural portable wooden shrine with a square base and curvilinear thatch roof.
6. **Ganesa Ratha** - A rectangular entrance portico is attached to the already complex ensemble of forms of the Sahadeva Ratha.
7. **Pidari Ratha**
8. **Valaiyankuttai Ratha**

##### Formal Innovations in the Rathas

Each of the rathas, except the Draupadi, **exhibits a storeyed elevation of the roof**, each storey terminating in a **convex rolled cornice, ornamented with chaitya window arches**. The walls of the ground storey are broken up by pilasters and sculptured niches, while the upper storeys are surrounded by small pavilions.

Three of the rathas are varying interpretations of the Buddhist chaitya hall. The most elementary of these is rectangular in plan with a peripheral colonnade covered with a barrel-vaulted roof.

The form of the **Dharmaraj** proved to be the king among all. Built over a square plan, each receding level of the pyramidal roof is in itself a miniature model of the Buddhist vihara. What were cells for the residence of the monk in the original are here frozen into either miniature ornamental domes or barrel-vaulted turrets. The place of the central open court of the living vihara is taken up by a solid core of rectangular masonry diminishing in size as it approaches the apex. The Chalukyan builder and the Pallava sculptor had thus between themselves evolved an appropriately aesthetic spire (vimana) for the garbhagriha of the South Indian temple.

The monolithic vimanas are peculiar to Mahabalipuram. The **most exquisite of the five** is the Dharmaraja Ratha, with a three-storied vimana and a square base.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Rajasimha Group - First Structural Temples</mark>

This group marks the transition from rock-cut to structural temple architecture. There are five examples in this group:
- At **Mahabalipuram** - Shore Temple, Isvara temple, and Mukunda temple
- At **Panamalai** in South Arcot
- The **Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram** - the most mature example

##### The Shore Temple, Mahabalipuram

The **Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram**, possibly the first structural temple to be built in the South, was constructed during the reign of Rajasimha (CE 700–728). The temple consists of **two shrines** (or three shrines per another source - two to Shiva and one to Vishnu/Anantashayana), symmetrically joined to each other. The exterior wall of the shrine dedicated to Vishnu and the interior of the boundary wall are elaborately carved and sculpted. The compound includes evidence of a water tank, an early gopuram, and sculptures of Nandi, though erosion has affected some carvings.

Unlike other structures of the region, the Shore Temple is a **five-storeyed rock-cut monolith**. It is one of the earliest and most important structural temples in southern India.

##### The Kailasanatha Temple, Kanchipuram

An **organic and unified conception of a temple scheme**, in which all the trappings of the Dravida style are clearly expressed and harmoniously adjusted to one another, first comes into view in the celebrated **Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram**, also built by Rajasimha. With all the appurtenances - the **walled court, the gopuram, the pillared mandapa and the vimana** - all complete and in their forms and positions, the Kailasanatha temple at Kanchipuram may be described as one of the **key monuments of the early Dravida style**.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Nandivarman Group - Later Pallava Architecture</mark>

The **Nandivarman Group** mostly consists of small temples except the **Vaikuntaperumal temple at Kanchipuram** and in no way forms an advance on the achievements of the previous age. But they are more ornate, resembling the Chola architecture.

Best examples are:
- The temples of **Muktesvara and Matangesvara** at Kanchipuram
- The **Vadamalisvara** at Orgadam near Chingalput
- The **Parasuramesvara** at Gudimallam near Renigunta

##### The Vaikuntaperumal Temple, Kanchipuram

A more developed sense of composition is clearly evident in the **Vaikunta Perumal temple at Kanchipuram**, built by Nandivarman II. The germs of the idea embodied in the Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal are carried to their fruition here. The entrance portico of the mandapa is dispensed with and greater body added to the mass of the cella by a pair instead of just one pradakshina path.

The **diminishing form of the spire of the Vaikunta Perumal** is the only example of an Indian temple that actually makes functional use of the substantial mass embodied in the profile of the vimana. The **vimana interior is actually designed as three distinct usable storeys**, each with its own central shrine. The two lower spires are surrounded by pradakshina paths as well. The temple is virtually hugged by a colonnaded veranda a mere 8 ft (2.4 m) away and consistently parallel to the mass of the outer walls of the main temple.

The **inner surface of the unpunctured walls of the surrounding veranda is adorned with a large as-life, freely composed sculptured frieze glorifying the origins and history of the Pallava kings**. The tightly knit plan, the intimate spaces, the multiple shrines, and the self-glorifying Pallava sculpture suggest that the Vaikuntaperumal was not a public shrine - rather, it was the **private family chapel of royalty**, situated within or adjacent to the palace precinct.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Progress of the Dravida Style - A Broader View</mark>

The study of temple art and architectural styles in the **Pallava-Chola periods (seventh to thirteenth centuries AD)** has established the importance of art history in providing additional insights into the role of the temple in the socio-political and cultural integration of this region.

**Rock-cut architecture** and its allied technique of rock-carvings dominated Pallava-Pandya art of the seventh-ninth centuries, while the **structural mode replaced it entirely by the tenth century AD** on account of its potentialities for the horizontal spread of temple sites as nuclei of agrarian settlements and for the expansion and concentration of temple complexes in the manifestation of urban forms and space.

The Dravida style progressed the garbhagriha with a moderate-sized tower (sikhara) and a pillared porch (ardha-mandapa) together called the vimana, into a **vertically dominant pyramidal structure of impressive heights by the eleventh century AD**.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE CHOLA PHASE - ZENITH OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Overview</mark>

The rich heritage of the Pallava tradition passed on to the Cholas, under whom the Dravida style enters yet another brilliant and distinctive phase. Temple architecture, particularly the Dravida or south Indian style of architecture, **reached the pinnacle of glory under the Cholas**. The chief feature of a Chola temple is the **vimana or the storey**, which was later eclipsed by the richly ornamented **gopuram or gateway**.

Under the Cholas, **temples became the centres of life**, particularly in the rural areas. The village assembly invariably held its meetings in the temple mandapa, which became an additional feature of the Chola temple architecture.

The **temple assumed the character of the chief ideological apparatus** for evolving the political iconography of the Cholas through the stupendous royal temple projects, marking the zenith of the Dravida style of architecture and allied arts. A near total identity was established between God and the king, which enabled the sacred and temporal domains to coincide.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Early Phase - Early Chola Temples</mark>

The **Pudukkotai district in Tamil Nadu** has an unusually large number of early Chola temples. The most important among them is undoubtedly the **Vijayalaya Cholesvara temple at Narthamalai** (also called Melamalai). Other important temples in this early category include:
- **Nagesvarasvami temple at Kumbhakonam**
- **Kuranganatha temple at Srinivasanallur**
- The twin temples of **Agastyisvara and Cholesvara at Tiruvalisvaram**
- The **earliest Chola temple at Dadapuram** in South Arcot, built in the tenth century

In the initial stages, architecturally, the Chola temples are simple and modest. **Sepulchral temples** (pallip-padai) were also built, where the kings were buried.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Mature Phase - The Brihadishvara Temple, Thanjavur (Tanjore)</mark>

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">The Temple and Its Context</mark>

The best example of the mature Chola phase is the **Siva or the Brihadeesvara or the Rajarajesvara temple**, built in **1009 CE** by **Rajaraja I**, at Tanjore. It is the **largest and tallest** of all Indian temples of the medieval period - at **216 feet (approximately 65.8 metres, or 70 metres in another reference)** - and a masterpiece of south Indian architecture.

Once Thanjavur (modern Tanjore) fell to the Chola king Rajaraja the Great (985–1081), he commissioned the Brihadesvara temple. Until that time, even after 500 years of building experience, **no tower higher than 60 ft (18.2 m)** had been attempted by the Hindu craftsmen of India. The masons of Tanjore now set out to make amends for the timidity of the southern builders in one big leap, conceiving of a spire more than **three times the maximum height** achieved so far.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Architectural Details</mark>

An ambitious superstructure required that the cells be proportionately enlarged in plan. In the great temple of Tanjore, it became a massive square of **82 ft (24 m) side**, containing within it a pradakshina path running all the way around the cella. The cube of the garbhagriha was carried vertically up to a height of 50 ft (15 m). Thereafter, the familiar pyramidal tower soared another **130 ft (39.6 m) into the sky**, capped by a single enormous **domical stone weighing more than 80 tons** - a monolithic octagonal dome-shaped stupika.

Two flat-roofed mandapas in front of the vimana are placed as always along the central axis, the largeness of their size in keeping with the proportions of the vimana. The portico housing the sculpture of the holy Nandi bull is situated along the same axis but is detached from the main temple. The entire complex stands today in the middle of a quadrangle defined by a peripheral veranda of two rows of columns. The **200 ft (60.8 m) high stone tower** which rises impressively over the sea of courtyards around is a gigantic reproduction of the familiar Dravidian vimana.

The **temples sanctum houses a two-storeyed lingam of Shiva**. Elaborate mythological narratives adorn the walls, depicted through painted murals and sculptures. **Two large gopurams** with intricate sculptural programs were conceived along with the temple. Stucco figures on the vimana, while possibly added during the Maratha period, contribute to the temple's grandeur.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Marco Polo Carving</mark>

In this temple, a carving of a man's head with a **European hat** is found on one side of the temple in a subsidiary structure, which is believed to be that of **Marco Polo** (late 13th century), the Venetian traveller.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Symbolic Significance</mark>

The temple is a fitting memorial to the material achievements of the Cholas under Rajaraja I. Under the Cholas, it attained a remarkable balance in plan, design and elevation, as in the royal temples at Tanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram, with the addition of large pillared halls in a single alignment. Enclosed by a **courtyard (prakara)** with **entrance gateways (gopuras) at the cardinal points**, it obtained **subsidiary shrines** in the courtyard for the lesser gods and guardian deities positioned at various points as per canonical requirements.

Two of these subsidiary shrines significantly housed:
- The **Tamil deity Murugan**, which the Brahminical pantheon admitted as a member of Shiva's family
- **Goddess Parvati or Uma**, who in each centre took the place of the local mother goddess incorporated into the Puranic pantheon

The whole complex of shrines followed closely the **symbolism of the cosmos-territory**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Temple of Gangaikondacholapuram</mark>

The **temple of Gangaikondacholapuram**, also dedicated to Siva or Brihadesvara, was the creation of **Rajendra I**, meant to excel its predecessor in every conceivable way. Erected around **1030 CE**, the greater elaboration in its appearance attests to the more affluent state of the Chola empire under Rajendra I. It is **larger in plan though not as tall** as the previous one at Tanjore.

Growing Chola power was accompanied by an equally passionate desire to build new cities that would be an appropriate architectural symbol of their expanding empire. The greatness of an Indian city of the middle ages was measured not by its town-planning wonders, nor its streets and bazaars and houses, but rather by the **magnificence of the temple housing the presiding deity**.

The **main vimana** rising from a base even larger than that at Tanjore, is transformed into a form embodying **concave quoins** at the ends of convex planes of sculptured surfaces. In contrast to the gridded and geometrically perfect vimana of Tanjore that symbolises conscious might, the **fluid lines of the latter are imbued with a subconscious grace**.

The **mandapa** of this temple on the other hand is totally different from the concept of Thanjavur. It is a huge flat-roofed hall of **75 x 195 ft (22.8 x 59.4 m)**. The entire structure is built on a high platform. The approach aisles designed like processional paths are left at ground level. With the Hindu builder's aversion to any system of construction other than the trabeate, it was inevitable that such a large room be held up by a veritable forest of over 150 closely spaced, slender columns. The first of its kind, this hall foreshadows the emergence of the famous **halls of thousand columns** of later temple complexes.

The Gangaikondacholapuram temple was built to commemorate Rajendra I's victories in North India during his expedition to the Ganga valley. Today, though, all that remains of Rajendra's great city is a few straggling village huts, his great city having long ago crumbled into dust. The city temple, though reduced to ruins, is a **flamboyant reminder of vanished Chola glory**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Later Phase - Other Notable Chola Temples</mark>

Two Chola temples worth mentioning in the later phase are:
1. The **Airavatesvara temple at Darasuram** (Tanjore District)
2. The **Kampaharesvara temple at Tribhuvanam**

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE GOPURAM - EVOLUTION AND SIGNIFICANCE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Stages of Evolution</mark>

The **form of the gopuram** derived partly from a pyramid and partly from a barrel-vaulted form derived from the Buddhist chaitya. Looking back to the wide array of religious and secular forms of the past, for an adequate alternative to crown the soaring tower, the craftsmen found that the **barrel vault roof of the rectangular Buddhist chaitya** had served the purpose eminently. This functional form, reduced now to an ornamental solid shape, crowned with a row of finials over the ridge, proved a fitting climax for the soaring tower.

In its earliest stages, the profile of the gopuram was a **rigid pyramidal tower**, its horizontal tiers clearly defined by a series of vertical pilasters and horizontal cornices. Inevitably, however, the Hindu sculptor infused into it a more plastic quality by making its profile **curvilinear**. Subsequently, the pilasters and cornices lost their sharpness and became absorbed into one large amorphous jumbled mass of sculpture.

Every inch of the surface of the body of the gopuram was covered with the usual efflorescence of tier upon tier of sculpted and even polychromed gods, apsaras, and demons.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Gopuram's Growing Dominance</mark>

Over a period of time, a number of such towers rising around a temple became the familiar skyline of any sizable urban settlement in the southern country. The temple had by now evolved into the centre not only of the religious but also social, economic and cultural life.

Once completed in the seventh century, the **temple city of Madurai** became the model for temple building in the South. Many old village shrines expanded into temple cities. As a result, there is hardly a temple of any religious consequence in the South which has not been surrounded by prakramas, entered through a series of axially aligned gopurams.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Gopuram's Height Overtaking the Vimana</mark>

One of the most interesting evolutionary features in Dravida temples is that the main central tower (vimana) over the garbhagriha is often one of the smallest towers in the entire complex. The outermost gopurams grew to be the tallest structures. The **outermost, more than 150 ft (48 m) high gopurams of the Meenakshi temple** are the finest examples of the curvilinear type of form.

The **temple at Srivilliputtur** is entered through a gopuram of **14 distinct storeys soaring over 200 ft (61 m) into the skies**.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE SRIRANGAM TEMPLE - THE TEMPLE CITY MODEL</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Concentric Enclosure System</mark>

The **Srirangam temple in Tiruchirapalli** showcases multiple **concentric rectangular enclosure walls**, each with gopurams of varying heights. Over the years, the core of the Srirangam temple enclosure of **80 x 240 ft (24 x 73 m)** had grown into a massive temple city of **2880 x 2475 ft (878 x 754 m)** sprawling over an area of more than a quarter square mile.

The **original open court** surrounding the garbhagriha was completely roofed over (with the many elaborate rituals to be performed in the vicinity of the central shrine). The sacred image of the presiding deity could only be dimly perceived in the uncertain light of myriads of lamps, acquiring an even more mysterious and sacred quality.

**Sacred tanks** were built - one a conventional rectangular one representing the sun, and a semicircular one representing the moon - both becoming part of another courtyard of the ever-growing temple.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Functional Spaces within the Temple City</mark>

The inner prakramas contained:
- Pavilions for devotional singing and storytelling
- Bathing tanks for ritual ablutions
- Guest houses for important visitors

To such enclosures only caste Hindus were allowed. In the innermost courts were the kitchens of the Brahmins, pavilions for the dancing girls, and the treasury, over both of which the Brahmin kept a watchful eye. Admittance to these was often restricted to the upper caste only. Even the chosen few allowed to have darshan of the deity could wear only a brief loincloth. The actual cella containing the graven image of the god was, of course, open only to the officiating priest and none else - not even royalty.

The rather fortuitous design of a temple as a series of **concentric fortifications adorned with massive gopurams** became an accepted style of temple building in the South.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">HALLS OF THOUSAND PILLARS - THE LATER TEMPLE COMPLEX</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Mandura Temple Complex</mark>

With temple building beginning to approximate more to city and town planning, the building craftsman's art was restricted to the erection of pavilions, halls, and gopurams. The **hall situated within the Meenakshi temple complex** at Madurai needed **985 pillars** to support its roof.

There is a hall measuring **240 x 250 ft (73 x 76 m)** - the famous **hall of thousand pillars**. Today, ironically enough, the hall is a museum of drawings and photographs of the entire gamut of 1200 years of temple architecture of the South. Evidently, the builders and the patrons were undaunted by the monotony of their productions, drawing consolation from the massive size of their building ventures.

They took seven years to build another, even larger but less distinguished **330 ft (110.5 m) deep Choultri** - this time at the command of the last Nayak, Tirumalai - who had it built outside the main precinct of the Madurai temple, south of the outermost gopurams.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Rameshwaram - The Longest Corridors</mark>

**Rameshwaram**, situated at the tip of a narrow strip of land jutting out into the sea, is a maze of generously proportioned pillared verandas. The temple is not only surrounded by corridors but is connected to the outer entrances by **transverse covered passageways**. The masons patiently crafted enormous columns - more like elaborate inverted pyramids than mere pillars. **Rameshwaram has thereby acquired the dubious architectural record of possessing the longest corridors in the world**, aggregating more than **3000 ft (914 m) in length**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Other Notable Temple Complexes</mark>

- The **Shiva temple at Chidambaram** boasts of a central shrine that is in the shape of a chariot.
- The **Jambukeshvara temple** has probably the most massive and elaborate shafts and brackets for its columns.
- The **Raya Gopurams** at Thiruvannamalai and Chidambaram speak to the glorious epoch of Vijayanagar.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE VIJAYANAGARA PERIOD - SYNTHESIS AND ELABORATION</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Vijayanagar Architecture - The Dravida Style with New Features</mark>

The Vijayanagara rulers produced a new style of architecture. The chief characteristics of the **Vijayanagara architecture** were:
- The construction of tall **Raya Gopurams** or gateways
- The **Kalyanamandapam** (marriage hall)
- Sculptures on the pillars carved with distinctive features; the **horse** was the most common animal depicted on the pillars
- **Large mandapams** containing one hundred pillars as well as one thousand pillars in some big temples

Fine examples of **Kalyana mandapa** can be seen at:
- **Vellore**
- The **Varadharajaswami and Ekamparanatha temples** at Kanchipuram
- The **Jambukesvara temple** at Thiruchirapalli

During the period of the Vijayanagar kings, temples were built in **Hampi, Shringeri, Tirupati, Kalahasti, Nandi, Kolar, Srishaila**, etc.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">New Structural Additions</mark>

New elements were introduced in the temple architecture. In addition to the main shrine, **a smaller temple was built in the north-west called the Amma Shrine** where the Lord or main deity's consort resided. This practice, which began in the late Chola period, now became the rule.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Synthesis with Islamic Styles</mark>

**Vijayanagara synthesised Dravida temple architecture with Islamic styles** from neighbouring sultanates. The ruins from the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries reflect a period of cultural fusion, wealth, and exploration. Vijayanagaras eclectic architecture embodies a synthesis of centuries-old traditions with occasional foreign influences.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Vittalaswamy and Hazara Temples at Hampi</mark>

The finest temples of Vijayanagara are in **Hampi**, which has been designated as a World Heritage City. Their capital city, Vijayanagar, stood on the south bank of the river Tungabhadra.

- The **famous Hazara temple**, built during the reign of Krishnadeva Raya, is one of the most perfect specimens of Hindu temple architecture in existence.
- The **Vittalaswamy temple** is also a fine example of Vijayanagar style. In the opinion of Fergusson, "it shows the extreme limit in florid magnificence to which the style advanced." The Vittalaswamy temple with its **saptaswara musical pillars and the Stone Chariot** are a few architectural wonders.
- The **rath (chariot) of the Vithala temple** was wrought from a single block of hard granite.

The important features of Vijayanagara style of temple architecture include **monolithic pillars, ornate bracelets and decoration** on the exterior side of the walls.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE KAILASANATHA TEMPLE AT ELLORA - THE DRAVIDA STYLE IN ROCK-CUT FORM</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Connection to the Dravida Style</mark>

The **Kailasanatha temple at Ellora** (Cave No. 16), dedicated to Shiva, is a **complete Dravida building carved entirely from living rock**. It was built under the patronage of the great Rashtrakuta king **Krishna I**.

The Kailasa temple is a living rock replica of a structural form, larger than could even have been constructed through the stone masonry technology of the eighth century. The Kailasa temple was equal in area to the Parthenon and one and a half times its height. The first step was to cut three massive trenches to isolate an island of rock over **200 ft (65.60 m) long and 100 ft (32.80 m) high** from the oft-worked cliff-like formations of Ellora.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Features</mark>

The flat-roofed mandapa in front of the sanctum, seemingly supported on 16 columns in groups of four, the five shrines which surrounded the cella, and a pavilion housing a larger-than-life sacred Nandi bull, rest on a **25 ft (7.6 m) high plinth**. Such a massive stylobate was obviously meant to lift the entire composition out of the yawning pit around. The fashioning of the front screen of natural rock into an entrance gateway completed the essentials of the main temple.

It has:
- A Nandi shrine and a **gopuram-like gateway**
- Surrounding cloisters and subsidiary shrines
- An imposing **tower or vimana rising to thirty metres**

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Visual and Symbolic Character</mark>

The stucco worker covered the entire surface of the Kailasa temple with a **white gesso**. When complete, it shone brightly, like the snow-capped peak of Mount Kailasa in the Himalayas, the mythological earthly abode of the God Shiva. The sculptures on the plinth were at one time coated with a thin layer of polychromed plaster. The Muslim invaders of the sixteenth century who saw it in its blazing glory referred to the temple as the **Rang Mahal** (the coloured palace).

The Kailasa was to be the great and glorious **swan-song of rock architecture in India**. The legendary architect responsible for its completion admitted that it could only be by magic that he had constructed even this one. That magic was truly never repeated - the Kailasa was to be great and glorious and final.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE VESARA STYLE - THE DRAVIDA-INFLUENCED HYBRID</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Definition and Origins</mark>

The **Vesara style** is also known as the **Chalukyan or Deccan style**. Its beginnings may be traced back to the days of the early Chalukyan kings in the 7th and 8th centuries AD. At **Aihole and Pattadakal** and other places, Dravida and Nagara temples were being erected side by side. This co-existence afforded an opportunity for a certain admixture of the ideas of the two, leading to the emergence - under the later Chalukyan rulers - of a mixed or hybrid style. In this development, it is the **Dravida, rather than the Nagara conception** that played a comparatively more important role.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Features of the Chalukyan/Vesara Temple</mark>

The Chalukyan temple, like the Dravida, consists of two main features:
- The **vimana** and the **mandapa**, joined by an **antarala**
- Occasionally, an additional open mandapa in front

In course of time, there is a marked tendency to **compress the heights of the storeyed stages of the vimana**. At the same time, ornamental niche motifs, repeated one above the other up the ascent of the tower, simulate the vertical bands of the northern spire - an evident inspiration from the Nagara shikhara.

The Chalukyan temple presents an **essential divergence from the Dravida** in not having its sanctum cella enclosed within a covered ambulatory.

In the treatment of the exterior walls, there seems to have been a blending of Nagara and Dravida ideas:
- The walls are broken up by **ratha offsets** in the characteristic Nagara fashion
- Further spaced at regular intervals by **pilasters** in accordance with the usual Dravida mode
- The recesses thus formed are usually filled up by niches with superstructures of the Nagara or of the Dravida style, "thus producing a refrain of great artistic beauty"

Some of the Chalukyan and most of the Hoysala temples are distinguished for their **multiple-shrined compositions** in which two, three or four shrines are arranged around the common mandapa hall. The Chalukyan temple, or its descendant, the Hoysala, is also characterised by an **exuberant plastic ornament** covering all its external surfaces, which seem to have a richly fretted appearance from the base to the top. In the interior, the pillars and door-frames, as well as ceilings, are likewise exuberantly treated.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Virupaksha Temple at Pattadakal</mark>

In the **temple of Virupaksha** (built at Pattadakal, 740 AD), the Chalukyan builder decided to surmount the garbhagriha with the more acceptable form of the southern vimana. In striking contrast to the Papanath, it at once presents an altogether substantial silhouette. To achieve equable balance between the upper and the lower parts of the temple, a **richly sculptured barrel-shaped form** was projected out from the middle of the vimana to interlock the tower and the mandapas. It effectively restrains the perceptible horizontal thrust of the mass below.

The mandapa, for the first time, is not treated merely as just another hall attached to the cella - rather, a more convincing spatial link-up is established between the two by **enveloping the dark square cella of the garbhagriha within the definable rectangular structure of the outer walls of the mandapa**. The corridor-like space along the back and the sides of the inner sanctum thus becomes a virtual pradakshina or circumambulatory path for the worshipper. The mandapa in turn could be approached through any of the three porticos in the middle of its outer walls.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Hoysalas</mark>

The **Hoysalas**, succeeding the Cholas and Pandyas, became prominent patrons in South India. In the southern Deccan, approximately a hundred temples have been discovered. The **Hoysaleshvara temple at Halebid, Karnataka**, constructed in **1150 CE** by the Hoysala king using **dark schist stone**, is one of the three most frequently discussed temples, alongside those at **Belur** and **Somnathpuram**.

Temples at Belur, Halebidu, and Somnathpuram are characterised by:
- **Stellate-plan** (star-shaped ground plans), growing extremely complex with soapstone carvings
- A unique **vesara style**, blending Dravida and Nagara elements
- Extraordinarily **elaborate and exuberant exterior surfaces** covered with soapstone carvings

The **Halebidu temple**, dedicated to Shiva as Nataraja, is a **dual structure** with a mandapa for music and dance, featuring intricate carvings of animals and deities.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">URBANISATION AND THE TEMPLE AS ADMINISTRATIVE CENTRE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temples as Focal Points of Urban Architecture</mark>

Temples in Tamil Nadu - such as those in **Kanchipuram, Thanjavur, Madurai, and Kumbakonam** - became focal points of urban architecture. **Temples transformed into rich administrative centres**, exerting control over extensive land areas during the eighth to twelfth centuries.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temples as Social and Economic Institutions</mark>

Under the Cholas, the temple gradually assumed the responsibility of management of the local distribution process. Supervisory functions were frequently transferred from local assemblies to temples, especially due to huge temple endowments. The temple assumed the character of a **super-ordinate instrument of integration** from the ninth century AD. Under the middle Cholas (985–1118 AD), its role increasingly expanded in the forging of institutional links for territorial sovereignty, particularly through the imperial temples like those at Tanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram.

The temple's **subsequent extension horizontally into a huge complex** of structures in the late Chola and post-Chola periods - with several pillared halls and additional enclosures with towering gateways - indicates a further elaboration of the temple's role in incorporating various levels of society in a complex of relationships through the temple's rituals and activities. This relationship reflects **socio-political dominance and stratification**.

At the apex of this society stood the **royal family**, as the authors and patrons of the temple, invariably associated with the main structures like the shrines (vimanas) and gateways (gopuras). Royalty was followed by the ritually pure **Brahmin priests** performing worship, an administrative elite, dominant agrarian and mercantile groups involved in temple administration, and the hierarchy ended up with the lower categories.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temples and Urban Growth</mark>

Temple geography and ecology have provided a useful method of contextualising the data on temple distribution and its correlation with actual politico-economic integration. In the subsequent economic diversification leading to the growth of urban centres, the temple's role has been significant. The character of the towns and cities was **determined by the temple**, which formed the core of a settlement and its expansion in a horizontal pattern with a remarkably clear horizontal stratification of space for hierarchical caste and occupational groups.

The more popular a temple became - the more praised in song and more attractive for pilgrims - the greater became the value of its patronage and the number of people whose identity was attached to it. Rising bhakti devotionalism enhanced the value of pilgrimage, as it increased temple donations and investments. Donations became increasingly popular as a means and indicator of social mobility as temples became commercial centres, landowners, employers and manufacturing centres. Increasing participation in temple rituals made them more effective sites for **social ranking**, as temple honours were distributed according to rank and all worshippers were positioned in ranked proximity to the deity.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Integrative Role of Royal Temples</mark>

The impressive economic outreach of the temples is best elucidated by the **stupendous royal temples**. In the case of Tanjavur, it covered the whole Chola kingdom and in politico-cultural centres like Kanchipuram, it covered the respective mandalam. In the arrangements for the huge endowments to these temples, the reciprocal flow of revenues from the peripheral to the core areas of the state and vice versa are confirmed by royal records. For Tanjavur, it included even northern Sri Lanka, with several sabhas and nagarams supplying paddy or commodity, ritual and other consumable articles for gold deposits received from the royal temple.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">STRUCTURAL ELEMENTS AND SPATIAL ORGANISATION OF DRAVIDA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Garbhagriha (Sanctum Sanctorum)</mark>

The **garbhagriha** is the sanctum sanctorum - the innermost chamber of a temple housing the main deity. Initially a small cubicle with a single entrance, it evolved into a larger chamber over time. It is designed to house the main icon, which becomes the focal point of significant ritual attention. In Dravida temples, the sanctum is invariably situated **within an ambulatory hall** (pradakshina path).

The image of God, enshrined in the inner sanctum, could look out upon the world **only through one opening**, in the direction of the Jagamohana/Mandapa. Admittance to the actual cella containing the graven image of the god was open only to the officiating priest and none else - not even royalty.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Vimana (Pyramidal Tower)</mark>

The **vimana** is the pyramid-shaped tower above the sanctum in Dravida-style temples. It is formed by an accumulation of storey after storey in receding dimensions. As mentioned, in South Indian temples the **shikhara refers only to the crowning element** - usually an octagonal cupola or small stupika - placed at the very top of the vimana.

The number of storeys in a vimana **varied from five to seven**, and they had a typical style. The chief feature of a Chola temple is the building of a storey upon a storey above the chief deity room.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Mandapa (Hall or Pavilion)</mark>

The **mandapa** is a hall or pavilion in front of a temple, used for gatherings and rituals. In South Indian temples, it evolved significantly:
- Early mandapas were simple pillared halls attached to the cella
- Later mandapas grew into enormous **pillared halls** containing one hundred or even one thousand pillars in some big temples
- The **Natmandir** within Orissan-style temples was where devadasis (literally, female servants of the god) danced, ostensibly to entertain the gods
- The **Bhog Mandir** was a hall where food received from the houses of rich donors was supposedly blessed by the holy presence and a substantial part of it distributed to the poor
- In the **Chola period**, the village assembly invariably held its meetings in the temple mandapa

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Pradakshina Path (Circumambulatory)</mark>

The **pradakshina path** (ambulatory) is an enclosed walkway surrounding the garbhagriha, allowing worshippers to circumambulate the sanctum. This is a defining feature of the Dravida temple - the sanctum cella is invariably situated within an ambulatory hall. In the Vaikuntaperumal temple at Kanchipuram, there are even **two pradakshina paths** surrounding the cella, making that temple unique.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Gopuram (Gateway Tower)</mark>

The **gopuram** is the central gateway tower of the compound wall. In the Dravida style, it is one of the most visually distinctive and dominant elements:
- Entrance to a Dravida temple complex is through the gopuram
- Over time, gopurams grew taller than the central vimana
- They are adorned with sculptures of dvarapalas, gods, apsaras, and demons
- Their profile evolved from a **rigid pyramid** to a **curvilinear form**

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Prakara (Compound Wall and Enclosure)</mark>

The **prakara** is the compound wall enclosing the entire temple complex. Dravida temples are characterised by being **enclosed within a compound wall** - unlike Nagara temples in North India, which typically lack elaborate boundary walls or gateways. In elaborate temple complexes like Srirangam, multiple concentric prakaras with their own gopurams were constructed over centuries.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Temple Tank (Pushkarani/Kunda)</mark>

Temple complexes in the Dravida style often include **large water reservoirs or temple tanks**. Sacred tanks represented cosmic elements:
- A conventional **rectangular tank** representing the sun
- A **semicircular tank** representing the moon
These became part of ever-growing temple complexes.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Subsidiary Shrines</mark>

Subsidiary shrines around the main temple are dedicated to the family or incarnations of the main deity, adding layers of significance to the temple complex. As per canonical requirements, guardian deities are positioned at various points in the courtyard. Under the Cholas, two of these subsidiary shrines notably housed the Tamil deity **Murugan** and **goddess Parvati or Uma**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Antarala (Vestibule)</mark>

In the Vesara/Chalukyan version of the Dravida-influenced temples, the vimana and the mandapa are **joined by an antarala** (vestibule or antechamber). The worshipper offers his momentary obeisance to the gods from a narrow antechamber-like space between the opening to the cella and the clearly defined space of the mandapa.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Nandi Pavilion</mark>

The **Nandi pavilion** is the space housing a statue of Nandi, the sacred bull, usually positioned in front of and along the axial alignment of the main temple. In the Brihadishvara temple at Tanjore, the portico housing the sculpture of the holy Nandi bull is situated along the same central axis but is **detached from the main temple**. Two stambhas (pillars) - reminiscent of the Asokan pillars in front of Buddhist chaitya halls at Karli - are placed on either side of it.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">SCULPTURE AND ICONOGRAPHY IN DRAVIDA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Placement of Sculptures</mark>

The **placement of deity images** within a Dravida temple is carefully planned:
- **Dvarapalas** (door-keepers) guard gateways or gopurams - fierce, imposing figures flanking entrances
- **Erotic images** (mithunas), the **nine auspicious planets** (navagrahas), and **yakshas** are strategically placed at entrances for protective purposes
- **Ashtadikpalas** (deities of the eight directions) face the eight key directions on the outer walls of the sanctum and/or the temple
- Various forms or aspects of the **main divinity** adorn the outer walls of sanctums

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Ornamental Elements</mark>

Distinct elements of ornamentation used in Dravida temples include:
- **Gavaksha** - arch-like decorative motifs
- **Vyala/Yali** - mythical animal figures
- **Kalpa-lata** - scroll-like decorative motifs
- **Amalaka** - circular stone disk at the top of the shikhara
- **Kalasha** - decorative finial or pot-like structure crowning the temple

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Chola Sculpture</mark>

The Chola period witnessed great strides in the field of sculpture. The **three main classes of Chola sculpture** are:
1. **Portraits** - Three well-preserved and nearly life-size portraits are on the walls of the Kuranganatha temple at Srinivasanallur, and several others in the Nagesvara temple at Kumbhakonam
2. **Icons** - The walls of the Brihadesvara temples at Tanjore and Gangaikondacholapuram contain numerous icons of large size and fine execution. The Siva temple at Tiruvalisvaram is a veritable museum of superb early Chola iconography
3. **Decorative sculpture** - Exhibited in many forms: architectural motifs, floral and vegetal patterns, friezes of animals, birds, dancing figures, and legendary and Puranic stories

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Bronze Sculptures in Connection with Dravida Temples</mark>

South India, especially during the **Chola period in Tamil Nadu** (tenth to twelfth centuries), witnessed the creation of beautiful and exquisite bronze statues. The Cholas are particularly known for their **Nataraja bronzes** - bronze statues of Nataraja or the dancing Shiva - which are masterpieces of this medium. The best example is the **Nataraja image in the Nagesvara temple at Kumbhakonam**.

A group of three bronzes of **Rama, Lakshmana and Sita** with Hanuman at their feet from Tirukkadaiyur (Tanjore District) is one of the finest products of Chola bronze-making of the reign of Rajaraja I.

The **iconography of Shiva** in the eighth-century Pallava period evolved, and the dancing form of Shiva as **Nataraja** fully developed during the Chola period. The Thanjavur region showcased a wide range of Shiva iconography, including the **Ardhanarisvara murti** depicting the union of Shiva and Parvati, and the remarkable **ninth-century Kalyanasundara Murti**, which represents the Panigrahana (marriage ceremony) through two separate statuettes.

During the sixteenth-century **Vijayanagara period**, sculptors experimented with **portrait sculpture** to preserve the likeness of royal patrons. Life-size standing portrait statues at Tirupati depicted Krishnadeva Raya with his queens, showcasing both likeness and idealisation in facial features and physical body modelling.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Wall Paintings (Murals)</mark>

**Chola wall paintings** are found on the walls of the Vijayalaya Cholesvara and Rajarajesvara temples:
- On the walls of the **Vijayalaya Cholesvara temple**, large painted figures of Mahakala, Devi and Shiva are still visible
- In the **Rajarajesvara temple**, scenes representing Shiva in his abode of Kailasa as Nataraja and Tripurantaka are painted on the walls in large and forceful compositions

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE VASTU SHASTRA AND COSMOLOGICAL FRAMEWORK OF DRAVIDA TEMPLE DESIGN</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Role of the Brahmins and the Vastu Shastra</mark>

The basic concepts of temple design were evolved through the creative activity of master craftsmen. Nevertheless, it was the **Brahmins who began laying down elaborate and complex rules** of layouts for building. These were intentionally made complex enough to be well beyond the comprehension of a layman or even a skilled craftsman - and consequently beyond his rights to challenge.

In the great Hindu compendiums of architectural rules - the **Vastu Shastra** - there is much that is deliberately esoteric verbiage. The very minutest of the acts of building - such as the layout, choice of site, testing of solid conditions, and even the thickness of walls and columns - were based not on technological, but rather on **mythological and even astronomical considerations**. Formulae were drawn up to indicate how the thickness of a wall, or the span between the columns of a temple, were to be determined from:
- The **day or time** when construction commenced
- The **orientation** of the temple
- The **caste of the major donor**
- Even the **position of the stars** in the sky

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Vastupurusha Mandala - The Magic of the Square</mark>

The guiding philosophy of the **Mandala** - the Vastupurusha Mandala - was centred on the **square**: the most basic, rational and elementary of all geometric forms. For the earthly abode of God, there could be nothing but the most perfect of forms - obviously either the circle (a polygon of infinite sides) or a square (a polygon of equal sides joined at right angles). The Hindu chose the square.

Percy Brown's assessment of the Indian counterpart - the Mandala - states that if faithfully followed, **failure would be impossible**. This is a fact borne out by the quality of Indian temple activity through centuries, spread over territories separated by thousands of miles.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Role of the Craftsmen - The Senis (Ancient Guilds)</mark>

Even during the formative stages of art in India, building craftsmen recognised not only the specialised quality of their skill but also of its intrinsic value to society. As early as the seventh century BC, when building activity was carried out with temporary materials like timber and unbaked brick, craftsmen had organised themselves into **guilds (Senis)** to effectively guard their economic, technical and artistic interests.

An association of this nature, in its earlier stages, would accept **antevasis** (literally, "the boarders") as resident apprentices to train them in the skills of their crafts. Over a period of time, when the more laborious arts of cave and stone architecture became popular, the trainee required not only long periods of theoretical instruction, but also practical experience at sites often remote from urban centres. Under such circumstances, **heredity became a custom**. The skills of the trade were conveniently passed on from father to son.

The builder, though working for the property-owning wealthy class, himself rarely acquired any immovable property. The convention of heredity, apart from its practical function, assured the skilled artisan that his son would at least continue to retain his status in society. The transfer of skills to his offspring was the **craftsman's insurance policy** on his skills.

On some occasions the magnitude of their task would force a group of craftsmen to settle down near the site of their labours for a generation or more. It was such a settlement at a single site - rather than purely academic thought - that resulted in the growth of an **art centre**, giving rise to a school and a style of architecture. So it was that a group of craftsmen working continuously for over a century on the building ventures of the Chandelas gradually evolved the characteristic Khajuraho style.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE TEMPLE AS COSMIC SYMBOL - IDEOLOGY AND SYMBOLISM</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temple as the Cosmos</mark>

The whole complex of shrines in a Dravida temple followed closely the **symbolism of the cosmos-territory**. Under the Cholas, this symbolism attained a remarkable balance in plan, design and elevation in the royal temples at Tanjavur and Gangaikondacholapuram.

The **bhakti ideology** assisted in the process of enhancing the power of both the divine and human sovereigns through the **symbolism of the cosmos-temple-territory**. A near total identity was established between God and the king, which enabled the sacred and temporal domains to coincide. The temple thus represented **multiple facets and complex processes** of this development through its architecture, sculpture, iconography, rituals and institutional organisation.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Transformation of Local Deities</mark>

The **absorption of local deities, rituals, symbols and spiritualism** into Puranic literature and related myth, folklore and artistic representation constituted Hindu worship by enhancing the cultural potency of local deities, their devotees and their patrons. Local cults were woven into Puranic traditions and temple rituals as local communities came under royal authority.

The transformation of a local temple to a regional sacred place is best illustrated by the history of the **temple city of Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu**. Chidambaram is identified with the cult of Shiva as the **King of Dancers (Nataraja)**. The origin of the cult seems to have been the worship of a stone at a local pond. The stone was later identified as the Shiva lingam and was worshipped as the mulasthana (place of origin). The identification of the local dancing god with Shiva seems to have been done by the **sixth century AD**.

In a similar fashion, the incorporation of **Minakshi**, the fish-eyed goddess of the Pandyas of Madurai, into the patriarchal Sanskrit tradition was achieved by identifying her with Shiva's wife, Parvati, and making the **marriage of Shiva and Parvati** the central feature of the Minakshi cult.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">POLITICAL IMPORTANCE AND IDEOLOGY OF DRAVIDA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temple as Ideological Apparatus</mark>

The temple assumed the character of the **chief ideological apparatus** for evolving the political iconography of the Cholas through the stupendous royal temple projects, marking the zenith of the Dravida style of architecture and allied arts.

The **devotional temple-based Hinduism** of the Pallava-Chola period is believed to have served the Brahmin-peasant alliance which constituted a foundation of localised self-governing peasant regions. It facilitated the legitimisation of the dominance of privileged elements within the peasantry - the nattar - on whom the brahmadeya depended.

Contrary perspectives, which perceive the reality of royal power in the localities, take the position that it was the **ideology of the ruling dynasty** and not of the supposed Brahmin-dominant peasant alliance.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Royal Temples and Territorial Sovereignty</mark>

The impressive economic outreach of the temples - best elucidated by the stupendous **royal temples at Tanjavur and Kanchipuram** - covered not only the entire Chola kingdom but also extended into northern Sri Lanka. The reciprocal flow of revenues from the peripheral to the core areas of the state and vice versa are confirmed by royal records.

The temple afforded a **foothold for the kings to intervene in local affairs**. The ties that may have existed between the local temple and local elite were severed by the expanding economy of the temple and management of resources across nadu limits and by the centralisation measures of the kings through supervision of temple affairs by royal officials (muvendavelar, koyirramar), auditing or enquiring into temple endowments, scale of temple expenses, and making reallocations.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temple-Based Bhakti and the Chola Consolidation</mark>

The Cholas, in particular, adopted, elaborated and zealously practised the bhakti ideology through various measures, such as:
- The collection of the bhakti hymns
- Their popularisation through temple rituals and grants for such rituals
- The **construction of temples** in all the centres associated with the bhakti hymns

The Chola king Rajaraja I initiated the system of prefacing the stone inscriptions of the reign with an account in set terms of its chief events, kept up to date by additions from time to time. **Worship of deceased rulers and construction of temples as tributes to dead kings** was a special feature of the Chola period.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">KEY MONUMENTS OF THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram)</mark>

- **Sculptural Panel**: One of the world's largest and oldest sculptural panels, measuring nearly **30 metres in length and 15 metres in height**. Scholars offer different interpretations - some believe it represents the **descent of the Ganga**, while others associate it with **Kiratarjuniya** or Arjuna's penance, a work by the poet Bharavi. There is also speculation that the entire tableau serves as a prashasti, praising the Pallava king.
- The **Shore Temple**: Possibly the first structural temple to be built in the South. Contains two shrines to Shiva and one to Vishnu (Anantashayana).
- The **Seven Rathas (Eight Pagodas)**: Free-standing monolithic models of structural buildings.
- A coastal town with Pallava temples dating back to the **seventh and eighth centuries**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Kanchipuram</mark>

- **Kailasanatha Temple** (built by Rajasimha): Key monument of the early Dravida style, with all features - walled court, gopuram, pillared mandapa and vimana - complete and harmoniously arranged.
- **Vaikunta Perumal Temple** (built by Nandivarman II): Unique for having three distinct usable storeys within the vimana, each with its own central shrine, and two pradakshina paths.
- Kanchipuram (along with Thanjavur, Madurai, and Kumbakonam) was one of the focal points of **urban temple architecture** that became a rich administrative centre.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Brihadishvara Temple (Rajarajesvara), Tanjore</mark>

- Built by **Rajaraja I** (Rajaraja Chola) around **1009 CE**
- Height: **216 feet** (approximately 65–70 metres) - the largest and tallest of all Indian medieval temples
- Features a massive pyramidal vimana with a **monolithic octagonal dome-shaped stupika** weighing more than 80 tons
- Sanctum houses a **two-storeyed Shivalingam**
- Features **two large gopurams** with intricate sculptural programs
- Contains elaborate mythological narratives through painted murals and sculptures
- Stucco figures on the vimana (possibly added during the Maratha period)
- A carving of a man's head with a European hat - believed to be **Marco Polo** - is found in a subsidiary structure

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Gangaikondacholapuram Temple</mark>

- Built by **Rajendra I** around **1030 CE**
- Dedicated to Shiva (Brihadesvara)
- Larger in plan but not as tall as the Brihadishvara at Tanjore
- Main vimana features **concave quoins** at the ends of convex planes
- Mandapa: an enormous flat-roofed hall of **75 x 195 ft (22.8 x 59.4 m)**, supported by over **150 closely spaced, slender columns**
- Foreshadows the emergence of the famous **halls of thousand columns**
- Built to commemorate Rajendra I's victories in North India

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Airavatesvara Temple, Darasuram (Tanjore District)</mark>

- A later phase Chola temple
- Features a **miniature temple carved in the form of a chariot**, similar to Konark's concept

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Kampaharesvara Temple, Tribhuvanam</mark>

- Another later-phase Chola temple of note

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Meenakshi Temple, Madurai</mark>

- Best example of the fully evolved Dravida temple city model
- Has a hall requiring **985 pillars** to support its roof
- Famous **hall of thousand pillars** measuring **240 x 250 ft (73 x 76 m)** - today a museum of Indian temple architecture
- Outermost gopurams more than **150 ft (48 m) high** are the finest examples of the curvilinear type
- The central shrine (Meenakshi - Minakshi) originated as a local deity incorporated into the Puranic pantheon through identification with Parvati

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Srirangam Temple, Tiruchirapalli</mark>

- Multiple **concentric rectangular enclosure walls**, each with gopurams of varying heights
- Grew from a core of **80 x 240 ft (24 x 73 m)** to a temple city of **2880 x 2475 ft (878 x 754 m)**
- Showcases the fullest evolution of the **Dravida temple city concept**

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Rameshwaram Temple</mark>

- Situated at the tip of a narrow strip of land jutting out into the sea
- Famous for possessing the **longest corridors in the world**, aggregating more than **3000 ft (914 m) in length**
- Connected to outer entrances by **transverse covered passageways**
- Features enormous columns crafted like elaborate inverted pyramids

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Chidambaram Shiva Temple</mark>

- Identified with the cult of Shiva as **Nataraja** (King of Dancers)
- The central shrine is in the **shape of a chariot**
- The origin of the cult began as the worship of a local stone at a pond
- Over centuries transformed into a major regional sacred centre

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Vittalaswamy Temple and Hazara Temple, Hampi (Vijayanagara)</mark>

- **Hazara Temple**: Built during the reign of Krishnadeva Raya - considered one of the most perfect specimens of Hindu temple architecture in existence
- **Vittalaswamy Temple**: Features the famous **saptaswara musical pillars** and the **Stone Chariot** (rath) wrought from a single block of hard granite. Called "the extreme limit in florid magnificence to which the style advanced" by Fergusson
- Hampi has been designated as a **World Heritage City**

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Kailasanatha Temple, Ellora (Rashtrakuta)</mark>

- A complete **Dravida building carved entirely from living rock**
- Built under the patronage of **Krishna I** of the Rashtrakuta dynasty
- Equal in area to the Parthenon and **one and a half times its height**
- Vimana rising to **thirty metres**
- Originally covered with **white gesso (stucco)**, shining like the snow-capped Kailasa
- The Muslim invaders of the sixteenth century called it **"Rang Mahal"** (the coloured palace)
- The swan-song of rock architecture in India

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Srivilliputtur Temple</mark>

- Entered through a gopuram of **14 distinct storeys soaring over 200 ft (61 m)** into the skies
- One of the finest examples of the evolved, soaring gopuram

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Virupaksha Temple, Pattadakal</mark>

- A notable example of the **Dravida tradition** within the Chalukyan context
- Features the Dravida vimana surmounting the garbhagriha
- A richly sculptured barrel-shaped form connects vimana and mandapa
- The mandapa is approached through any of three porticos in the middle of its outer walls

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">DRAVIDA VERSUS NAGARA - KEY DIFFERENCES</mark>

| Feature | Dravida (South Indian) | Nagara (North Indian) |
|---|---|---|
| **Tower form** | Geometrically rising stepped pyramid (vimana) | Curvilinear spire (shikhara/rekha-prasada) |
| **Shikhara meaning** | Only the crowning finial (octagonal cupola/stupika) | The entire curvilinear tower |
| **Compound wall** | Enclosed within a compound wall | Typically absent |
| **Gateway** | Prominent gopuram | No elaborate gateway |
| **Entrance sculptures** | Fierce dvarapalas | River goddesses (Ganga, Yamuna) |
| **Ambulatory** | Sanctum invariably within ambulatory hall | Not invariably so |
| **Temple tank** | Large water reservoir often included | Less common |
| **Tower hierarchy** | Outer gopurams become tallest over time; main vimana often smallest | Garbhagriha always under tallest shikhara |
| **Ground plan shape** | Classified into 5 shapes (square, rectangular, elliptical, circular, octagonal) | Primarily square/cruciform |
| **Amalaka/Kalasha** | Equivalent function performed by the shikhara (cupola/stupika) | Amalaka and kalasha distinctly crown the shikhara |
| **Tower finial** | Octagonal cupola (stupika) | Kalasha (pot-like finial) over amalaka |

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">DYNASTIES AND THEIR CONTRIBUTIONS TO THE DRAVIDA STYLE</mark>

| Dynasty | Period | Key Contributions |
|---|---|---|
| **Pallavas** | 7th–9th century CE | Pioneers; rock-cut rathas at Mahabalipuram; Shore Temple; Kailasanatha and Vaikuntaperumal temples at Kanchipuram |
| **Chalukyas** | 7th–8th century CE | Dravida-Nagara hybrid experiments at Aihole, Badami, Pattadakal; Virupaksha temple |
| **Rashtrakutas** | 8th–9th century CE | Kailasanatha temple at Ellora - rock-cut Dravida masterpiece; Elephanta caves |
| **Cholas** | 9th–13th century CE | Zenith of Dravida style; Brihadishvara at Tanjore; Gangaikondacholapuram; Chola bronzes; temple as administrative and urban centre |
| **Hoysalas** | 12th–13th century CE | Stellate-plan Vesara temples at Halebid, Belur, Somnathpuram |
| **Vijayanagara** | 14th–16th century CE | Raya Gopurams; Kalyanamandapam; halls of thousand pillars; synthesis with Islamic elements; Hampi temples |
| **Pandyas/Nayaks** | Post-Chola period | Expansion of temple cities; enormous gopurams at Madurai, Rameshwaram, Srivilliputhur |

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">MISCELLANEOUS</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Bhitargaon Brick Temple - The Most Outstanding Early Example</mark>

The **Bhitargaon temple** (Kanpur district) was the earliest full structural temple and also the most outstanding example of the early structural phase. It introduced a regular counterbalance projection in the middle of each side, resulting in a **cruciform ground-plan** - a significant formal development that influenced later structural design.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Role of the Vesara Style</mark>

Scholars acknowledge a hybridised style called **Vesara**, which emerged around the mid-seventh century, distinct from the Nagara and Dravida styles. It is also known as the Chalukyan or Deccan style. In this development, the Dravida conception played a comparatively more important role than the Nagara. The Deccan was the main zone of the evolution of the Vesara form, with variations based on sub-regions and their dynastic preferences.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Dravida Temples and Water</mark>

**Proximity to water bodies** - tanks, rivers, or ponds - becomes integral to temple architecture in the South. Sacred tanks are built as part of the complex, representing cosmic bodies such as the sun and moon.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Stucco Work in Dravida Temples</mark>

Stucco work was an important decorative technique in Dravida temples:
- The entire surface of the **Kailasa temple at Ellora** was once covered with white gesso, shining like Mount Kailasa
- Stucco figures on the **vimana of the Brihadishvara** temple at Tanjore were possibly added during the Maratha period

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Devadasis and Ritual Functions</mark>

Within the Dravida temple complex, certain ritual functions were institutionalised:
- The **Natmandir** was a hall where **devadasis** (literally, female servants of the god) danced to entertain the gods
- The **Bhog Mandir** was a hall where food received from donors was blessed and distributed

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Symbolism of the Cosmos-Territory</mark>

The Dravida temple complex - from the garbhagriha at the core to the outermost gopuram - symbolised the **cosmos-territory**. This symbolism underpinned the entire socio-political and religious function of the temple in South Indian civilisation. The temple complex, through its spatial organisation, reflected the hierarchical ordering of society, from royalty and Brahmin priests at the core to ordinary devotees in the outer enclosures.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temples as Permanent Architecture</mark>

An architecturally consequential part of the societal arrangement of medieval India was the agreement to erect **only religious edifices in permanent materials like stone**. Secular architecture - including palaces for royalty - was assumed to be of a transitory, second-class nature and built in perishable materials like brick and timber. Thus, little secular architecture of the feudal period of any consequence has survived intact. The perceivable history of Indian architecture of the middle ages continues, therefore, to be a story of the **evolution of the art of temple building**.
