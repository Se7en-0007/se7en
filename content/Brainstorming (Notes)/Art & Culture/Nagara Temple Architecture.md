## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">INTRODUCTION AND CONTEXT</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Emergence of Regional Architectural Styles</mark>

The centuries between the eighth and the thirteenth stand out prominently from the point of view of the making of cultural traditions in India. The most arresting feature of these traditions is **regionalism**, which gets reflected in every sphere - whether in the formation of political power, development of arts, transformations in languages and literature, or religious manifestations. It was during this time that different architectural languages such as **Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara** matured and reached their fullest expression.

The emergence of regional cultural units such as Andhra, Assam, Bengal, Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, etc., was the outcome of significant material changes. The pace of agrarian changes and developments in the non-agrarian sector were setting the tone of feudal socio-economic formation. The political structure was deeply affected by these developments, and so was the cultural ethos.

The panorama of Indian temple architecture may be seen across an extremely wide chronological and geographical horizon - from the simple beginnings at Sanchi in the fifth century CE to the great edifices at Kanchi, Thanjavur, and Madurai, spanning more than a millennium.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Textual Tradition (Shilpashastras)</mark>

A number of architectural texts known as the **Shilpashastras** were written in ancient and early medieval India. The prominent ones that deal with the subject of temple architecture are:

- **Mayamata** (also Mayamatam) - the most complete medieval treatise on architecture
- **Manasara** (also Manoasara)
- **Shilparatna**
- **Kamikagama**
- **Kashyapasilpa** - has a specific chapter on the deities to be enshrined in the principal styles
- **Ishanagurudevapaddhati**
- **Aparajitapriccha** - specifically confines the Nagara style to the Madhyadesha (roughly the Ganga-Yamuna plains), and further mentions Lati and Vairati (Gujarat and Rajasthan respectively) as separate styles
- Other texts: **Silpratnakosa, Silpapradipa, Silpasastra, Bhubanapradipa, Vishnudharmottara Purana, Silpasarani** - codified by Shilpins and throw ample light on ancient Indian architecture

In the majority of these works, the subject is dealt with under three heads:
1. The **geographical distribution** of temple styles
2. Their **differentiation** from the point of view of shapes
3. Their **presiding deities and castes**

Some later texts like the Kamikagama and Kashyapasilpa show that the nature of ornamentation, number of storeys, and size of prasadas also constituted bases of differentiation.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE THREE MAJOR TEMPLE STYLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Classification Overview</mark>

The ancient texts on Indian temple architecture broadly classify temples into **three orders**: **Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara**. These terms indicate a tendency to highlight typological features of temples and their geographical distribution. They describe respectively temples that primarily employ:
- **Nagara** - square ground plans
- **Dravida** - octagonal ground plans
- **Vesara** - apsidal ground plans

These ground plans also regulate the vertical profile of the structure.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Geographical Distribution</mark>

- **Nagara** temples are generally identified with the **northern temple style**. All of northern India, from the foothills of the Himalayas to the central plateau of the Deccan, is furnished with temples in the northern style. The Nagara style is associated with the **land between the Himalayas and the Vindhyas**.
- **Dravida** temples are identified with the **southern temple style**. The Dravida style with the land between the **Krishna and Kaveri rivers** (also mentioned as between the Krishna river and Kanyakumari). The Dravida style comparatively speaking followed a more consistent development track.
- **Vesara** style is sometimes associated with the **area between the Vindhyas and the Krishna river**. Some texts ascribe the Vesara style to the country between the Vindhyas and the river Krishna, but there are texts placing it between the Vindhyas and the Agastya, the location of which is uncertain.

**Important caveat**: Since the temples of the Nagara type are found as far south as **Dharwad in Karnataka** and those of the Dravidian type as far north as **Ellora in Maharashtra**, a narrow and compartmentalized geographical classification is misleading. The Sanskrit Vastushastra texts distinguish a northern Nagara style from that of the south Dravida, but the textual evidence suggests that the northern and southern temple styles had become distinct from around the **sixth century CE**.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">DEFINITION AND ESSENTIAL FEATURES OF NAGARA STYLE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Definition</mark>

The prevalent style of temple architecture in northern India is known as **Nagara**. Northern temples are often constructed on a **stone platform** with steps leading up to it. Unlike South India, there is typically an **absence of elaborate boundary walls or gateways**.

The basic plan of the Nagara temple is **square**, with a number of **projections in the middle of each side**, giving it a **cruciform shape**. The temple's elevation is marked by a **conical or convex shikhara** (temple tower) consisting of several layers of carved courses, usually crowned by an **amalaka** (notched ring stone).

These two features - the **cruciform plan** and **curvilinear shikhara** - are visible in northern temples from the **6th century CE** (the late Gupta period), for example in the **Dashavatara temple at Deogarh** and the **brick temple at Bhitargaon** (both in UP).

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cosmic Symbolism</mark>

The temple's form was influenced by one prevailing idea - that the temple was the **deity's temporary heavenly abode on earth**. It was therefore conceived and modelled on the concept of the **heavenly-cosmic mountain**. This schema is visible in both the temple's plan and its elevation.

The **plan** is essentially a stepped square or diamond, embodying piled-up rock imagery in its overlapping and expanding forms. In **elevation**, the silhouette of the tower is repeatedly replicated, with each tower being larger than the last. The building blocks of the design were replicated until the final form was achieved.

Located at the temple's heart is the **garbhagriha** (womb chamber), recalling in its dark inner recesses the mountain-cave imagery of earlier times. The **cave chamber surmounted by the great mountain tower (shikhara)** combines two very powerful sets of imagery that persist as the defining elements of the traditional Indian temple.

The temple radiates in all directions - cardinal and transverse, as well as skywards - creating the pyramidal form. The ascending exterior surface is populated with divinities according to a hierarchical order. The temple is built on fundamental Indian concepts of **the infinite in the finite**, and the **cyclical process of creation, dissolution, and recreation** of forms.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Structural Elements (Technical Vocabulary)</mark>

Each temple style has its own distinctive technical language. The key elements of a Nagara temple are:

- **Garbhagriha**: The innermost sanctum (literally, "womb chamber"). Initially a small cubicle with a single entrance, it evolved into a larger chamber over time. It houses the main presiding deity (chief icon). The garbhagriha is always situated directly under the tallest tower or shikhara.
- **Vimana**: The sanctuary - the main structural component - which is the part surmounting the garbhagriha and includes the shikhara. Essentially it is the "well-proportioned" type.
- **Shikhara**: The tower or spire over the inner sanctum - the most characteristic feature of the Nagara style. It is lofty, curvilinear, tapering, and sometimes swelling. The word literally means "mountain peak."
- **Mandapa**: The pavilion or hall for the assembly of devotees; pillared hall in front of the shrine; used for gatherings and rituals.
- **Antarala**: A vestibule connecting the vimana and mandapa.
- **Ardhamandapa**: An entrance chamber / porch in front of the main mandapa, forming a transitional area between the outside and the mandapa.
- **Pradakshinapatha**: The circumambulatory passage surrounding the sanctuary and hall.
- **Pitha/Jagati**: The raised platform or plinth on which the temple stands.
- **Amalaka**: A circular, flat, fluted disc-like stone (the fruit-shaped capping stone), a **signature motif of the Nagara style**, usually at the summit of the shikhara. This conspicuous element was absent in South Indian temples.
- **Kalasha**: The decorative pot-shaped finial or vase installed over the amalaka as a crowning element at the very top of the shikhara. Also called "purna-kalasha" (symbolic vase of plenty).
- **Natmandir** / **Natamandira**: The dance hall - evolved subsequently in Odishan temples to add dignity and magnificence.
- **Bhogamandapa**: A hall in a temple where food from donors is supposed to be blessed by the enshrined deity.
- **Panchayatana**: A style of temple where the main shrine is built on a rectangular plinth and has four subsidiary shrines at the four corners (total of five shrines).
- **Urusringa** (also **uru-srngas**): Half-profile shikharas superimposed over the main shikhara, particularly in the Khajuraho style. These miniature spires cluster around the main shikhara, multiplying the tower forms.
- **Jangha**: The bottom portion / wall section of the spire in certain North Indian temples.
- **Bhadra**: The main architectural projections/offsets on the shikhara's sides - where icons are installed.
- **Devakoshthas**: Niches on the outer walls framing images of various deities.
- **Dvarapalas**: Doorkeeper figures flanking the entrance to the sanctum.
- **Gavaksha**: A horse-shoe-shaped window motif used as an ornamental element.
- **Kudu**: Another term for the horseshoe-shaped window or arch motif.
- **Vyala/Yali**: A leaping/composite mythical beast motif used in temple ornamentation.
- **Kalpa-lata**: A decorative motif (wish-granting creeper) used on temple walls.
- **Torana**: Ceremonial gateway arching motif.
- **Stambha/Dhvajastambha**: A pillar / flag pillar, positioned axially before the sanctum; in Vaishnava temples, surmounted by Garuda (Garudastambha) as a standard pillar.
- **Vahana**: The mount or vehicle of the main deity, positioned axially before the sanctum.
- **Nandi pavilion**: Space housing the statue of Nandi (Shiva's sacred bull), positioned in front of the main Shaiva temple.
- **Sandhara style**: A temple style with an enclosed passage for circumambulation (pradakshinapatha enclosed within the sanctum walls).
- **Nirandhara**: Temple with a pradakshinapatha (circumambulatory path).
- **Sandhara** (without pradikshinapatha).
- **Sarvatobhadra**: Temple accessible from all sides.
- **Rekha-prasada/Rekha deul**: Another name for the curvilinear/latina type shikhara, characteristic of the classic Nagara style. ("Rekha" means "line" referring to the curved outline of the tower.)

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Shikhara: Types and Evolution</mark>

Early temples had a **single tower (shikhara)**, but later ones featured **multiple towers**. Multiple types of shikhara evolved over time. The variations in the shape of the shikhara lead to different **subdivisions of Nagara temples**:

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">a) Latina Type (Rekha-Prasada Type)</mark>
- This is a **simple shikhara** - the basic form. It is famously known as the **Latina** or **Rekha-Prasada** type.
- It has a **square base** and walls that **curve or slope inward**, culminating in a **point** at the top.
- It is the most basic and archetypal form of the North Indian shikhara.
- Latina buildings evolved to support many smaller towers, **clustered together like rising mountain peaks**. The effect is that of a conglomerate of spires of which the largest is in the center.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">b) Phamsana Type</mark>
- Phamsana buildings are **broader and shorter** than latina ones.
- Their roofs consist of **several slabs gently rising to a single point**, presenting a different and flatter profile compared to the sharply rising towers of the latina type.
- In many North Indian temples, the **phamsana design is applied to mandapas** (halls), while the main garbhagriha is housed in a latina building.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">c) Valabhi Type</mark>
- Characterized by **rectangular buildings** with a roof rising into a **vaulted chamber** (wagon-vaulted or barrel-vaulted structure).
- The edge of the vaulted chamber is **rounded**, resembling ancient bamboo or wooden wagons. Hence often referred to as **wagon-vaulted buildings**.
- The Valabhi type of shikhara finds similarities with the **ground plan of Buddhist rock-cut chaitya caves**.
- This connection shows the influence of pre-existing Buddhist architectural traditions on Nagara temple architecture.
- At Jageshwar (Uttarakhand), a few temples are built in the **Valabhi mode** with rectangular plans.

The Nagara temple architecture's form is influenced by **ancient building forms existing before the fifth century CE**.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">ICONOGRAPHIC PRINCIPLES IN NAGARA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Placement of Deities</mark>

The placement of deity images within a temple is carefully planned according to textual prescriptions. In **Nagara temples**, **river goddesses Ganga and Yamuna** are commonly found at the **entrance of a garbhagriha**.

Various forms or aspects of the main divinity adorn the **outer walls of sanctums**. **Ashtadikpalas** (deities of eight directions) face the eight key directions on the outer walls of the sanctum and/or the temple.

**Erotic images (mithunas)**, the **nine auspicious planets (navagrahas)**, and **yakshas** are strategically placed at **entrances** for protective purposes.

**Subsidiary shrines** around the main temple are dedicated to the family or incarnations of the main deity.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Canonical Textual Prescriptions</mark>

The Vastushastra of Visvakarma (11th century) sets out a specific order:
> "Let the five divinities Brahma, Vishnu, Rudra, Ishvara (Mahesa) and Sadasiva live in each urah-srnaga on each bhadra offset."

That is, each icon in turn is to be installed on one of the main architectural projections on the shikhara.

The Mayamata prescribes:
> "At each level of the temple the architect should place divinities in the cardinal directions... Two door-guardians, Nandi and Mahakala, to the east; Dakshinamurti to the south; Vishnu or the Lingodbhava to the west; and Brahma to the north..."

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Presiding Deities by Temple Style</mark>

Textual prescriptions regarding presiding deities and styles:
- **Shanta-murtis** (peaceful, calm and serene deities) are to be installed in **Nagara** temples.
- Couples or moving deities in Vesara shrines.
- Heroic, dancing, or enjoying deities in Dravida structures.

Similarly, textual prescriptions associate Nagara style with Brahmanas, Dravida with Kshatriyas, and Vesara with Vaishyas - but these must not be taken in a compartmentalized, rigid sense.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Iconography (General)</mark>

The study of images of deities is a facet of art history known as **iconography**. While the fundamental myth and meaning of a deity may endure for centuries, its specific usage in a particular location often responds to local, social, political, or geographical contexts. Every region and period has produced distinct styles of deity images. As temples grew more complex, **additive geometry** was employed - adding rhythmically projecting, symmetrical walls and niches without deviating from the fundamental shrine plan.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE GUPTA PERIOD: FORMATIVE STAGE OF NAGARA STYLE (300–600 CE)</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Overview</mark>

The **Gupta period (300–600 CE)** saw the **beginnings of systematic construction** on the basis of structural principles in temple architecture. By evolving the Nagara and Dravida styles, Gupta art ushered in a formative and creative age with unlimited scope for future development. Interestingly, for the first time in the Gupta period, Brahmanical temples were constructed in **permanent material (stone)**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Basic Structural Elements</mark>

The basic elements of a Gupta-period temple:
- A **square sanctum (garbhagriha)** for the image
- A small **pillared portico (mukhamandapa)**
- Sometimes a **covered circumambulatory passage (pradakshinapatha)** around the sanctum

During this period in stone construction, there was a tendency to use stones larger than what the size of the building warranted - because the relationship between the strength/stability of construction and the economy of materials was yet to be understood. Stone was usually prepared at the site of the quarry, and often sculpting was done there too.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Early Gupta Temples</mark>

The **two surviving early Gupta temples** are:
- One at **Udaigiri near Vidisha** (Madhya Pradesh)
- One at **Sanchi near the stupa** (Madhya Pradesh)

Both are made of **sandstone** and represent a significant architectural development, featuring a **flat roof** and indicating shared design elements in Hindu and Buddhist temple construction. Early Gupta temples are relatively modest with **four pillars supporting a mandapa and garbhagriha**.

Other notable sites from this early era include **Eran, Nachna-Kuthara, and Udaygiri near Vidisha** in Madhya Pradesh, and **Deogarh** in Uttar Pradesh. These temples, characterized by simplicity, typically comprised a **veranda, a hall, and a shrine at the rear**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Bhitargaon Brick Temple (6th century CE)</mark>

Located in **Bhitargaon, Kanpur district, UP**, this is one of the oldest surviving Hindu pilgrimage centres - an architecturally rich structure constructed with **brick and terracotta**. Elevated on a high terrace, the temple has a sanctum 68.25 feet in height from ground level. The platform size of the shrine measures almost 47 feet. The terraced brick foundation has on its three sides a **central offset**. An anteroom within the shrine is linked by an oblong passage. This remarkable edifice of diminishing tiers represents an important early example of the Nagara form.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Deogarh: The Dashavatara Temple (Early 6th Century CE)</mark>

The **Dashavatara temple at Deogarh** in Uttar Pradesh (Jhansi District), dating to the early sixth century CE, exemplifies **late Gupta Period temple architecture** and is a key monument in the history of the Nagara style.

- The Deogarh temple follows the **panchayatana** style - a main shrine built on a rectangular plinth with **four subsidiary shrines at the four corners** (total five shrines).
- The **curvilinear shikhara** (curving latina or Rekha-prasada type) indicates an **early example of the classic Nagara style**.
- This **west-facing temple** has a grand doorway featuring sculptures of **Ganga and Yamuna**.
- The temple's depictions of Vishnu in various forms led to the misconception that it was a **Dashavatara temple** - but the original dedication of the four subsidiary shrines remains unknown.
- The main reliefs of Vishnu adorn the walls: **Sheshashayana (Anantashayana)** - Vishnu reclining on the serpent Ananta; **Nara-Narayan** - showing the discussion between the human soul and the eternal divine; and **Gajendramoksha** - the story of achieving moksha, symbolically communicated by Vishnu's suppression of an asura who had taken the form of an elephant.
- The beginnings of the typical Nagara shikhara can also be seen in the **Mahadeva temple at Nachna Kuthara** (7th century) and the **brick Lakshmana temple at Sirpur** (both in MP).

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE NORTHERN STYLE: CHRONOLOGICAL AND GEOGRAPHICAL SPREAD</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Northern, Central, and Western India (5th–7th Centuries)</mark>

Key temples from this early period:
- **Parvati temple at Nachna** (south-east of Khajuraho, M.P.)
- **Dashavatara temple at Deogarh** (Jhansi District, U.P.)
- **Brick temple at Bhitaragon** (Kanpur District, U.P.)
- **Vishnu temple at Gop** (Gujarat)
- **Mundeshwari temple** - an unusual example of **octagonal plan** at Ramgarh, Bihar
- Temples at **Sanchi and Jigawa** (both in Madhya Pradesh)

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Deccan and Central India (6th–8th Centuries)</mark>

- Cave temples at **Ellora** (near Aurangabad, Maharashtra)
- **Elephanta** (near Mumbai)
- **Badami** (north Karnataka) - Early Chalukyan
- Early-Chalukyan temples in north Karnataka at **Badami, Aihole** (Ladkhan temples), and **Pattadakal** (Papanatha and Galganatha temples)
- Nagara style temples discovered at Aihole, Pattadakal, Alampur, and Mahakuteshwar
- The **Huchchappayya temple at Aihole** - the oldest temple showing the beginning of the **cruciform ground plan**. In this, the three components (garbha-griha, mandapa, and ardhamandapa) are built one after another forming a cruciform ground plan. The ardhamandapa is designed as an **open porch**.
- A slight variation is visible at **Jambulinga temple at Pattadakal** which has a **closed ardha-mandapa**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Western and Central India (8th–13th Centuries)</mark>

- **Harihara and other temples at Osian** (north of Jodhpur, Rajasthan)
- **Jelika Mandir** (Gwalior)
- **Chandella temples at Khajuraho** - especially Lakshmana, Kandariya Mahadeva, and Vishvanatha temples
- Temples at **Roda** (north of Modhera, Gujarat)
- **Sun temple at Modhera** (Gujarat)
- **Marble temples of the Jains at Mt. Abu** (Rajasthan)
- **Lingaraja temple** (11th century, Bhubaneswar)
- **Rajarani temple** (11th century, Bhubaneswar) - shows an interesting experiment with miniature shikharas clustered around the jangha as in the temples of western and central India including those at Khajuraho.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Eastern India (8th–13th Centuries)</mark>

- **Parashurameshvar, Vaital Deul, Mukteshvar, Lingaraj, and Rajarani temples** (all at Bhubaneswar)
- **Sun temple at Konarka** (Odisha)
- **Jagannatha temple at Puri** (Odisha)

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Himalayan Belt (8th Century Onwards)</mark>

- **Sun temple at Martand** (Kashmir)
- **Shiva temple at Pandrethan** (Kashmir)
- **Vishnu temple at Aventesvamin** (Kashmir)
- **Temple at Masrur** (Kangra, Himachal Pradesh) - a multi-towered temple excavated into a natural escarpment in the 9th century
- Brahmanical temples in Nepal (Kathmandu, Patan, Bhadgaon)

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">REGIONAL SUB-STYLES OF NAGARA ARCHITECTURE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Central India (Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan)</mark>

Ancient temples in Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan share common traits within the Nagara idiom.

#### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Khajuraho Temples (Chandela Kings, 10th Century)</mark>

Temples at Khajuraho, constructed by the **Chandela Kings** in the tenth century, show significant evolution of the Nagara style. They were built between the **7th and 10th centuries**.

**Lakshmana Temple** (dedicated to Vishnu):
- Built in **954 CE** by Chandela king **Dhanga** (also mentioned as completed by Yashovarman).
- Displays a **curved pyramidal fashion in shikharas** and prominent **amalaka** (horizontal fluted disc) and **kalasha** (vase).
- All Nagara temples from this period feature the standard crowning elements of **amalaka and kalasha**.
- This temple exhibits **projecting balconies and verandahs**, setting it apart from Deogarh.
- Temple plan is **panchayana type** with ardhamandapa, mandapa, maha-mandapa, and garbhagriha with vimana.
- Constructed on a **heavy plinth**, each part has a separate roof rising backwards.
- Projected porches on hall walls for light and ventilation (not accessible to visitors).
- Outer walls adorned with sculptures, especially on garbhagriha and circumambulatory path.
- Tall shikhara on garbhagriha, known for **erotic sculptures**, many carved on the plinth wall.
- Tier arrangements on walls provide specific spaces for image placement.
- **Chaturmukha Vishnu** image in the garbhagriha; four shrines in each corner featuring Vishnu and Surya.
- Drapery and ornaments receive meticulous attention in the sculptures.
- The entire structure is placed on a **high platform** with walls decorated with beautiful carvings.

**Kandariya Mahadeva Temple**:
- Built by Chandela rulers; **epitomizes Central Indian temple architecture during the medieval period** and is perhaps the **grandest of over 35 substantial temples** built at the Chandella capital.
- Represents the **climax of the mature Nagara style** of temple architecture (c. 1000 CE).
- Sculptures are **highly stylized**, often in full relief with sharp features like noses, chins, and slanting eyes.
- **Erotic sculptures (mithunas)** - embracing couple sculptures - are considered auspicious and are integral to the temple.
- The miniature spires (urusrngas) clustered around the main shikhara are characteristic features.
- At Khajuraho, a beginning was made with a **plain shikhara without any embellishment of the miniature spires** in the earlier examples, which became characteristics of the later ones.

**Vishwanatha Temple**: Another notable Khajuraho temple (also spelled Vishvanatha).

**Chausanth Yogini Temple** at Khajuraho: The oldest temple at the site, predating the tenth century, dedicated to goddesses associated with **Tantric worship**. Between the seventh and tenth centuries, a number of temples dedicated to the **cult of yoginis** are scattered across Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and extending as far south as Tamil Nadu.

**Khajuraho - Diversity**: The complex has numerous Hindu temples, Jain temples, and the Chausanth Yogini temple. Temples showcase the coexistence of spiritual and erotic expressions in Hindu temple art.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">West India (Gujarat, Rajasthan, Western Madhya Pradesh)</mark>

Temples in north-western India showcase a variety of stones:
- **Sandstone** is common.
- **Grey to black basalt** appears in 10th-to-12th-century temple sculptures.
- **Soft white marble**, manipulatable and exuberant, is seen in Jain temples at **Mount Abu** (10th–12th century) and the 15th-century temple at **Ranakpur**.

**Art-historical Site Samlaji (Gujarat)**:
- A significant art-historical site blending earlier regional traditions with a post-Gupta style.
- Grey schist sculptures in the region, dating between the **6th and 8th centuries CE**, showcase a distinctive style influenced by the mix of traditions.

**Sun Temple at Modhera**:
- Built in **1026 CE** by **Raja Bhimdev I** of the Solanki Dynasty.
- A prominent 11th-century structure.
- A massive rectangular stepped tank, **Surya Kund**, is situated in front of the temple (measuring a hundred square meters), featuring **108 miniature shrines** carved between its steps - showcasing the influence of water bodies in sacred architecture.
- The **sabha mandapa** is open on all sides, following the fashion of western and central Indian temples in the early 11th century.
- The temple's intricate carving and sculpture work reflect the influence of **Gujarat's wood-carving tradition**, evident in the lavish carving and sculptures.
- The **central small shrine, facing east**, remains plain to allow the sun to shine directly into it during the **equinoxes** - emphasizing the temple's connection with celestial events.
- Proximity to water bodies (tanks, rivers, ponds) becomes integral to temple architecture in the region.

**Other West Indian Temples**: Temples at **Kiradu, Ghumli, Sejakpur, Navlakha temples** (all in western India); **Sas-Bahu temple** at Gwalior; temples at **Osian** (Rajasthan).

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">East India - Odisha (Kalinga) Sub-Style</mark>

Odisha's temples form a **distinct sub-style within the Nagara order**. The main temple sites are located in ancient Kalinga (modern Puri District), including **Bhubaneswar (ancient Tribhuvanesvara), Puri, and Konark**.

**Classification of Odisha temples**: Classified into:
- **Rekhapida (Rekha deul)** - curvilinear shikhara type
- **Pidhadeul (Pidha deul)** - pyramidal/flat roof type
- **Khakhra** - barrel-vaulted type (apsidal)
- **Gaudiya** - the fourth type (mentioned in local manuscripts of Odisha)

The **Aparajitapriccha** (confining Nagara style to Madhyadesha) and local Odisha manuscripts both recognize these four main types of Odisha-style temples.

**Distinctive features of Odishan Nagara temples**:
- **Shikharas** (called **"deul"** in Odisha) are **vertical until the top, sharply curving inwards**, with **square ground plans**. They are characterized by a near-vertical rise with a sharp curve only near the top.
- **Deuls are preceded by mandapas** (called **"jagamohana"** in Odisha).
- Odishan temples often include **boundary walls**, distinguishing them from other northern styles.
- The exterior of the Nagara type is characterized by **horizontal tiers**, as in the jagamohana or porch in front of the sanctum of the Lingaraja temple at Bhubaneswar.
- The vimana is usually **circular in plan** (in Odisha context).
- The **natmandir** (dance hall) and **bhogamandapa** evolved subsequently in Odishan temples.

**Parashurameshvar Temple (Bhubaneswar)**:
- Late 6th–early 7th century. Described as "the **most complete early expression of the eastern Indian Nagara style** with tapering shikhara."

**Mukteshvar Temple** (10th century, Bhubaneswar): Represents the typical Odishan form.

**Rajarani Temple** (11th century, Bhubaneswar): Shows an interesting experiment with miniature shikharas clustered around the **jangha** (bottom portion of the spire), similar to temples of western and central India including Khajuraho.

**Lingaraja Temple** (11th century, Bhubaneswar): Shows the **culmination and grandeur** of this type of temple in Odisha.

**Sun Temple at Konark** (c. 1240 CE):
- A colossal creation - the shikhara reached **70 m** before collapsing in the 19th century.
- The vast complex is within a **quadrilateral precinct** of which the **jagamohana** (dance pavilion/mandapa) has survived.
- The temple **resembles a chariot** - its walls covered in detailed ornamental carvings, including **12 pairs of enormous wheels** representing the Sun god's chariot wheels.
- A **massive sculpture of Surya** carved out of green stone and strategically placed images captured the sun's rays in the garbhagriha.
- Iron beams were used in the sanctuary and hall.
- A **palm-leaf manuscript** concerning the building operations of the temple at Konark lists the workmen, their salaries, rules of conduct, and provides an account over several years of the various building operations.
- The portrait of **King Narasimha (1238–64)**, the builder of the Sun Temple at Konark, receiving spiritual instruction from his chief priest, is depicted on a panel in black shale. The figures are treated **hierarchically** - the priest is represented at a larger scale to define the correct guru-pupil relationship.

**Jagannatha Temple at Puri**: Another major Odishan Nagara temple.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Hills (Kumaon, Garhwal, Himachal Pradesh, Kashmir)</mark>

The hills of Kumaon, Garhwal, Himachal, and Kashmir gave rise to a **distinctive form of architecture**. Proximity to Gandhara sites like Taxila and Peshawar influenced Kashmir, blending with Gupta and post-Gupta traditions from Sarnath, Mathura, Gujarat, and Bengal. Frequent travels of Brahmin pundits and Buddhist monks contributed to the intermingling of Buddhist and Hindu traditions.

**Wooden Tradition in the Hills**: Hills maintained their own tradition of **wooden buildings with pitched roofs**. Temples often showcase a blend of styles - the main garbhagriha and shikhara in rekha-prasada or latina style, while the mandapa reflects an older form of wooden architecture. Some temples adopt a **pagoda shape**, highlighting the uniqueness of hill architecture. The sloping and gabled roofs which are preserved only in stone in the temples of Kashmir can be seen in these areas in pure wooden context.

**Kashmir - The Karkota Period (8th–9th Centuries)**:
- **Pandrethan temple**, possibly dedicated to Shiva - exemplifies Kashmir's wooden building tradition, featuring a peaked roof slanting outward. Moderately ornamented, it deviates from post-Gupta aesthetics, emphasizing simplicity with a row of elephants and a decorated doorway.
- **Sun temple at Martand** and **Vishnu temple at Aventesvamin** are important Kashmiri temples.
- In the Kashmir valley, temples bear **two or three roofs** which were copied from the usual wooden roofs. In stone buildings the interval between the two roofs is closed with ornaments.

**Temple at Masrur (Kangra, Himachal Pradesh)**: In the 9th century, a remarkable **multi-towered temple was excavated into a natural escarpment at Masrur** in Kangra.

**Nagara Architecture in Kumaon**:
- Temples at **Jageshwar** (near Almora) and **Champavat** (near Pithoragarh) in Kumaon represent classic examples of Nagara architecture in the region.
- The **Jageshwar valley** (35 km north-east of Almora, Uttarakhand) has **150 stone monuments**, almost 200 stone steles, many commemorative monuments, and has yielded a large number of copper alloy statues.
- Two clusters of temples: one at **Dandeshwar** in the middle of the valley, and the other at Jageshwar proper. Most temples are dedicated to Shiva.
- A few are rectangular and built in the **Valabhi mode**; the rest have square plans showing the selective influence of the Nagara style. But the artists and craftsmen experimented and created something new and different.
- The creative process involved many factors including the unique geographic setting in a thickly forested area, with many **devadara trees** (which have a special association with Shiva), along the north-northeast flowing stream called the **Jataganga**.
- The earliest phase of temple building at Jageshwar is dated to the **7th–8th century** - represented by shrines such as the **Nava Durga temple**. The iconography of images and representations of **Lakulisha** and **mukhalingas** (carved with one or five faces of Shiva - eka-mukhalingas and pancha-mukhalingas) indicate that the early temples were associated with the **Pashupata sect**.
- The **Mrityunjaya temple** (mid-9th to mid-10th century) reflects changes in architectural form, sculptural style, and an enhancement in the scale of patronage. It is the first temple in the valley with a large front hall attached.
- In the 10th century, the **Dandeshvara temple** became the largest temple in the valley.
- By the 12th century, the central Himalayas had become a part of larger pilgrimage networks.

**Chamba (Himachal Pradesh)**: Exhibits an amalgamation of local traditions with a post-Gupta style, seen in sculptures like Mahishasuramardini and Narasimha at the **Laksna-Devi Mandir**. Influenced by the metal sculpture tradition of Kashmir. An inscription at the temple, built during the reign of **Meruvarman** in the 7th century, indicates its historical significance.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">ECOLOGICAL SETTING, RAW MATERIALS, AND REGIONALISM</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Climate and Roof Forms</mark>

The stylistic evolution of temples was rooted in **ecological setting**:
- In **relatively heavy rainfall areas** of the western coast of India and Bengal, temples have **sloping tiled roofs**, giving rise to timber gables.
- To overcome hazards of snow and hail, **wooden sloped roofs** are also employed in the temples of the Himalayan belt.
- In general, the **hotter and drier the climate**, the **flatter the roof** - open porches provide shaded seating and pierced stone screens are used to filter the light.
- Features noticeable in the famous **Ladkhan temple of the Chalukyas at Aihole** (north Karnataka) are direct adaptations of **thatch and timber village and community halls**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Raw Materials</mark>

The availability of raw materials profoundly affected styles of craftsmanship:
- The transition from wood to stone (attributed to the Mauryas, 3rd century BCE) was a great step forward.
- **Hard and crystalline rocks** prevented detailed carving.
- **Soft and sedimentary stone** permitted great precision.
- **Friable and schist-like stones** (used by Hoysala architects at Belur and Halebid) promoted the carving of mouldings created by sharp and angled incisions.
- **Brick building traditions** continued where there was an absence of good stone - e.g., temples at Bishnupur in Bengal.
- The influence of **timber and bamboo** techniques represents a unique architectural development in north-eastern Assam.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Construction Techniques</mark>

An **organised building programme** was followed in the erection of structural temples:
- Bricks were baked either on or near the site; stone was mostly **quarried locally**.
- Stone from quarries was sometimes transported to the building site on **wooden rollers drawn by elephants** or **floated on barges** along rivers and canals.
- At the site, masons roughly shaped the stone blocks, which were then **hoisted into position by rope pulleys on scaffolding**.
- **Ramps were constructed of timber and sand** to facilitate placing extremely heavy stone pieces.
- A classic example is the stone constituting the huge shikhara of the **Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur**: the stone weighing about 80 tonnes was raised by being dragged on an inclined plane with its base about **seven kilometres away at Sarapallan** (literally meaning "elevation from depression").
- Occasionally, as in Konark, **iron beams** were used in the sanctuary and hall.
- The architects, artisans, and workmen engaged in temple building were organized into **groups functioning as guilds**.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">DECORATIVE ELEMENTS IN NAGARA TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Evolution of Ornamentation</mark>

The evolution of various styles in terms of decorations, ornamentations, and embellishments is a natural phenomenon, though these elements did not affect the basic structure of temples.

Key decorative elements:
- **Pillars** grew from simple oblong shafts in early Pallava structures to extremely finely chiselled columns.
- **Niches**, **pavilions**, and **horse-shoe-shaped windows (kudu)** are important decorative motifs that help delineate stages of evolution.
- The tendency is towards a constant increase in embellishments.
- **Shikharas, ceilings, and other walls** receive great attention from artisans and craftsmen.
- **Erotic carvings (mithunas)** on plinths and walls - considered auspicious symbols, fertility symbols, or Tantric representations.
- **Amalaka and kalasha** are standard crowning elements of all Nagara temples from the classical period.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Additive Geometry</mark>

As temples grew more complex, **additive geometry** was employed. This involved adding rhythmically projecting, symmetrical walls and niches without deviating from the fundamental shrine plan. This structural evolution is seen in changes occurring within different variations of Nagara temples. The multiplying forms of the tower envelop and protect the image chamber within.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Sculpture as Integral to Temple Form</mark>

Sculpture is **integral to the form and meaning** of the temple and is not merely surface adornment. It has an **organic presence, growing out of the very fabric and body of the temple**. From the outset, a predilection for surface decoration resulted in the development of a strong tradition of sculptural embellishment. This began as **relief panels enhanced with plaster and naturalistic painting**, sited on both interior and exterior surfaces.

There is nothing random or casual about the placement of icons - these icons are sited within a **formal structure of hierarchically ordered spaces** serving to define their place in the social order. This hierarchy of icons radiates out from the center of the temple, which resides in the cult icon in the garbhagriha.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">NAGARA STYLE IN DECCAN: EARLY CHALUKYAN EXPERIMENTS</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Early Chalukyan Temples (6th–8th Centuries)</mark>

**Pulakesin I** founded the western Chalukya kingdom around **Badami in 543 CE**. The early western Chalukyas dominated most of the Deccan until the mid-8th century. In Karnataka, the western Chalukyas blended various styles, leading to **experimental hybrid vesara architecture**. Early Chalukyan architectural activity included rock-cut caves, which evolved into structural temples.

The nagara style temples have been discovered at **Aihole, Pattadakal, Alampur, and Mahakuteshwar**. The temples at Alampur were built by their collateral branch, the **Chalukyas of Vengi**.

Some of the Chalukyan temples show evolution of the Nagara style:
- **Beginning of the cruciform ground plan** - oldest example is the **Huchchappayya temple at Aihole** (three components: garbha-griha, mandapa, ardhamandapa built in cruciform).
- The garbha-griha has the **typical Nagara shikhara** (curvilinear roof).
- **Jambulinga temple at Pattadakal** shows a slight variation with a **closed ardha-mandapa**.

At **Mahakuta** (near Badami), there are about 20 temples belonging to the early Western Chalukya period, almost all with **northern style curvilinear shikharas**.

The **Papanatha temple at Pattadakal** is the most notable northern style temple among the Chalukyan structures, built in the Nagara style (while also revealing attempts to combine northern and southern features). It is dedicated to Lord Shiva.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Durga Temple at Aihole</mark>

The **Durga temple at Aihole** is unique - it has an **early apsidal shrine reminiscent of Buddhist chaitya halls**, surrounded by a later veranda with a **stylistically Nagara shikhara**. The mandapa and verandah are basically in the Dravida style, while the **shikhara is a variety of the Nagara style**. The temple's apsidal design is also referred to as "**Gajaprastha**" (referring to the shape of an elephant's back). The Durga temple was probably built in about 725–730 CE during the reign of the Chalukya king Vijayaditya.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Lad Khan Temple at Aihole</mark>

The **Lad Khan temple at Aihole** in Karnataka is inspired by **wooden-roofed hill temples** but constructed entirely in stone. It is a flat-roofed building; the temples at Aihole were originally assembly halls and were later converted into temples. The Ladkhan temple also has a small shrine on its roof accessed by a staircase below.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Eastern Chalukyan Temples</mark>

Eastern Chalukyan temples at **Mahakuta** and **Swarga Brahma temples** show the assimilation of northern styles from Odisha and Rajasthan.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">BENGAL SUB-STYLE (WITHIN BROADER NAGARA CONTEXT)</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Evolution of Styles in Bengal</mark>

Bengal's temple styles vary from the **Pala style** (9th–11th centuries) to the **Sena style** (11th–13th centuries). Pala rulers, patrons of Buddhist monastic sites, influenced the local Vanga style in temples, such as the **Siddheshvara Mahadeva temple in Barakar**, evolving into loftier forms in subsequent centuries. Characterized by a **tall curving shikhara crowned by a large amalaka**, it shares similarities with contemporary temples in Odisha.

Temples submerged in **Telkupi, Purulia District**, showcased architectural styles aware of prevalent Nagara sub-types in North India. The black-to-grey basalt and chlorite stone pillars, along with arched niches in these temples, significantly influenced the **earliest Bengal sultanate buildings at Gaur and Pandua**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Terracotta Brick Temples in Bengal (17th Century)</mark>

**Terracotta brick temples** in Bengal, influenced by local vernacular traditions and Islamic architecture, proliferated in the seventeenth century. Prominent locations: **Vishnupur, Bankura, Burdwan, and Birbhum**.

Local vernacular building traditions played a key role - especially the shape of the **curving or sloping side of the bamboo roof** seen in Bengali huts. This distinctive feature, known as the **"Bangla roof"**, later found adoption in **Mughal buildings**, becoming a recognizable architectural element across North India. During the Mughal period and beyond, scores of terracotta brick temples were constructed across Bengal and Bangladesh. The temples of Bishnupur (such as the famous **Keshta Raya temple**, 17th century) are built with a variety of roof forms on square and rectangular plans. Architectural influence also extends to incorporating forms of **arches and domes taken from Islamic architecture**.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE MAHABODHI TEMPLE AT BODHGAYA (A UNIQUE CASE)</mark>

The **Mahabodhi Temple at Bodhgaya**, initially built by King Ashoka, underwent modifications over time. The vedika around the shrine is said to be post-Mauryan, about 100 BCE. The current temple structure reflects the Colonial Period reconstruction of the original 7th-century design.

The design of the temple is unusual: **"It is, strictly speaking, neither Dravida nor Nagara. It is narrow, like a Nagara temple, but it rises without curving, like a Dravida one."**

By the 12th century, the Mahabodhi temple had assumed, in its overall form, the essential elements of a Hindu Nagara-style temple (sanctum, porch, and tower) - as evidenced by a 12th-century Pala period model in black basalt. This Buddhist shrine had assumed the form of a Hindu Nagara-style temple, showing cross-tradition architectural influence.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">TEMPLES AND INDIAN CULTURAL ETHOS</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Social and Cultural Functions</mark>

Indian temples symbolized the very mundane urges of humans and were used for varied activities of the community as a whole:

- **General education**: Many endowments to temples were specifically made for the establishment of colleges incorporated into temple complexes. Teaching of grammar, astrology, recitation of the Vedas, the Epics (Ramayana and Mahabharata), and the Puranas were encouraged.
- **Music and dance**: Generally formed part of the daily ritual of temples and during special celebrations and annual festivals. Large temples maintained their own musicians (vocal and instrumental), together with dancers, actors, and teachers of performing arts.
- **Life-size delineations of musicians** in a 10th-century temple at Khajuraho and in the Sun temple at Konark provide eloquent testimonies.
- The **natamandir** (dancing hall) is an absolutely integral element in Odishan and other temples.
- The **devadasi institution**: Temple maidens played a significant role in dancing and singing of devotional hymns by which the temple god was entertained. Chola emperor Rajaraja I (984–1012 CE) constructed two long streets for the accommodation of **400 dancing women** attached to the Brihadishvara temple at Thanjavur.
- **Annual festivals** provided opportunities for mingling of mythology and folklore - e.g., the annual **rathayatra** of the Jagannatha temple at Puri.
- **Pilgrimage (tirthayatra)**: Another mechanism through which the participation of the community in temple activities was facilitated.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Economic Role of Temples</mark>

As temples provided work and the means of livelihood for a large number of persons, they were able to exert great influence on the economic life of people. Even small temples needed the services of priests, garland-makers, and suppliers of clarified butter, milk, and oil.

One of the most detailed accounts preserved of the number of people supported by a temple and their wages is found in an inscription on the **Thanjavur temple, dated 1011 CE**. The list includes cooks, gardeners, dance-masters, garland-makers, musicians, wood-carvers, painters, choir-groups for singing hymns in Sanskrit and Tamil, accountants, watchmen, and a host of other officials and servants - totalling **more than 600 persons**.

The medieval temple became more than a place solely devoted to the performance of pujas. As the wealthiest institutions within their communities, temples assumed other non-religious functions:
- **Banks**: Served as principal moneylenders.
- **Major landowners** and significant employers.
- Hosted an array of community activities, from weddings to theatrical and musical performances.
- Inscriptions at Rajaraja's Brihadishvara temple record that the temple was a major source of employment, having temple administrators, a chief accountant/treasurer, a staff of clerks, priests and their families, astrologers, dancers, musicians, goldsmiths, carpenters, washer men, guards, and caretakers.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">ORGANISATION OF THE BUILDING PROGRAMME</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Guilds and Labour Organisation</mark>

The architects, artisans, and workmen engaged in the various activities associated with the building of a temple were **organized into groups functioning as guilds**. The Konark temple manuscript lists the workmen, their salaries, and rules of conduct, and provides an account over several years of the various building operations.

An 11th-century panel from Khajuraho shows **cuttings, chiselling, and transporting stone** for temples.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">NAGARA STYLE vs. DRAVIDA STYLE: KEY DISTINCTIONS</mark>

| Feature | Nagara Style | Dravida Style |
|---|---|---|
| Geographic spread | North India (Himalayas to Vindhyas/Deccan) | South India (Krishna/Kaveri to Kanyakumari) |
| Ground plan | Square, cruciform projections | Square inner sanctum within large covered enclosure |
| Tower over sanctum | Curvilinear/convex shikhara (tapering, sometimes swelling) | Pyramidal vimana (progressively smaller storeys) |
| Crowning element | Amalaka + Kalasha | Stupika (small dome/octagonal cupola) |
| Term "Shikhara" | Refers to entire tower over the sanctum | Refers only to the small crowning element (equivalent to amalaka + kalasha of North India) |
| Boundary walls/gateways | Generally absent; no elaborate boundary walls | Enclosed within compound walls; prominent central gateway (gopuram) |
| River goddesses at entrance | Ganga and Yamuna at garbhagriha entrance | Dvarapalas (doorkeepers) guard gateways/gopurams |
| Platform | Stone platform with steps | Temple set within enclosure |
| Mandapa names | Mandapa, antarala, ardhamandapa | Often jagamohana (Odisha), or sabha mandapa |
| Term for sanctum tower | Shikhara (general north Indian term) | Vimana (pyramidal tower, especially in Tamil Nadu) |
| Emphasis on height | Shikhara grows taller over centuries | Main temple often has smallest tower; gopurams grow larger over time |
| Water bodies | Less integral (some exceptions like Modhera) | Large temple tanks/reservoirs integral to complex |

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE VESARA STYLE (HYBRID BETWEEN NAGARA AND DRAVIDA)</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Definition and Nature</mark>

The term **"Vesara"** literally means **"mule"** - implying a hybrid. It is a style that **borrowed from the northern (Nagara) and southern (Dravida) styles**. It is difficult to define precisely, as the mixture of northern and southern elements may vary from temple to temple.

Some scholars recognize the Vesara style as an **independent form resulting from the selective integration of Nagara and Dravida elements**. The term is "not free from vagueness" - some of the texts ascribe the Vesara style to the country between the **Vindhyas and the river Krishna**, but there are texts placing it between the Vindhyas and the Agastya (the location of which is uncertain).

Scholar Adam Hardy (1995) points out that Nagara and Dravida should be understood as **architectural languages**, providing a vocabulary, a range of elements, and a family of forms which can be put together in different ways. He suggests that the term **"Karnata-Dravida"** is a better term than Vesara for the Chalukya temples of the Deccan.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Prototypes and Early Examples</mark>

The **Buddhist chaitya halls** of the early centuries CE situated in the Western Ghats in Maharashtra may be said to be **prototypes of this style**. Its most conspicuous feature is the **apsidal ground plan**.

Early examples (7th–10th centuries):
- Structures at **Chezarla** (Andhra Pradesh)
- **Aihole** - Durga temple
- **Mahabalipuram** - Sahadeva and Draupadi rathas
- Kerala Shiva temples at **Trikkandiyur and Tiruvannur**

Post-10th-century classic examples:
- **Nataraja shrine at Chidambaram** (Tamil Nadu)
- **Vamana temple at Kizhavellur** (District Kottayam, Kerala)

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Chalukyan Vesara Architecture</mark>

Scholars acknowledge the hybridized style called **Vesara** which emerged around the **mid-seventh century**, distinct from the Nagara and Dravida styles. Early Western Chalukyas (based at Badami) blended various styles, leading to experimental hybrid Vesara architecture.

The Chalukyan buildings showcase a hybridization of styles. The **Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal** is a notable example exhibiting elaborate Dravida tradition, while the **Papanatha temple at Pattadakal** is an example reflecting Nagara tradition/northern style.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Hoysala Temples (Stellate-Plan / Vesara)</mark>

The **Hoysalas**, succeeding the Cholas and Pandyas, became prominent patrons in South India. In the southern Deccan, approximately a **hundred temples** have been discovered.

Temples at **Belur, Halebidu (Halebid), and Somnathpuram** are characterized by:
- **Stellate-plan (star-shaped)** - growing extremely complex
- **Soapstone (chlorite schist) carvings** - extremely intricate
- A unique **Vesara style blending Dravida and Nagara elements**

**Hoysaleshvara temple at Halebid, Karnataka**:
- Constructed in **1150 CE** by the Hoysala king using **dark schist stone**.
- One of the three most frequently discussed Hoysala temples (alongside Belur and Somnathpuram).
- Dedicated to Shiva as Nataraja.
- A **dual structure** with a mandapa for music and dance.
- Features intricate carvings of animals and deities.
- A presumed **portrait of the architect** (holding a rule and discus) is represented in a prominent position on the temple exterior.

***

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">THE EARLY MEDIEVAL PERIOD AND TEMPLE BUILDING</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Royal Patronage</mark>

The post-Gupta period (650–1300 CE) witnessed brisk building activity with experiments in various temple forms. The art of the post-Gupta centuries was **supported mainly by kings of different principalities, feudatories, and military chiefs** who alone could patronise large-scale art activities.

Temples served as **representatives of the might and glory of the kings** who had them built. The loftier the temple, the greater was the might reflected. There was indeed a definite correlation. The construction of large temples and their regular maintenance required the mobilization of huge amounts of resources, both financial and human.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Medieval Factor in Sculpture</mark>

Stylistically, the sculptures adorning Nagara and other temples of the early medieval period are characterized by what art historian **Niharranjan Rai** describes as the **"medieval factor"**:
- A certain amount of **slenderness** and an accent on **sharp angles and lines**
- The roundness of bodily form acquires **flatness**
- The curves lose their convexity and turn into the **concave**
- **Western and central Indian sculptures**, eastern Indian and Himalayan metal images, Gujarati and Rajasthani book and textile illustrations - all registered this new conception of form from the post-10th centuries

The creative climax of each art-region is not reached at the same time:
- Bihar and Bengal: **9th and 10th centuries**
- Odisha: **12th and 13th centuries**
- Central India: **10th and 11th centuries**
- Rajasthan: **10th century**
- Gujarat: **11th century**
- The far south: **10th–11th centuries**
- The Deccan alone shows a story of increasing torpor and ceases to be a sculptural province after the 8th century

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Divine Hierarchy in Temple Iconography</mark>

All over the country, post-Gupta iconography prominently displays a **divine hierarchy reflecting the pyramidal ranks in feudal society**. Vishnu, Shiva, and Durga appear as supreme deities lording over many other divinities of unequal sizes, placed in lower positions as retainers and attendants. The supreme Mother Goddess is clearly established as an independent divinity in iconography from this time.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">KEY TERMINOLOGY SUMMARY (NAGARA STYLE)</mark>

The following is a comprehensive listing of all important Nagara-style architectural terminology as discussed across the sources:

| Term | Meaning/Significance |
|---|---|
| Nagara | The North Indian temple style; literally relates to the city/urban form |
| Garbhagriha | The innermost sanctum - "womb chamber" - housing the primary deity |
| Vimana | The sanctuary unit (sanctum + its superstructure) |
| Shikhara | The curvilinear tower/spire above the garbhagriha in Nagara temples |
| Latina / Rekha-Prasada | The simplest, most archetypal form of Nagara shikhara - square base with curving/sloping walls culminating in a point |
| Phamsana | Broader, shorter Nagara shikhara type with multiple slabs rising to a single point; often used for mandapas |
| Valabhi | Rectangular wagon-vaulted type of Nagara building; roof rises into a rounded vaulted chamber |
| Amalaka | Notched disc-like stone at the summit of the shikhara; signature Nagara motif |
| Kalasha | The pot/vase-shaped finial atop the amalaka; crowning element |
| Mandapa | Pillared hall for congregating devotees |
| Ardhamandapa | Entrance porch/antechamber before the main mandapa |
| Antarala | Vestibule connecting vimana and mandapa |
| Pradakshinapatha | Circumambulatory passage around the sanctum |
| Panchayatana | Five-shrine layout - main shrine + four subsidiary shrines at corners |
| Urusringa | Miniature subsidiary spires on the main shikhara (Khajuraho style) |
| Jangha | The wall portion / lower part of the spire |
| Bhadra | The main offset/projection on the shikhara where icons are placed |
| Deul | Odishan term for the shikhara/entire temple |
| Jagamohana | Odishan term for the mandapa/hall in front of the sanctum |
| Natmandir | Dance hall (especially in Odishan temples) |
| Bhogamandapa | Hall for food offerings |
| Gavaksha | Horse-shoe arch/window ornamental motif |
| Kudu | Horseshoe-arch ornamental window motif |
| Torana | Ceremonial arched gateway motif |
| Stambha | Pillar or column |
| Dhvajastambha | Standard/flag pillar before the sanctum |
| Pitha/Jagati | Raised stone platform/plinth on which the temple stands |
| Devakoshthas | Wall niches framing sculptural icons |

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">NOTABLE INDIVIDUAL TEMPLES</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Dashavatara Temple, Deogarh (Early 6th Century CE)</mark>

Already described in detail under Section 5.5. It is the **locus classicus** of early Nagara style: panchayatana layout, curvilinear latina shikhara, Ganga-Yamuna doorway sculptures, and large relief panels of Vishnu (Sheshashayana, Nara-Narayan, Gajendramoksha) on the three remaining outer walls. The west-facing orientation is notable. The original dedication of the four subsidiary shrines remains unknown.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Lakshmana Temple, Khajuraho (954 CE)</mark>

- Built by Chandela king **Dhanga** (also noted as completed by Yashovarman) in 954 CE.
- Dedicated to **Vishnu**.
- **Plan type**: Panchayana (also called panchayatana) - with ardhamandapa, mandapa, maha-mandapa, and garbhagriha with vimana; four subsidiary shrines at corners.
- Constructed on a **heavy plinth**.
- **Projecting balconies and verandahs** are distinctive features.
- Outer walls adorned with sculptures, especially on garbhagriha and circumambulatory path.
- Tall shikhara with prominent **amalaka** and **kalasha**.
- **Erotic sculptures** on the plinth wall.
- **Chaturmukha Vishnu** image in the garbhagriha; four shrines in corners featuring Vishnu and Surya.
- Represents **developed Chandella temple architecture**.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Kandariya Mahadeva Temple, Khajuraho (c. 1000 CE)</mark>

- **Epitomizes Central Indian temple architecture** during the medieval period.
- **Grandest of over 35 substantial temples** at the Chandella capital.
- Represents the **climax of the mature Nagara style**.
- Highly stylized sculptures in full relief with sharp features.
- Erotic mithuna sculptures are integral and considered auspicious.
- Characteristic **urusrngas** (miniature spires) cluster around the main shikhara, evoking a mountain cluster.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Parashurameshvar Temple, Bhubaneswar (Late 6th–Early 7th Century CE)</mark>

- The **most complete early expression of the eastern Indian Nagara style** with tapering shikhara.
- Demonstrates the **remarkably mature style** of the 8th century in which models advance extremely quickly, producing some of the most advanced temples of the period.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Sun Temple, Konark (c. 1240 CE)</mark>

- Colossal creation in the **Surya cult** tradition.
- Builder: **King Narasimha (1238–64)** of the Eastern Ganga period.
- Shikhara reached **70 m** before collapsing in the 19th century.
- Within a quadrilateral precinct; the **jagamohana (dance pavilion)** has survived.
- Temple **resembles a chariot** with 12 pairs of enormous wheels representing the Sun god's chariot.
- Massive sculpture of Surya in **green stone**.
- Elaborate sculptural decoration on all surfaces.
- Iron beams used in construction.
- Palm-leaf manuscript records building operations in detail.
- Portrait of king and chief priest depicted hierarchically - priest at larger scale.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Sun Temple, Modhera, Gujarat (1026 CE)</mark>

Already detailed in Section 7.2 above. Built by Raja Bhimdev I of the Solanki Dynasty. Features Surya Kund (stepped tank with 108 miniature shrines), open sabha mandapa, solar alignment during equinoxes, and rich wood-carving influenced sculptural decoration.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Lingaraja Temple, Bhubaneswar (11th Century CE)</mark>

- Shows the **culmination and grandeur** of the Odishan Nagara sub-style.
- Exterior characterized by **horizontal tiers** in the jagamohana (porch in front of the sanctum).
- The vimana is usually circular in plan.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Mahabodhi Temple, Bodhgaya</mark>

Discussed in Section 12. A unique temple that is "neither Dravida nor Nagara" but narrow like a Nagara temple while rising without curving like a Dravida one. By the 12th century Pala period, it had assumed the essential elements of a Hindu Nagara-style temple.

## <mark style="background: #FFB86CA6;">SIGNIFICANCE OF NAGARA ARCHITECTURE</mark>

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Evolution as a Continuous Process</mark>

The development of Indian temple design was largely concerned with the ways in which **multiplication and elaboration** could be achieved. The elements are organic in the sense that they **multiply and reveal themselves, one form giving birth to the next** in a chain reaction. Thus the temple is built on fundamental Indian concepts of **the infinite in the finite**, and the cyclical process of creation, dissolution, and recreation of forms.

By the **7th–8th centuries**, the mature Nagara style emerged with the gradual extension of the porch into a long ante-room - the Malegitti Shivalay temple at Badami and the Virupaksha temple at Pattadakal are superb examples. In eastern India, this phase is represented by the remarkably mature style of the **Parashurameshvar temple in Bhubaneswar** (8th century).

The most elaborate expression of the mature Nagara style is the **Kandariya Mahadeva temple at Khajuraho** (built by the Chandella rulers).

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cross-Traditional Influence</mark>

The northern style is also witnessed in a Buddhist shrine - the **Mahabodhi** - which had assumed, by the 12th century, the essential elements of a Hindu Nagara-style temple. This demonstrates how the Nagara architectural vocabulary permeated across religious boundaries.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Regionalism as the Defining Feature</mark>

The emergence of regional architectural styles - of which Nagara is the dominant northern expression - is the **most arresting feature** of Indian cultural traditions between the 8th and 13th centuries. These traditions are rooted in ecological settings, availability of raw materials, patronage systems, and the interplay of local and pan-Indian artistic vocabularies. Nagara, Dravida, and Vesara represent not just building styles, but distinct **architectural languages** that could be adapted, hybridized, and innovated upon across centuries and regions.

### <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Temple as the Axis of Urban Life</mark>

In the second half of the first millennium CE, developments in urban planning placed the **temple at the axial centre of the town or city**. This represented a significant reorientation in the organization of urban space. The temple town was an urban complex with a large temple at its centre, **defining the orientation of streets** and directing the flow of traffic to itself. The medieval temple became more than a place solely devoted to performance of pujas - it was the **economic, social, and spiritual hub** of the community.
