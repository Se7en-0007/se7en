## Rupee Appreciation: Concept, Causes & Effects

### Definition & Numerical Representation
* **Rupee Appreciation** = Increase in value of Indian Rupee with respect to foreign currencies (**US Dollar**, **Euro**, **Pound**, **Yen**, **Yuan**).
* **Exchange Rate Shift**: Change from $1 = ₹60 → $1 = ₹30.
* **Interpretation**: Earlier paid ₹60 to get $1; now pay ₹30 to get same $1 → Fewer rupees needed → **Rupee value increased** (Dollar value decreased).

### Cause of Appreciation: Dollar Surplus
* **Core Cause** = **Surplus of Dollars** in domestic market (Dollar Inflow > Dollar Outflow).
* **Driving Factors**:
  * **Trade Balance**: **Exports > Imports**.
  * **Capital Inflow**: Foreign Investment in India (**FDI / FPI**) > Outward Investment by Indians.
  * **Remittances**: Inward Remittances (NRI/Indian working in US sending money back) > Outward Remittances (foreigners working in India sending money home).

![[FC 1.png|500]]

### Impact on Stakeholders
* **Importers (Gainers)**:
  * Pay fewer rupees for same dollar imports ($1 = ₹30 → $1 = ₹15).
  * Imports become cheaper → **Imports into India increase**.
* **Exporters (Losers)**:
  * Earn fewer rupees when converting export dollars ($1 = ₹30 → $1 = ₹15).
  * Net export earnings fall → **Exports from India decrease**.

### Layman Misconception vs. Economic Reality
* **Childhood Perception**: Belief that **$1 = ₹1** equals standard of living in US.
* **Economic Reality**: Large-scale appreciation causes **severe damage to economy**:
  1. **Destroys Export-Oriented Industries**: IT sector (**Infosys** exporting $1 software earns ₹1 instead of ₹85), Engineering goods, Pharmaceuticals.
  2. **Hurts Domestic Manufacturing**: Cheap foreign imports flood market (e.g. luxury foreign goods imported cheaply → demand for domestic Maruti cars/manufactures collapses).
* **Dutch Disease** = Adverse effect on domestic manufacturing and national economy caused by large-scale appreciation of domestic currency.

## Rupee Depreciation vs. Devaluation & Appreciation vs. Revaluation

### Classification by Driving Force

| Outcome | Triggered by Market Forces (Demand & Supply) | Triggered by Central Bank (RBI) Intervention |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fall in Rupee Value** ($1 = ₹30 → $1 = ₹60) | **Rupee Depreciation** | **Rupee Devaluation** (RBI buys dollars → injects rupees) |
| **Rise in Rupee Value** ($1 = ₹60 → $1 = ₹30) | **Rupee Appreciation** | **Rupee Revaluation** (RBI sells dollars → sucks out rupees) |

### Comparative Analysis: What is Good for the Economy ?
* **Rupee Depreciation**: Makes essential imports (Crude Oil) costly → Triggers **Imported Inflation**.
* **Rupee Appreciation**: Makes imports cheaper & reduces export earnings → Causes **Dutch Disease** (hurts domestic manufacturing).
* **Conclusion**: **Both sudden appreciation and sudden depreciation are equally bad** for Indian economy in short run.
* **Long-Run Vision (Viksit Bharat 2047)**:
  * Focus is NOT to artificially inflate Rupee value.
  * Focus is **Atmanirbhar Bharat** → Boost domestic manufacturing → **Exports ↑ & Imports ↓**.
  * As a natural byproduct, Dollar Inflow > Outflow → Rupee **may appreciate naturally** in long run, but target is **Viksit Bharat**, not currency appreciation itself.

## Market Stabilization Scheme (MSS) & Sterilization

### Purpose & Problem Statement
* **Problem**: To stop Rupee appreciation, RBI buys surplus dollars → Injects massive rupees into economy → Triggers **Inflation**.
* **Solution**: RBI must suck back excess rupees injected during dollar purchase.
* **Tool** = **Market Stabilization Scheme (MSS)** (introduced for liquidity absorption).
* **Sterilization Operations** = Central bank operations (OMO / MSS) designed to absorb excess domestic currency liquidity injected through foreign exchange purchases.

### Mechanics of MSS
1. RBI issues special **MSS Bonds** to commercial banks.
2. Banks buy MSS bonds using excess rupees → Excess liquidity sucked out.
3. RBI holds collected money in a **separate dedicated account** (NOT given to Government).

### Key Differences: OMO vs. MSS

| Parameter | Open Market Operations (OMO) | Market Stabilization Scheme (MSS) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Action Type** | Buying OR Selling G-Secs | **Selling ONLY** (MSS Bonds) |
| **Liquidity Impact** | Injects OR Sucks out liquidity | **Sucks out liquidity ONLY** |
| **Government Debt Status** | Part of **Government Borrowings** (Govt spends funds) | **NOT part of Government Borrowings** (Parked with RBI) |
| **Current Operational Status** | Active primary tool | Largely replaced by **Forex Swaps** |

## Forex Swaps: Sell/Buy vs. Buy/Sell

### Definition & Concept
* **Forex Swap** = Two-leg transaction where RBI exchanges Rupees and US Dollars with commercial banks simultaneously agreeing to reverse transaction at a future date at a fixed exchange rate.
* **Core Rule for Impact Evaluation**: Always judge monetary & liquidity impact **immediately after FIRST LEG** of swap.

### Forex Sell/Buy vs. Forex Buy/Sell Swaps

| Parameter | Forex Sell/Buy Swap | Forex Buy/Sell Swap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **First Leg Action** | RBI **Sells Dollars** to banks → **Sucks out Rupees** | RBI **Buys Dollars** from banks → **Injects Rupees** |
| **Second Leg Action** | RBI **Buys back Dollars** ← Returns Rupees | RBI **Sells back Dollars** ← Takes back Rupees |
| **Adopted During** | **Rupee Depreciation** + **Inflation** | **Rupee Appreciation** + **Economic Slowdown** |
| **Impact on Forex Reserves** | **Decreases** (after Leg 1) | **Increases** (after Leg 1) |
| **Impact on Rupee Liquidity** | **Decreases** (Rupees sucked out) | **Increases** (Rupees injected) |
| **Impact on Interest Rates** | Bank liquidity ↓ → **Loan Rates ↑** | Bank liquidity ↑ → **Loan Rates ↓** |
| **Exchange Rate Impact** | Prevents Depreciation (**Revaluation**) | Prevents Appreciation (**Devaluation**) |
| **Policy Stance** | **Contractionary** (controls inflation) | **Expansionary** (promotes growth during slowdown) |

## Yield Rate Management & Bond Yield Basics

### Background & COVID-19 Context
* **COVID-19 Pandemic Impact**: Government borrowings surged drastically to fund stimulus/relief.
* **Consequence**: Heavy borrowing → Surge in **Bond Yields**.
* **Why High Bond Yields in Slowdown are Harmful**:
  1. Increases Government's borrowing cost.
  2. Increases interest rates on commercial loans across economy.
  3. Discourages private capital investment during economic downturn.
* **RBI Interventions to Lower Bond Yields**:
  1. **Open Market Operations (OMO)**
  2. **Operation Twist**
  3. **Government Securities Acquisition Program (G-SAP)**

### The Three Immutable Characteristics of a Bond
1. **Face Value** (e.g. ₹200)
2. **Coupon Rate** (e.g. 10% per annum)
3. **Maturity Period** (e.g. 5 Years)

* **GOLDEN RULE**: These 3 parameters **NEVER CHANGE** throughout the tenure of the bond in both **Primary** and **Secondary** markets.
* **Interest Income**: Calculated as Coupon Rate on Face Value (Coupon Rate * Face Value). Absolute interest payout remains **fixed** (e.g., 10% of ₹200 = ₹20/year).

### Bond Price Dynamics
* **Bond Price** = Price at which investor buys bond in primary/secondary market.
* **Market Mechanics**: Behaves like **Apples** (Demand ↑ → Bond Price ↑; Demand ↓ → Bond Price ↓).
* **Primary Market**: Dated bonds generally issued at **Face Value** (except T-Bills/CMBs issued at discount).
* **Secondary Market**: Bond price fluctuates continuously based on demand/supply.
* **Critical Rule**: Interest is ALWAYS paid on **Face Value**, NEVER on Bond Price!

### Bond Yield Concept & Universal Formula
* **Bond Yield** = Annual percentage rate of return on actual investment in a bond.
* **Formula**:

$$\text{Bond Yield} = \left(\frac{\text{Interest Income}}{\text{Bond Price}}\right) \times 100$$

* **Inverse Relationship**: **Bond Yield is INVERSELY proportional to Bond Price**.

$$\text{Bond Price} \uparrow \implies \text{Bond Yield} \downarrow \quad \mid \quad \text{Bond Price} \downarrow \implies \text{Bond Yield} \uparrow$$

### Numerical Illustration (Example)

| Investor / Scenario | Purchase Price (Bond Price) | Absolute Interest Income | Bond Yield Calculation | Resulting Bond Yield |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Investor (Issuer Price)** | **₹200** (Equal to Face Value) | ₹20 | (20 / 200) * 100 | **10.00%** |
| **Investor X (Secondary Market)** | **₹220** (Price Surge) | ₹20 | (20 / 220) * 100 | **9.09%** |
| **Investor Y (Secondary Market)** | **₹210** (Price Dip) | ₹20 | (20 / 210) * 100 | **9.52%** |

## Factors Affecting Bond Yields

### Direct Proportionality Rules
Bond yields in India are **DIRECTLY PROPORTIONAL** to 3 core macroeconomic factors:

$$\text{Bond Yield} \propto \text{Government Borrowings}$$
$$\text{Bond Yield} \propto \text{Rate of Interest (India \& US Fed)}$$
$$\text{Bond Yield} \propto \text{Rate of Inflation}$$

### Causal Chain 1: Government Borrowings → Bond Yield

![[FC 2.png|700]]

* *Reverse*: Government Borrowings ↓ → G-Sec Supply ↓ → Bond Price ↑ → **Bond Yield ↓**

### Causal Chain 2: Rate of Inflation & Interest Rates → Bond Yield

![[FC 3.png|700]]

* *Reverse (Economic Slowdown)*: Inflation ↓ or Expansionary Policy → Bank Deposit Interest Rates ↓ → Bonds become MORE attractive → Demand for Bonds ↑ → Bond Price ↑ → **Bond Yield ↓**

  1. Actions of United States Federal Reserve.
  2. Actions of Reserve Bank of India.
  3. Inflation and short-term interest rates.
