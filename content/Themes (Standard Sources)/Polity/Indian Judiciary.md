## Foundational Concepts, Separation of Powers and Constitutionalism

The Indian judiciary is structured as a singular, integrated system that stands at the pinnacle of the constitutional order. Unlike federal models such as that of the United States, which feature a double system of courts (federal and state operating in parallel), India possesses a unified judicial hierarchy. This structural integration ensures the uniform administration of civil, criminal, and constitutional law throughout the territory of India.

![[Pasted image 20260826163051.png|400]]

### Constitutionalism and The Rule of Law
**Constitutionalism** is the political-legal philosophy that insists governance must be conducted in accordance with established, predictable constitutional norms, thereby placing institutional limitations on the exercise of arbitrary power. It transforms the classical "police state" (which focuses primarily on law and order and external defense) into a modern **social welfare state**. In a social welfare state, the government’s operations expand exponentially to promote socioeconomic justice, necessitating robust mechanisms of **judicial review** to ensure state actions conform to the fundamental law of the land and protect individual liberties.

The **Rule of Law**, which forms the bedrock of the Indian Constitution, acts as a shield against the exercise of arbitrary or capricious executive or legislative power. The judiciary acts as the ultimate guardian and interpreter of the Constitution, ensuring that no branch of government exceeds its mandated powers.

### Separation of Powers and Judicial Independence
The concept of the **Separation of Powers** between the legislature, the executive, and the judiciary has been elevated by the Supreme Court of India to the level of the **Basic Structure of the Constitution**. 

*   **Article 50**: Located in the Directive Principles of State Policy, this article mandates that the State shall take steps to separate the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State. This separation is vital to insulate judicial officers from executive pressure, ensuring they can administer justice impartially, "without fear or favour."
*   **Article 121**: Imposes a strict constitutional restriction on discussions in Parliament. It states that no discussion shall take place in Parliament with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court in the discharge of his duties, except upon a substantive motion for presenting an address to the President praying for the removal of the Judge.
*   **Article 211**: Mirrors Article 121 at the state level, providing that no discussion shall take place in the Legislature of a State with respect to the conduct of any Judge of the Supreme Court or of a High Court in the discharge of his duties.

These provisions collectively protect judges from political criticism and insulate the judiciary from populist and parliamentary pressures.

## The Supreme Court of India (Articles 124–147)

The Supreme Court of India was inaugurated on January 28, 1950, succeeding the Federal Court of India (established under the Government of India Act of 1935). It replaced the British Privy Council as the highest court of appeal, meaning its jurisdiction and powers are far more extensive than those of its predecessors. **Articles 124 to 147** in Part V of the Constitution govern the organization, independence, jurisdiction, powers, and procedures of the Supreme Court.

### Composition, Appointment and The Collegium System
#### **Article 124: Establishment and Constitution of The Supreme Court**
*   **Article 124(1)**: Establishes the Supreme Court of India, consisting of a Chief Justice of India (CJI) and, until Parliament by law prescribes a larger number, of not more than seven other Judges. Parliament has progressively increased the maximum number of judges using its legislative powers under this article:
    *   *Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Amendment Act, 2019*: Fixed the maximum number of judges at **33 (excluding the Chief Justice of India)**, bringing the total strength to **34**.
*   **Article 124(2)**: Governs the appointment of judges. Every Judge of the Supreme Court is appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal. 

#### **Evolution of The Appointment Process: The Judges' Cases**
The constitutional text originally mandated that the President appoint judges "after consultation" with such judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts as deemed necessary. The interpretation of the word **"consultation"** has undergone a dramatic judicial evolution across four landmark cases:

1.  **First Judges Case (*S.P. Gupta v. Union of India*, 1981)**: The Supreme Court held that "consultation" does not mean "concurrence." It ruled that the executive (the Central Cabinet acting through the President) held final primacy in appointments, and the opinion of the CJI was not binding.
2.  **Second Judges Case (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India*, 1993)**: The Court overruled *S.P. Gupta*. It interpreted "consultation" to mean **"concurrence."** It held that the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court must be appointed as the CJI. For other appointments, the CJI must tender advice to the President after consulting a **Collegium** of two senior-most colleagues. The executive could return a recommendation for reconsideration, but if the Collegium unanimously reiterated the recommendation, the executive was bound to make the appointment.
3.  **Third Judges Case (*In Re-Presidential Reference*, 1998)**: On a presidential reference under Article 143, a nine-judge bench expanded the Collegium. It held that the CJI must consult a **Collegium of four senior-most judges** of the Supreme Court. If even two judges express an adverse opinion, the CJI cannot send the recommendation. Recommendations made without complying with these norms are not binding on the Government.
4.  **Fourth Judges Case / The NJAC Judgment (2015)**: 
    *   The **99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014** had inserted **Articles 124A, 124B, and 124C** to establish a **National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC)** to replace the Collegium system.
    *   The NJAC was to consist of: (a) the CJI as Chairperson, ex officio; (b) two other senior Supreme Court judges; (c) the Union Law Minister; and (d) two "eminent persons" nominated by a committee of the Prime Minister, the CJI, and the Leader of the Opposition.
    *   In 2015, a five-judge bench struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act as unconstitutional and void. The Court ruled that:
        *   The judicial component was denied adequate representation, violating the **Independence of the Judiciary**, which is a part of the basic structure.
        *   The inclusion of the Law Minister introduced political influence into judicial appointments.
        *   The term "eminent persons" was dangerously vague and undefined, threatening the integrity of the selection process.
    *   Consequently, the **Collegium system was fully restored**.

### Qualifications, Tenure and Removal of Judges
#### **Qualifications (Article 124(3))**
To be eligible for appointment as a Supreme Court judge, a person must be a citizen of India and:
1.  Must have been for at least **five years** a Judge of a High Court (or of two or more such courts in succession); OR
2.  Must have been for at least **10 years** an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more such courts in succession); OR
3.  Must be, in the opinion of the President, a **distinguished jurist**. 
*Note: No minimum age is prescribed for appointment.*

#### **Tenure and Contingencies**
A judge holds office until they attain the **age of 65 years** (Article 124(2)). A judge may cease to hold office by:
*   Resignation: Writing under their hand addressed to the President (Article 124(2)(a)).
*   Removal: By the President upon a parliamentary address (Article 124(2)(b)).

#### **Removal Process (Article 124(4) & (5))**
A Supreme Court Judge can only be removed on the grounds of **"proved misbehaviour"** or **"incapacity."** The procedure is highly rigid and involves both constitutional mandates and statutory rules under the **Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968**:
1.  A motion for removal must be signed by at least **100 members of the Lok Sabha** or **50 members of the Rajya Sabha** and presented to the Speaker/Chairman.
2.  If admitted, the Speaker/Chairman constitutes a **three-member committee** consisting of: (a) a Supreme Court judge, (b) a Chief Justice of a High Court, and (c) a distinguished jurist.
3.  The committee investigates the charges. If it finds the judge guilty of misbehaviour or suffering from incapacity, the House can consider the motion.
4.  The motion must be passed by each House of Parliament by a **Special Majority**:
    *   A majority of the **total membership** of that House, AND
    *   A majority of **not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting** in that House.
5.  Once passed, an address is presented to the President in the same session, who then issues an order of removal.
6.  *Article 121* prevents any discussion on the floor of the House regarding the judge's conduct until the committee has submitted its report and the motion is formally taken up.

#### **Post-Retirement Restriction (Article 124(7))**
No person who has held office as a permanent Judge of the Supreme Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority within the territory of India. This maintains the dignity of the office and prevents potential conflicts of interest.

### Salaries, Working Arrangements and Court Administration
*   **Article 125**: Establishes that the salaries, allowances, leave, and pension of judges are determined by Parliament by law. Crucially, *Article 125(2)* provides that neither the privileges nor the allowances of a judge, nor their rights in respect of leave or pension, shall be varied to their disadvantage after appointment. The only exception is during a **Financial Emergency under Article 360(4)(b)**, where the President may order a temporary reduction. Currently, the salary of the CJI is ₹2,80,000 per month, and for other judges, it is ₹2,50,000 per month.
*   **Article 126**: Provides for the appointment of an **acting Chief Justice of India** by the President when the office of the CJI is vacant, or when the CJI is absent or unable to perform their duties.
*   **Article 127**: Governs the appointment of **ad hoc Judges**. If at any time there is a lack of quorum of Supreme Court judges to hold or continue a session, the CJI, with the previous consent of the President and after consultation with the Chief Justice of the High Court concerned, may request in writing the attendance of a High Court judge (who is fully qualified to be an SC judge) to sit as an ad hoc judge.
*   **Article 128**: Enables the CJI, with the previous consent of the President, to request a **retired Supreme Court Judge** or a retired High Court Judge (qualified for SC appointment) to sit and act as a Judge of the Supreme Court. The retired judge must consent to this request.
*   **Article 129**: Declares the Supreme Court to be a **Court of Record**. It possesses all the powers of such a court, including the inherent power to punish for contempt of itself and subordinate courts.
*   **Article 130**: Designates the **seat of the Supreme Court** as Delhi. However, it is an enabling provision; the CJI may, with the approval of the President, appoint any other place or places as the seat of the Supreme Court.

## Jurisdictions and Powers of The Supreme Court

The Supreme Court of India is a multi-jurisdictional apex court, frequently characterized as one of the most powerful courts in the world. Its jurisdictions can be categorized into distinct, major areas:

![[Pasted image 20260826163331.png|700]]

### Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)
As a federal court, the Supreme Court possesses exclusive original jurisdiction to adjudicate disputes between different constituent units of the Indian Federation:
*   **Categories of Disputes**:
    1.  Between the Government of India (Centre) and one or more States.
    2.  Between the Centre and any State(s) on one side and one or more other States on the other side.
    3.  Between two or more States *inter se*.
*   **Key Requirement**: The dispute must involve a question of law or fact on which the existence or extent of a **legal right** depends. Purely political questions are excluded.
*   **Exclusions from Original Jurisdiction**:
    1.  Disputes arising out of any pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, or sanad which continues in force, or which explicitly excludes SC jurisdiction (*Article 131 Proviso* and *Article 363(1)*).
    2.  Inter-State water disputes referred to a statutory tribunal under **Article 262** and the **Inter-State Water Disputes Act, 1956**.
    3.  Matters referred to the **Finance Commission (Article 280)**.
    4.  Adjustment of certain expenses and pensions between the Union and States (*Articles 257(4), 258(3), and 290*).
*   **Locus Standi**: Only the constituent units of the federation (Union and States) can file a suit under Article 131. Private individuals, statutory corporations, or government companies cannot invoke this jurisdiction (*State of Bihar v. Union of India*).

### Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32)
*   **Article 32**: Guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the Fundamental Rights. The right to move the SC is itself a **Fundamental Right**.
*   The Supreme Court can issue directions, orders, or writs, including **habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari**.
*   **Original yet Non-Exclusive**: It is original because an aggrieved citizen can go directly to the Supreme Court without filing an appeal. However, it is concurrent with the High Court's writ jurisdiction under **Article 226**.
*   *Article 32* cannot be suspended except during a National Emergency as provided by the Constitution under **Article 359**.

### Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132–136)
The appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court is categorized by the nature of the cases:

#### **Constitutional Matters (Article 132)**
An appeal lies to the Supreme Court from any judgment, decree, or final order of a High Court, if the High Court certifies under **Article 134A** that the case involves a **substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution**.

#### **Civil Matters (Article 133)**
An appeal lies in civil proceedings if the High Court certifies under Article 134A that:
*   The case involves a substantial question of law of **general importance**, AND
*   In the opinion of the High Court, the question **needs to be decided by the Supreme Court**.

#### **Criminal Matters (Article 134)**
An appeal lies to the Supreme Court in criminal matters in two ways:
*   **As of Right (Without Certificate)**:
    1.  If a High Court has, on appeal, reversed an order of acquittal of an accused person and sentenced them to death.
    2.  If a High Court has withdrawn a case from a subordinate court for trial before itself, convicted the accused, and sentenced them to death.
*   **With Certificate**: If the High Court certifies under Article 134A that the case is a fit one for appeal to the Supreme Court. Parliament has also enacted the *Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970*, which expands these rights to cases where the sentence is life imprisonment or ten years or more.

#### **Certificate for Appeal (Article 134A)**
Inserted by the **44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978**, this article streamlines the certification process. A High Court may grant a certificate on its own motion, or on an oral application made by or on behalf of the party aggrieved, immediately after passing the judgment.

#### **Existing Federal Court Jurisdiction (Article 135)**
The Supreme Court possesses jurisdiction over any matter to which Articles 133 or 134 do not apply, if the jurisdiction and powers in relation to that matter were exercisable by the Federal Court immediately before the commencement of the Constitution.

#### **Appeal by Special Leave (Article 136)**
*   **Article 136(1)**: Confers an extraordinary, discretionary, and plenary power on the Supreme Court. The court may grant **Special Leave to Appeal (SLP)** from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence, or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India.
*   **Plenitude of Power**: This is a residuary, sweeping power. It is unaffected by the limitations of Articles 132, 133, and 134. It can be granted against both final and interlocutory orders, in constitutional, civil, criminal, income-tax, labour, or revenue matters.
*   **Exclusion (Article 136(2))**: The Supreme Court cannot grant special leave to appeal against any judgment, determination, sentence, or order passed by any court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the **Armed Forces** (military tribunals and court-martials).
*   **Locus Standi**: The court has adopted a highly liberal view of locus standi under Article 136. A third party who is not a party to the original suit can file an SLP if there is a gross miscarriage of justice (*P.S.R. Sadhanantham v. Arunachalam*, 1980).

### Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)
The President of India can seek the advisory opinion of the Supreme Court under two distinct categories:

| Parameter | Article 143(1) - Question of Law/Fact of Public Importance | Article 143(2) - Pre-Constitution Treaties & Agreements |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin** | Arises on questions of law or fact of public importance which have arisen or are likely to arise. | Arises on disputes arising out of any pre-Constitution treaty, agreement, covenant, or sanad. |
| **SC Duty** | The Supreme Court **may** tender or **may refuse** to tender its opinion (discretionary). | The Supreme Court **must** tender its opinion to the President (mandatory). |
| **Binding Nature** | The opinion is purely advisory and **not binding** on the President. | The opinion is purely advisory and **not binding** on the President. |
| **Examples & Cases** | *Delhi Laws Act Case*, *In Re Special Reference No 1 of 1998* (Collegium), *Ayodhya Case* (where SC declined to answer). | References regarding pre-Constitution covenants with Princely States. |

*Note: While the advisory opinion is not a judicial pronouncement and is not binding on the President, the propositions of law declared by the Supreme Court during such references are binding on all subordinate courts under Article 141.*

### Extraordinary Substantive Powers

#### **Power of Review (Article 137)**
Subject to any parliamentary legislation or rules made under Article 145, the Supreme Court has the power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it. This is used to correct any patent errors apparent on the face of the record or grave injustices.

#### **Power To Do Complete Justice (Article 142)**
*   **Article 142(1)**: Empowers the Supreme Court to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing **"complete justice"** in any cause or matter pending before it.
*   Any such decree or order is enforceable throughout the territory of India.
*   **Scope**: This is an elastic, supplementary, and independent source of substantive power. The Court has characterized it as an "untrammelled reservoir of power." It is not limited by statutory provisions. If there is a "legislative vacuum" or a rigid statutory barrier that causes manifest injustice, the Supreme Court can bypass it to do complete justice (e.g., granting a divorce on the ground of an "irretrievable breakdown of marriage" under Article 142, even though the Hindu Marriage Act does not explicitly list it as a ground).
*   **Limitations**: The Court cannot exercise this power in direct conflict with an express, major statutory provision, and must only use it when otherwise seized of valid jurisdiction (*Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India*).

#### **Miscellaneous Administrative and Declaratory Articles**
*   **Article 138**: Parliament can enlarge the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court with respect to any matter in the Union List. Its jurisdiction can also be expanded via special agreements between the Centre and States.
*   **Article 139**: Parliament may by law confer on the SC the power to issue writs for purposes other than the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
*   **Article 139A**: Governs the transfer of cases. If cases involving the same or substantially the same questions of law are pending before the SC and one or more High Courts, or before multiple High Courts, the SC can withdraw these cases and dispose of them itself. It also empowers the SC to transfer any case, appeal, or other proceeding from one High Court to another.
*   **Article 140**: Parliament may by law confer ancillary, supplemental powers on the SC to enable it to exercise its jurisdiction more effectively.
*   **Article 141: Law Declared by the Supreme Court to be Binding**: The law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India. This establishes the doctrine of **stare decisis** and judicial uniformity.
*   **Article 144**: All authorities, civil and judicial, in the territory of India shall act in aid of the Supreme Court.
*   **Article 145: Rules of Court**: The Supreme Court is empowered to make rules for regulating the practice and procedure of the court (including rules on advocates practicing before it, division of benches, and appeals). These rules require the approval of the President and are subject to laws made by Parliament.
*   **Article 146**: Ensures administrative independence. Appointments of officers and servants of the Supreme Court are made by the CJI or designated officers. The conditions of service are prescribed by rules made by the CJI. Rules relating to salaries, allowances, leave, or pensions require the approval of the President due to their financial implications. The administrative expenses of the Supreme Court are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India** and are non-votable.
*   **Article 147**: Clarifies that references to the interpretation of the Constitution include references to the interpretation of the Government of India Act, 1935, and the Indian Independence Act, 1947.

## The High Courts in The States (Articles 214–231)

The High Court stands at the head of a State's judicial administration. Unlike federal systems, High Courts are not completely separated from the central Union; they form part of a singular, integrated judicial hierarchy. **Articles 214 to 231** in Part VI of the Constitution govern the organization, jurisdictions, and powers of the High Courts.

### Composition, Appointment and Conditions of Service
*   **Article 214**: Mandates that there shall be a High Court for each State.
*   **Article 215**: Declares every High Court to be a **Court of Record**, possessing the power to punish for contempt of itself and subordinate courts.
*   **Article 216**: Governs the composition. Every High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other Judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary to appoint. The Constitution does not specify the maximum strength of a High Court, leaving it entirely to the discretion of the President based on workload.
*   **Article 217: Appointment and Qualifications**:
    *   **Appointment**: High Court judges are appointed by the President. In making the appointment, the President must consult: (a) the Chief Justice of India; (b) the Governor of the State; and (c) in the case of appointment of a judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court.
    *   **Judicial Consultation Process**: Under the Collegium system, the CJI must consult a Collegium of the **two senior-most judges of the Supreme Court** who are familiar with the High Court of the State. The proposal is initiated by the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court.
    *   **Qualifications (Article 217(2))**: A person must be a citizen of India, not being over 62 years of age, and must have:
        1.  Held for at least **10 years** a judicial office in the territory of India; OR
        2.  Been for at least **10 years** an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more such courts in succession).
        *Note: Unlike the Supreme Court, there is no provision in Article 217 for appointing a "distinguished jurist" as a High Court judge.*
    *   **Age Disputes (Article 217(3))**: If any question arises as to the age of a High Court Judge, the question is decided by the President after consultation with the CJI, and the decision of the President is final.
*   **Article 218**: Applies certain provisions of the Supreme Court (specifically Article 124, clauses (4) and (5) regarding impeachment and removal) to the High Courts.
*   **Article 219**: Judges must make and subscribe an oath or affirmation before the Governor of the State (or a person appointed by him) according to the form set out in the Third Schedule.
*   **Article 220**: Imposes a restriction on practice. No person who has held office as a permanent Judge of a High Court shall plead or act in any court or before any authority in India except the Supreme Court and other High Courts. They are barred from practicing in the High Court where they were permanent, and in any subordinate courts.
*   **Article 221**: Dictates salaries and allowances. High Court Judges are paid salaries determined by Parliament. Currently, the Chief Justice of a High Court receives ₹2,50,000 per month, and other High Court Judges receive ₹2,25,000 per month. Their pensions are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of India**, whereas their active salaries are **charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State**. Their allowances and rights cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment.
*   **Article 222**: Governs the transfer of judges. The President may, on the recommendation of the CJI, transfer a judge from one High Court to any other High Court. The CJI must consult a Collegium of **four senior-most SC judges** along with the Chief Justices of both the sending and receiving High Courts.
*   **Article 223**: Appointment of an acting Chief Justice of a High Court.
*   **Article 224: Appointment of Additional and Acting Judges**:
    *   **Additional Judges (Article 224(1))**: The President may appoint qualified persons to be additional judges for a temporary period **not exceeding two years** to clear arrears of work.
    *   **Acting Judges (Article 224(2))**: Appointed when a permanent judge is temporarily absent or acting as Chief Justice.
    *   *Age Limit*: Neither an additional nor an acting judge can hold office beyond **62 years** of age.
*   **Article 224A**: The Chief Justice of a High Court, with the previous consent of the President, can request a retired High Court Judge to sit and act as a Judge of the High Court.

### Jurisdictions of The High Courts (Articles 225–228)
*   **Article 225**: Preserves the pre-constitutional jurisdiction and powers of existing High Courts. Crucially, it removed a historical restriction from the British era, which had barred High Courts from exercising original jurisdiction over matters concerning **public revenue** or its collection.
*   **Article 228**: If a High Court is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, the High Court **must withdraw the case** and may:
    *   Dispose of the case itself, OR
    *   Determine the question of law and return the case to the subordinate court along with its judgment, directing the subordinate court to dispose of the case in conformity with the judgment.

## Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226) and Supervisory Powers (Article 227)

The most significant and frequently invoked powers of the High Court are its constitutional writ jurisdiction under **Article 226** and its power of superintendence under **Article 227**. These form part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

### Comparison of Article 226 and Article 32
While both Articles 32 and 226 empower the superior courts to issue the same set of five prerogative writs, their scopes and territorial reaches differ significantly:

| Parameter | Article 32 (Supreme Court) | Article 226 (High Court) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature of Right** | Moving the SC is itself a **Fundamental Right**. The SC cannot refuse to entertain a valid petition. | It is a **constitutional, discretionary power** granted to the High Court. The HC can decline to exercise it. |
| **Scope of Jurisdiction** | Can issue writs **only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights** (Part III). | Can issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights **AND "for any other purpose"** (ordinary legal rights). |
| **Territorial Reach** | Throughout the entire territory of India against any person, authority, or government. | Limited to the territorial limits of the State, OR outside if the **cause of action** arises wholly or partly within its territory. |
| **Status of Jurisdiction** | Original, apex power. | Original, supervisory power. |

### The Concept of "Cause of Action" in Article 226(2)
Originally, a High Court could only issue writs to authorities physically located within its boundaries. This caused great hardship; for example, a litigant in a distant state had to travel to New Delhi to challenge an order of the Central Government. 

The **15th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1963** inserted **Article 226(2)** to resolve this. It provides that a High Court can issue writs to any government, authority, or person located outside its state boundaries, if the **"cause of action" arises wholly or partly within its territorial jurisdiction**.
*   **Definition**: A cause of action refers to the bundle of essential, material facts that are imperative for the suitor to allege and prove to support their right to a judgment.
*   **Application**: A cause of action arises at: (a) the place where the impugned order was made, and (b) the place where its consequences fall on the person concerned. A writ petition challenging the constitutionality of a central law is not maintainable in the Delhi High Court merely because the seat of the Union is in Delhi; it can be filed in any High Court where the cause of action arises.

### Types and Scope of Prerogative Writs
The High Courts and Supreme Court can issue five types of writs to control public, administrative, and judicial authorities:

![[Pasted image 20260826163657.png|700]]

#### **Habeas Corpus ("To Have the Body")**
*   **Purpose**: To secure the immediate release of a person who has been unlawfully or arbitrarily detained.
*   **Scope**: It is a powerful shield for personal liberty under Article 21. It can be issued against both public authorities and private individuals.
*   **Locus Standi**: Relaxed. A relative or friend can file a petition on behalf of the detained person.

#### **Mandamus ("We Command")**
*   **Purpose**: To compel a public official, statutory body, corporation, inferior court, or the government to perform a mandatory public or statutory duty that they have refused to perform.
*   **Scope**: There must be a legal right in the petitioner and a corresponding mandatory public duty in the respondent.
*   **Exceptions**: Mandamus **cannot** be issued:
    *   Against the President of India or State Governors in their personal capacities (*Article 361*).
    *   Against private individuals or private bodies.
    *   To enforce a purely contractual obligation.
    *   To enforce a departmental instruction that lacks statutory force.
    *   When the duty is discretionary and not mandatory.
    *   To compel the legislature to enact a law.

#### **Prohibition ("To Forbid")**
*   **Purpose**: Issued by a higher court to an inferior court or tribunal to prevent the latter from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction it does not legally possess.
*   **Key Distinction**: Unlike Mandamus, which directs activity, **Prohibition directs inactivity**.
*   **Target**: Can only be issued against **judicial or quasi-judicial authorities**; it does not lie against purely administrative bodies or private individuals.

#### **Certiorari ("To Be More Fully Informed")**
*   **Purpose**: Issued by a higher court to an inferior court or tribunal to quash an order or decision already passed.
*   **Grounds for Certiorari**:
    1.  Excess of jurisdiction or lack of jurisdiction.
    2.  Violation of the principles of **Natural Justice** (e.g., *audi alteram partem* - the right to a fair hearing).
    3.  An **error of law apparent on the face of the record** (a patent, obvious error that does not require elaborate argument to prove).
*   **Target**: Originally limited to judicial and quasi-judicial bodies. However, modern judicial rulings have obliterated the distinction between administrative and quasi-judicial actions. Certiorari can now be issued to quash purely administrative decisions if they affect civil rights and violate natural justice.
*   *Note: Judicial orders of a civil court are not amenable to certiorari under Article 226; instead, the High Court exercises supervisory control over them under Article 227.*

#### **Quo Warranto ("By What Authority")**
*   **Purpose**: To inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a public office of a permanent character created by statute or by the Constitution.
*   **Locus Standi**: Any public-spirited citizen can file a petition, even if they have no personal grievance regarding the office.
*   **Requirement**: The office must be a public office, not a private one, and must be held in clear violation of statutory or constitutional rules.

### The "Alternative Efficacious Remedy" Rule
The High Court's jurisdiction under Article 226 is highly discretionary. A key self-imposed limitation is that the High Court **ordinarily does not entertain a writ petition if an alternative, equally efficacious statutory remedy is available** to the litigant.
*   **Nature of the Rule**: It is a rule of policy, convenience, and discretion, not a rule of law. The existence of an alternative remedy does not oust the jurisdiction of the High Court.
*   **Exceptions**: A writ petition is directly maintainable despite the existence of an alternative remedy in the following cases:
    1.  For the enforcement of a **Fundamental Right**.
    2.  Where there is a clear **violation of the principles of Natural Justice**.
    3.  Where the impugned order or proceeding is passed **completely without jurisdiction**.
    4.  Where the constitutionality or **validity of a statute is challenged**.
    5.  Where the alternative remedy is too burdensome, onerous, or ill-suited to the situation.

### Laches (Undue Delay)
No limitation period is prescribed for filing a writ petition under Article 226. However, under the doctrine of **laches**, the High Court may refuse to exercise its discretionary jurisdiction if the petitioner has approached the court after an inordinate, unexplained delay. Courts do not assist those who sleep over their rights and allow stale claims to arise, as this would unsettle settled matters. The court evaluates each case based on its specific facts.

### Power of Superintendence (Article 227)
*   **Article 227(1)**: Confers on every High Court the power of **superintendence over all courts and tribunals** throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction (except military courts and tribunals constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces under *Article 227(4)*).
*   **Dual Superintendence**: This power is both **administrative** (prescribing forms, calling for returns, settling tables of fees) and **judicial**.

#### **Distinct Differences between Article 226 and Article 227**
1.  **Jurisdiction Type**: Article 226 is an exercise of the High Court's **original jurisdiction**, whereas Article 227 is a **supervisory jurisdiction** that is corrective and akin to a revisional or appellate corrective power.
2.  **Scope of Remedy**: Under Article 226, the High Court can only annul or quash an order (e.g., via Certiorari) but cannot substitute its own decision. Under Article 227, the High Court possesses broader powers; it can not only quash an order but also **substitute its own decision** or give directions to guide the subordinate court on how to proceed.
3.  **Initiation**: Article 226 must be formally invoked by an aggrieved party. Article 227 can be exercised **suo motu** (on the court's own motion) by the High Court.
4.  **Target**: Article 226 writs are directed against persons, authorities, and the State. Article 227 is strictly directed against subordinate courts and tribunals.

### High Court Administration and Staff (Article 229)
To ensure the absolute independence of the High Courts, **Article 229** vests complete control over the court's officers and staff in the Chief Justice:
*   Appointments of officers and servants of a High Court are made by the Chief Justice or designated judges.
*   The Governor of the State may by rule require consultation with the State Public Service Commission for certain appointments.
*   The conditions of service are determined by rules made by the Chief Justice. Rules relating to salaries, allowances, leave, or pensions require the approval of the Governor.
*   The administrative expenses of the High Court are **charged upon the Consolidated Fund of the State**, making them non-votable.

## The Subordinate Judiciary (Articles 233–237)

The subordinate courts function below the High Court and are in direct, daily contact with the public. **Articles 233 to 237** in Chapter VI of Part VI of the Constitution govern their organization, recruitment, and independence.

### Structural Hierarchy of Subordinate Courts
The organizational structure, nomenclature, and jurisdictions of subordinate courts are determined by the respective States, resulting in slight regional variations. However, the generic three-tier hierarchy is structured as follows:

![[Pasted image 20260826163816.png|500]]

#### **The District and Sessions Judge**
*   The highest judicial officer in a district.
*   Possesses dual capacities: when presiding over civil matters, they are known as the **District Judge**; when presiding over criminal cases, they are known as the **Sessions Judge**.
*   Exercises both judicial (original and appellate) and administrative powers.
*   **Powers**: The Sessions Judge can impose any sentence authorized by law, including life imprisonment and **capital punishment (death sentence)**. However, any death sentence passed by a Sessions Judge is subject to mandatory **confirmation by the High Court**, whether or not the convicted person files an appeal.

### Appointment of District Judges (Article 233)
*   **Article 233(1)**: Appointments, posting, and promotion of district judges in a State are made by the **Governor of the State in consultation with the High Court** exercising jurisdiction in relation to such State.
*   **Sources of Recruitment**:
    1.  **Promotion from Judicial Service**: For persons already in the judicial service of the State. The Governor appoints them based on the recommendation and in consultation with the High Court.
    2.  **Direct Recruitment from the Bar (Article 233(2))**: A person not already in the service of the State or Union is eligible only if:
        *   They have been an advocate or a pleader for **not less than seven years**.
        *   Their appointment is explicitly **recommended by the High Court**.
*   **Mandatory and Purposive Consultation**: The consultation with the High Court is a mandatory, constitutional condition precedent. The Governor cannot bypass the High Court's recommendations or appoint any person whose name is not in the panel submitted by the High Court (*State of Kerala v. A. Lakshmikutty*, 1987).
*   **Scope of "Service of the State"**: The Supreme Court has ruled that the phrase "service of the State" in Article 233(2) refers exclusively to the judicial service of the State. Executive officers, police officers, or excise officers are ineligible for appointment as district judges (*Chandra Mohan v. State of U.P.*, 1966).

### Recruitment of Subordinate Judges (Article 234)
Appointments of persons other than district judges to the judicial service of a State are made by the Governor in accordance with rules made by him for the purpose after consultation with the **State Public Service Commission (SPSC)** and the **High Court**.
*   **Procedure**: The SPSC conducts competitive examinations. The Supreme Court has mandated that a sitting Judge of the High Court, nominated by the Chief Justice, must participate as an expert in the interview process. The expert opinion of the High Court Judge must ordinarily be accepted by the SPSC (*Ashok Kumar Yadav v. State of Haryana*, 1987).

### Control over Subordinate Courts (Article 235)
To safeguard subordinate judges from executive interference, **Article 235** vests complete administrative and disciplinary control over them in the High Court:
*   This control extends to district courts and courts subordinate thereto, including postings, promotions, transfers, grant of leave, disciplinary inquiries, suspensions, and compulsory retirement.
*   **Binding Nature**: The High Court's recommendations regarding the compulsory retirement or disciplinary punishment of a subordinate judge are **binding on the Governor**, who is the formal appointing and dismissing authority (*State of West Bengal v. Nripendra Nath Bagchi*).

### Interpretations and Extensions (Articles 236–237)
*   **Article 236**: Defines key terms:
    *   **"District Judge"**: Includes judge of a city civil court, additional district judge, joint district judge, assistant district judge, chief judge of a small cause court, chief presidency magistrate, additional chief presidency magistrate, sessions judge, additional sessions judge, and assistant sessions judge.
    *   **"Judicial Service"**: Means a service consisting exclusively of persons intended to fill the post of district judge and other civil judicial posts inferior to the post of district judge.
*   **Article 237**: Empowers the Governor to direct, by public notification, that the constitutional provisions of this chapter (Articles 233–236) and rules made thereunder shall apply to any class or classes of **magistrates** in the State. This enables the formal integration of the magistracy into the independent judicial service, completing the separation of the judiciary from the executive.

## Quasi-Judicial Bodies, Tribunals and Administrative Adjudication

The expansion of governmental operations in the modern administrative age has led to a proliferation of adjudicatory bodies outside the traditional court hierarchy. These are commonly designated as **quasi-judicial bodies** or **tribunals**.

### Meaning and Characteristics of Quasi-Judicial Bodies
Quasi-judicial bodies are administrative authorities that have been vested with the power to adjudicate disputes and decide questions affecting the civil rights of citizens. They are not courts "pure and simple," but they partake of some features of both courts and the administration.
*   **Trappings of a Court**: A body is characterized as a "tribunal" if it possesses the "trappings of a court," which include:
    1.  The power to compel the attendance of witnesses and examine them on oath.
    2.  The duty to follow the fundamental rules of evidence and natural justice.
    3.  The power to impose legal sanctions (imprisonment, fines, damages).
    4.  The investiture of a part of the inherent **judicial power of the State** by statute or rule (*A.P.H.L. Conference, Shillong v. W.A. Sangma*, 1977).
*   **Distinction**: Unlike courts, tribunals are not bound by the strict, technical rules of the *Code of Civil Procedure, 1908* or the *Indian Evidence Act, 1872*. They are bound primarily by the principles of **Natural Justice** and statutory guidelines, ensuring speedy, inexpensive, and technically expert adjudication.

### Part XIV-A: Tribunals (Articles 323A and 323B)
The **42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976** introduced **Part XIV-A** to the Constitution, containing two articles designed to facilitate "tribunalisation" and reduce the backlog of cases in High Courts.

#### **Article 323A: Administrative Tribunals**
*   **Establishment**: Empowers **Parliament alone** to provide by law for the establishment of administrative tribunals to adjudicate disputes relating to the recruitment and conditions of service of public servants (Union, State, local bodies, public corporations).
*   **Statutory Implementation**: Pursuant to Article 323A, Parliament enacted the **Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985**, establishing:
    *   **Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)**: Headed by a Chairman, with regular benches across the country. It exercises original jurisdiction over service disputes of central government employees.
    *   **State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)**: Established by the Central Government on the specific request of State Governments. SATs can also be abolished by the Central Government if the concerned State requests it (*Orissa Administrative Tribunal Bar Association v. Union of India*, 2023).

#### **Article 323B: Tribunals for Other Matters**
*   **Establishment**: Empowers the **appropriate legislature** (both Parliament and State Legislatures, within their respective fields of legislative competence) to provide for the establishment of tribunals for the adjudication of disputes, complaints, or offences relating to:
    1.  Levy, assessment, collection, and enforcement of taxes.
    2.  Foreign exchange, import, and export.
    3.  Industrial and labour disputes.
    4.  Land reforms.
    5.  Ceiling on urban property.
    6.  Elections to Parliament and State Legislatures (excluding the general bar of *Article 329*).
    7.  Production, procurement, and distribution of foodstuffs.
    8.  Rent and tenancy rights.

#### **Key Differences Between Article 323A and Article 323B**

| Parameter | Article 323A | Article 323B |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Subject Matter** | Limited strictly to **public service matters** (civil servants' service disputes). | Relates to **other specific matters** (taxation, land reforms, labour, rent, etc.). |
| **Establishing Authority** | Established **only by Parliament** by law. | Can be established by **both Parliament and State Legislatures** based on their legislative lists. |
| **Structure & Hierarchy** | Contemplates only a single central/state tribunal structure; **no provision for a hierarchy** of tribunals. | Specifically provides for the establishment of a **hierarchy of tribunals**. |

### Judicial Review Over Tribunals: The Landmark *L. Chandra Kumar* Case (1997)
#### **The Exclusion of Jurisdiction Saga**
*   **Original Position**: Both Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d) authorized the legislatures to exclude the jurisdiction of all courts, including the writ jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and the Supreme Court under Article 32, leaving only the Supreme Court's discretionary Special Leave jurisdiction under Article 136.
*   **SP Sampath Kumar Case (1987)**: The Supreme Court originally upheld the validity of these exclusion clauses, stating that Parliament could substitute High Courts with administrative tribunals, provided the tribunals were viable and effective "real substitutes."

#### **The Ruling in *L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India* (1997)**
A seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court unanimously overruled *SP Sampath Kumar* and declared clauses ousting High Court jurisdiction as **unconstitutional**. The Court established the following landmark principles:
1.  The power of **judicial review** vested in High Courts under Articles 226/227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is an **inviolable Basic Feature of the Constitution**, which cannot be abridged or excluded even by a constitutional amendment.
2.  Tribunals established under Articles 323A or 323B **cannot act as independent substitutes** for High Courts. Instead, they perform a **supplemental role**.
3.  Tribunals function as **courts of first instance** in their respective areas of law. All litigants must approach the concerned tribunal first; they cannot bypass the tribunal and go directly to the High Court under Article 226.
4.  Crucially, **every decision of a tribunal is subject to scrutiny before a Division Bench of the High Court** within whose territorial jurisdiction the tribunal falls.
5.  Direct appeals from tribunals to the Supreme Court under Article 136 are **abolished**. An aggrieved party must move the High Court under Articles 226/227 first, and only against the High Court's Division Bench judgment can they appeal to the Supreme Court via Article 136.

### Key Examples of Quasi-Judicial Bodies and Tribunals
1.  **Election Commission of India**: While primarily an administrative body under Article 324, it acts in a quasi-judicial capacity when deciding symbol disputes between rival factions of political parties or determining the supervening disqualifications of members (*A.P.H.L. Conference, Shillong v. W.A. Sangma*).
2.  **The Speaker of the House**: Presiding officers of the legislatures act as tribunals when adjudicating questions of disqualification of members under the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law) (*Kihoto Hollohon v. Zachilhu*, 1993).
3.  **National Green Tribunal (NGT)**: Established under the *National Green Tribunal Act, 2010* for the effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection. Appeals from the NGT lie directly to the Supreme Court under Section 22 of the Act.
4.  **Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions**: A three-tier machinery established at the District, State, and National levels under the *Consumer Protection Act, 2019*. They possess the trappings of a court and are regarded as tribunals subject to certiorari and Article 136.
5.  **National Tax Tribunal (NTT) Case (2014)**: Parliament enacted the *National Tax Tribunal Act, 2005* to transfer the High Court's jurisdiction over substantial questions of tax law to a centralized tribunal. In *Madras Bar Association v. Union of India* (2014), the Supreme Court declared the entire NTT Act unconstitutional. The Court held that while the legislature can transfer certain adjudicatory powers to tribunals, the new body must incorporate the same level of independence, structural integrity, and characteristics of the traditional courts it seeks to replace.
6.  **Unlawful Activities Prevention Tribunal**: Under the *Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967*, any executive notification declaring an association "unlawful" must be confirmed by a tribunal headed by a sitting High Court Judge to prevent the arbitrary exercise of executive power.

## Supplementary Constitutional Organs and Adjudicatory Jurisdictions

To ensure complete coverage of the constitutional landscape regarding the judiciary and quasi-judiciary, several supplementary provisions and alternative mechanisms are structured within the constitutional framework:

### The All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) (Article 312)
*   **Constitutional Provision**: **Article 312(1)** empowers the Rajya Sabha to pass a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest to create an All-India Judicial Service (AIJS) common to the Union and the States.
*   **Structural Safeguards (Article 312(4))**: The article explicitly mandates that the AIJS **shall not include any post inferior to that of a district judge** as defined in Article 236. The recruitment of judicial officers below the rank of district judge remains within the exclusive domain of the respective State Governments and High Courts under Article 234.
*   **Constitutional Amendment Exemption**: Any law providing for the creation of the AIJS may contain necessary provisions for the amendment of the Subordinate Courts chapter (Chapter VI of Part VI) and is **not to be deemed an amendment of the Constitution** for the purposes of Article 368.

### High Courts for Union Territories (Article 241)
*   **Constitutional Authority**: **Article 241(1)** empowers Parliament by law to constitute a High Court for a Union Territory, or to declare any court in such a territory to be a High Court for all or any of the purposes of the Constitution.
*   **Extension/Exclusion (Article 230)**: Parliament is authorized by law to extend the jurisdiction of a High Court to, or exclude the jurisdiction of a High Court from, any Union Territory. For example, the Kerala High Court exercises jurisdiction over Lakshadweep, the Calcutta High Court over the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, and Chandigarh falls under the jurisdiction of the Punjab and Haryana High Court.

### Establishment of Additional Courts (Article 247)
*   **Constitutional Authority**: **Article 247** empowers Parliament to provide by law for the establishment of any **additional courts** for the better administration of laws made by Parliament or of any existing laws with respect to a matter enumerated in the **Union List** (List I of the Seventh Schedule). This power enables the creation of highly specialized judicial bodies to enforce central statutes (e.g., Special Courts under various central acts).

### Special Supreme Court Inquiry Jurisdictions (Article 317)
*   **Constitutional Mechanism**: Under **Article 317(1)**, the Chairman or any member of the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) or a State Public Service Commission (SPSC) can only be removed from office by the President on the ground of **misbehaviour**, after the Supreme Court, on a reference being made to it by the President, has held an inquiry and reported that the member ought to be removed.
*   **Binding Nature**: The advice and findings reported by the Supreme Court during an inquiry under Article 317(1) are **binding on the President**. The State Governor has only the power to suspend the member during the pendency of the Supreme Court inquiry.

### Special Administrative Tribunal for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana (Article 371D)
*   **Constitutional Authorization**: **Article 371D(3)** empowers the President to establish an Administrative Tribunal for the State of Andhra Pradesh (and subsequently Telangana) to exercise jurisdiction, power, and authority over service-related disputes, complaints, or grievances of persons holding civil posts under the State.
*   **The State Executive Veto Controversy**: Under *Article 371D(5)*, any final order of this tribunal became effective only upon its confirmation by the State Government, or after three months. Furthermore, the State Government was authorized to modify or annul the tribunal's order.
*   **Basic Structure Challenge**: In *Government of A.P. v. Medwin Educational Society*, the Supreme Court struck down the State Government's power to modify or annul the tribunal's final orders under the proviso to Article 371D(5). The Court held that allowing the executive to veto or alter judicial decisions violates the doctrine of **Separation of Powers** and the **Rule of Law**, rendering the tribunal an ineffective and subordinate mechanism of judicial review.

### Lok Adalats, NALSA and Article 39A
*   **Constitutional Obligation**: **Article 39A** (inserted by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976) directs the State to ensure that the operation of the legal system promotes justice on a basis of equal opportunity, and to provide **free legal aid** to ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities.
*   **Statutory Implementation**: Pursuant to Article 39A, Parliament enacted the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**, which came into force on November 9, 1995. It established:
    *   **National Legal Services Authority (NALSA)**: The apex body to formulate policies and principles for making legal services available.
    *   **State and District Legal Services Authorities**: To execute these programs locally.
*   **Lok Adalats (People's Courts)**: Organized under the Act as an alternative dispute resolution (ADR) forum. They possess statutory status. 
    *   **Jurisdiction**: Lok Adalats can settle pending court cases or disputes at the pre-litigation stage.
    *   **Award Bindingness**: The award passed by a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a **decree of a civil court** and is **final and binding** on all parties. No appeal lies against the award of a Lok Adalat before any court of law. If the parties are dissatisfied, they must initiate regular litigation by filing a fresh suit.

## Landmark Doctrines and Principles of Indian Jurisprudence

### The Doctrine of Stare Decisis (Article 141)
*   **Definition**: Literally, "to stand by things decided." It means that judicial precedents are binding on subordinate courts, ensuring consistency, predictability, and stability in the legal system.
*   **Constitutional Mandate**: Article 141 declares that the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within India.
*   **Key Rules of Precedent**:
    1.  The Supreme Court is **not bound by its own decisions** and can overrule them (e.g., *Second Judges Case* overruling *S.P. Gupta*).
    2.  A larger bench decision always prevails over a smaller bench decision.
    3.  High Courts are bound by Supreme Court decisions. Subordinate courts in a State are bound by the decisions of their respective High Court.

### The De Facto Doctrine
*   **Concept**: Designed to prevent chaos and preserve public order. It validates the official acts of a public officer, including a judge, even if their appointment is subsequently declared legally invalid due to some technical or constitutional defect.
*   **Judicial Application**: If the appointment of a district judge or a munsiff is struck down under Article 233 or 234, the judgments, decrees, and sentences delivered by them while in office remain fully valid, legal, and binding on the parties (*State of U.P. v. Rafiquddin*). The officer is treated as a *de facto* judge until properly removed.

### The Basic Structure Doctrine and Judicial Independence
First established in the landmark case of ***Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973)**, this doctrine holds that while Parliament has the power to amend the Constitution under **Article 368**, it cannot alter, destroy, or abridge its "basic features."
*   The Supreme Court has consistently held that the **Independence of the Judiciary**, the **Separation of Powers**, and the **Power of Judicial Review** (under Articles 32, 226, and 227) constitute inviolable parts of the Basic Structure.
*   Any constitutional amendment or statutory provision that seeks to oust judicial review or make the judiciary subordinate to executive control is ultra vires and void (e.g., the striking down of the NJAC and the ouster clauses of Articles 323A and 323B).

### Complete Justice and The Legislative Vacuum
Under Article 142, the Supreme Court acts as a "problem-solver" in areas where there is a **legislative vacuum** (a complete absence of statutory law to address a pressing social issue). The Court can issue guidelines and directions that function as the law of the land until Parliament enacts appropriate legislation (e.g., *Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan* regarding sexual harassment at workplaces, and *Vineet Narain* regarding CBI appointments). This is a unique, creative power that highlights the role of the Indian Supreme Court as a dynamic law-maker in a developing democracy.
