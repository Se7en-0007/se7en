## Conceptual and Definitional Foundations of Labor Economics

In economic theory, **unemployment** is defined as a macroeconomic state in which individuals within the eligible working-age bracket (conventionally **15 years and above** or **15–59 years** in standard Indian databases) are able-bodied, actively willing, available, and seeking productively and gainfully to contribute to economic activity, but are unable to find suitable employment at the prevailing market wage rate. 

The primary distinction is between **involuntary unemployment**—the core subject of public policy, where a worker is willing to work at the prevailing wage rate but cannot secure a job—and **voluntary unemployment**, which occurs when capable individuals choose not to work (e.g., because they are pursuing higher education, caring for households, or holding out for wages above the market equilibrium) and are thus excluded from official labor statistics.

### The Macroeconomic Classification of Population
To accurately quantify labor market conditions, international bodies (like the **ILO**) and national statistics institutions (like India’s **MoSPI**) segment the total population into three mutually exclusive categories:

1. **Employed (or Workforce)**: Persons engaged in any economic activity (production of goods and services), including self-employment, regular wage/salaried work, and casual labor.
2. **Unemployed**: Persons without work who are actively seeking work or are available for work during the specified reference period.
3. **Not in the Labour Force**: Persons who are neither working nor available/seeking work. This category includes students, rentiers, pensioners, homemakers, retired individuals, young children, and those disabled or too ill to work.

$$\text{Total Population} = \text{Labour Force} + \text{Not in Labour Force}$$
$$\text{Labour Force} = \text{Workforce (Employed)} + \text{Unemployed}$$

---

### Core Labor Market Indicators
Standard macroeconomic monitoring relies on several key metrics to capture the size, supply, and underutilization of labor. According to the **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS)** and the **Economic Survey of India**, these indicators are formulated as follows:

*   ### **1. Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR)**
    This measures the percentage of the working-age population that actively supplies labor (either by working or seeking work):
    $$\mathbf{LFPR} = \left( \frac{\text{Employed} + \text{Unemployed}}{\text{Total Population}} \right) \times 100 = \left( \frac{\text{Labour Force}}{\text{Total Population}} \right) \times 100$$
    *Note: In classical macroeconomic contexts, the denominator is restricted to the working-age population (e.g., 15–59 or 15–64 years) to eliminate the dependency ratio of children and the elderly.*

*   ### **2. Worker Population Ratio (WPR) / Work Force Participation Rate (WFPR)**
    This measures the actual proportion of the population that is productively employed:
    $$\mathbf{WPR} = \left( \frac{\text{Employed}}{\text{Total Population}} \right) \times 100 = \left( \frac{\text{Workforce}}{\text{Total Population}} \right) \times 100$$
    *Distinction*: While LFPR includes those seeking work (labor supply), WPR/WFPR only reflects those who have actually secured work (labor demand absorption).

*   ### **3. Unemployment Rate (UR)**
    This represents the percentage of unemployed persons relative to the active labor force, *not* the total population:
    $$\mathbf{UR} = \left( \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Labour Force}} \right) \times 100 = \left( \frac{\text{Labour Force} - \text{Workforce}}{\text{Labour Force}} \right) \times 100$$

*   ### **4. Proportion Unemployed (PU)**
    Unlike the UR, the PU measures the number of unemployed individuals relative to the entire population:
    $$\mathbf{PU} = \left( \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Total Population}} \right) \times 100$$
    *Mathematical Relationship*: The Unemployment Rate can also be expressed as:
    $$\mathbf{UR} = \frac{\mathbf{PU}}{\mathbf{LFPR}} \times 100$$

*   ### **5. Employment Rate (ER)**
    The proportion of the labor force that has succeeded in obtaining work:
    $$\mathbf{ER} = \left( \frac{\text{Employed}}{\text{Labour Force}} \right) \times 100$$

---

### "Person Rate" vs. "Time Rate"
A fundamental analytical debate in labor economics, particularly in developing countries with massive informal sectors, revolves around how unemployment is measured. The NSO/MoSPI categorizes indicators based on the unit of analysis:

1. **Person Rate (UPS, UPSS, CWS)**: This counts the number of *unique individuals* classified as employed or unemployed over the reference period. It is useful for identifying chronic, long-term "open" unemployment but often misses short-term or daily underutilization.
2. **Time Rate (CDS)**: This measures labor status in terms of *person-days* rather than unique persons. For example, if 100 individuals are unemployed for half a week, CDS aggregates this into 350 unemployed person-days. CDS is a highly precise and comprehensive measure because it captures both complete open unemployment and intermittent, daily underemployment.

## Forms of Unemployment

Unemployment manifests in structurally distinct forms, requiring tailored policy interventions.

### Frictional (or Search/Transitional) Unemployment
*   **Definition**: The temporary unemployment that arises when workers are in transition between jobs, relocating geographically, changing careers, or entering the labor force for the first time.
*   **Mechanism**: It is driven by **imperfect information** and structural frictions in the labor market. Job seekers and employers require time to search, interview, and match skills, meaning matching is never instantaneous.
*   **Economic Implications**: Frictional unemployment is short-term, largely voluntary, and is considered a healthy indicator of economic dynamism. It ensures that labor is reallocated to its most productive, high-wage use. **It exists even when an economy is at "full employment" (Natural Rate of Unemployment)**.

### Structural Unemployment
*   **Definition**: A persistent, long-term form of unemployment caused by a fundamental mismatch between the skills and location of job seekers and the requirements of available jobs.
*   **Causes**:
    *   **Supply-Side**: Rapid demographic growth outpacing capital formation, leaving a large portion of the youth labor force structurally excluded from high-growth capital-intensive industries.
    *   **Demand-Side (Technological Shifts)**: Sectoral changes render older skills obsolete. For instance, **Technological Unemployment** arises when capital, automation, robotics, or Artificial Intelligence (AI) replace human physical labor, reducing labor demand in traditional sectors.
    *   **Labour Immobility**: Geographic and occupational barriers prevent workers from shifting costlessly from declining "sunset" industries (e.g., jute, manual typing) to rising "sunrise" industries (e.g., IT, renewable energy).
*   **David Lilien’s Sectoral Shift Hypothesis (1982)**: Demonstrates that aggregate unemployment fluctuations can be driven by the slow reallocation of labor across sectors rather than simple changes in aggregate demand. In India, this mismatch is visible in the huge oversupply of general graduates alongside acute shortages of certified technical professionals.

### Cyclical (or Demand-Deficient) Unemployment
*   **Definition**: Unemployment that directly fluctuates with the phases of the **business cycle**.
*   **Mechanism**: 

$$\text{Economic Contraction/Recession} \implies \text{Fall in Aggregate Demand} \implies \text{Production Cuts} \implies \text{Layoffs \& Job Losses}$$

*   **Key Distinctions**: Cyclical unemployment is short-term and pro-cyclical. While it dominates developed Western economies (e.g., the aftermath of the 2008 Global Financial Crisis), developing countries like India suffer primarily from chronic, structural, and institutional defects rather than simple business cycle downturns.

### Disguised (or Hidden) Unemployment
*   **Definition**: A condition where more workers are engaged in an activity than are technically or economically required. On the surface, these individuals appear employed, but their removal would have zero impact on total output.
*   **Mathematical Concept**: The **Marginal Productivity of Labour (MPL)** of the disguised worker is zero, or in some instances, negative:
    $$\mathbf{MPL} = \frac{\Delta \text{Total Output}}{\Delta \text{Labour Input}} \approx 0$$
*   **Nurksian Capital Accumulation Potential**: Pioneer development economist **Ragnar Nurkse** argued that disguised unemployment in agrarian economies contains a **"concealed saving potential"**. If these surplus workers are systematically mobilized and transferred to productive public works (like rural roads, dams, and irrigation canals), the state can generate massive social overhead capital without reducing agricultural output.
*   **Lewis Dual-Sector Model Roadblocks**: Arthur Lewis's model relies on the smooth, costless transfer of rural surplus labor with zero marginal productivity to the urban industrial sector at a constant institutional wage. In India, this transition has stalled due to structural skill deficits, rigid urban labor regulations, and low labor absorption in formal manufacturing.

### Underemployment
*   **Definition**: The underutilization of an individual's full working capacity. It occurs in two ways:
    *   **Visible/Time-based**: Working fewer hours than one is willing and available to work (e.g., part-time workers seeking full-time roles).
    *   **Invisible/Skill-based**: Working in low-productivity, low-paying jobs that require far lower educational and technical qualifications than those possessed.
*   **Arvind Panagariya’s Underemployment Thesis**: Former NITI Aayog Vice-Chairman **Arvind Panagariya** argued that **"unemployment is not India’s primary problem; underemployment is."** In a developing economy lacking a comprehensive social safety net, absolute poverty prevents the poor from remaining openly unemployed. Instead, they are forced to engage in low-productivity, survival-driven informal activities (like street vending or marginal farming), appearing "employed" in standard surveys while remaining highly underemployed.

### Additional Forms of Unemployment
*   **Seasonal Unemployment**: Occurs during predictable, off-peak times of the year in industries governed by natural cycles (e.g., agriculture during lean periods between harvesting and sowing, sugar processing factories, or seasonal tourism). 
*   **Casual Unemployment**: Short-term unemployment affecting daily wage or contract laborers. It is triggered by the sudden termination of short-term contracts, raw material shortages, power deficits, or shifts in enterprise ownership.
*   **Vulnerable Employment**: A dominant category in India, defining workers in the unorganized sector who work without written employment contracts, regular wages, legal protection, or social security benefits.
*   **Chronic Unemployment**: Long-term, permanent unemployment resulting from deep-seated economic underdevelopment, an inadequate rate of capital formation, and a high population growth rate.

### Theoretical Macroeconomic Models

*   **The Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU) and NAIRU**
    Developed by **Milton Friedman** and **Edmund Phelps** in the late 1960s, the **Natural Rate of Unemployment (NRU)** is the equilibrium rate of unemployment that persists in the long run, even when the economy is producing at its potential level.
    $$\mathbf{NRU} = \text{Frictional Unemployment} + \text{Structural Unemployment}$$
    The **Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU)** is the specific level of unemployment at which the rate of inflation is stable. If policymakers use expansionary monetary or fiscal policies to force unemployment below this rate, it generates wage-price spiral pressures, causing inflation to accelerate.
*   **Unemployment Hysteresis**
    Formulated by **Olivier Blanchard** and **Lawrence Summers** (1986), **hysteresis** is a phenomenon where a temporary negative economic shock that increases cyclical unemployment has a permanent, scarring effect on the natural rate of unemployment. This occurs because:
    1.  **Human Capital Depreciation**: Prolonged joblessness erodes workers' professional skills.
    2.  **Employer Signaling**: Firms perceive long-term unemployment as a negative indicator of productivity, reducing hireability.
    3.  **Insider-Outsider Dynamics**: Unionized workers (insiders) negotiate high wages for themselves, keeping unemployed outsiders structurally excluded.
*   **The Unemployment Trap**
    A microeconomic state where government-provided unemployment benefits or social security transfers create a disincentive for unemployed individuals to seek work. If the net post-tax wage offered by a job is only marginally higher than the welfare benefits received while remaining idle, the **opportunity cost** of entering the workforce is too high, locking individuals into voluntary unemployment.

## Measurement Methodologies of Unemployment in India

Historically, India’s employment and unemployment data was collected through the **Quinquennial Employment-Unemployment Surveys (EUS)** conducted by the National Sample Survey Organisation (NSSO) from the 27th round (1972–73) to the 68th round (2011–12). To modernize and increase the frequency of labor market data, the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) replaced the quinquennial surveys in **2017** with the annual and quarterly **Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS)**.

### The Three Standard Activity Status Approaches
The PLFS classifies an individual's economic activity status using three different reference periods: one year (365 days), one week (7 days), and each day of the reference week.

| Measurement Approach | Reference Period | Core Unit / Unit of Measurement | Analytical & Policy Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Usual Status (UPS / UPSS)** | **365 days** preceding the survey | **Person Rate** (Categorization of persons) | Measures chronic, long-term, and open structural unemployment. |
| **Current Weekly Status (CWS)** | **7 days** preceding the survey | **Person Rate** (Categorization of persons) | Captures weekly activity dynamics and urban labor market fluctuations. |
| **Current Daily Status (CDS)** | **Each day** of the preceding 7 days | **Time Rate** (Aggregated person-days) | Most comprehensive measure; captures underemployment and daily work availability. |

---

### Usual Activity Status (US)
The usual activity status of an individual is determined based on the activities they pursued over a long-term reference period of **365 days** preceding the date of the survey. This is measured through two distinct, nested concepts:

*   **1. Usual Principal Status (UPS)**: 
    *   This is determined using the **major time criterion**.
    *   If an individual spent the major part of the year (**183 days or more**) engaged in economic work or seeking work, they are classified as being in the labor force.
    *   If they spent the majority of their time seeking work but unable to find it, they are classified as **UPS Unemployed**. This is also known as **open chronic unemployment**.
*   **2. Usual Subsidiary Status (SS)**:
    *   This captures marginal and seasonal workers who are primarily out of the labor force (e.g., homemakers or students) or unemployed under the UPS classification, but who pursued some subsidiary economic activity for **30 days or more** during the year.
*   **3. Usual Principal and Subsidiary Status (UPSS - Adjusted usual status)**:
    *   This is a hybrid, more inclusive approach that counts an individual as employed if they are classified as working under *either* the UPS or the SS framework.
    *   **Policy Effect**: Because UPSS classifies marginal, short-term, and subsidiary workers as employed, the estimated rate of chronic unemployment under UPSS is consistently lower than under UPS alone.

---

### Current Weekly Status (CWS)
*   **Reference Period**: The **7 days** preceding the survey date.
*   **Employment Threshold**: Under the **priority criterion**, an individual is classified as employed if they worked for **at least one hour** on **at least one day** during the reference week.
*   **Unemployment**: If they failed to get even one hour of work during the week but were actively seeking/available for work, they are classified as CWS Unemployed.
*   **Recent Update (2025)**: Starting in **April 2025**, MoSPI revamped the PLFS by introducing a monthly bulletin based on CWS, providing high-frequency indicators for both rural and urban areas.

---

### Current Daily Status (CDS)
The CDS approach tracks the detailed **daily time disposition** of an individual for each of the 7 days preceding the survey. It is considered the most accurate measure of the actual utilization of labor in agricultural and informal economies.

*   **Strict Allocation Rules (Priority-cum-Major Time Criterion)**:
    *   **Full-Day Employment**: If an individual works for **4 hours or more** on any given day, they are classified as employed for the entire day (1.0 person-day).
    *   **Half-Day Employment**: If they work for **1 hour or more but less than 4 hours**, they are classified as employed for half a day (0.5 person-day). The remaining half-day is classified as unemployed (if they were actively seeking work) or out of the labor force.
    *   **Full-Day Unemployment**: If they did not work even for 1 hour but were seeking/available for work for **4 hours or more**, they are unemployed for the full day (1.0 person-day).
    *   **Half-Day Unemployment**: If they did not work and were seeking/available for work for **1 to 4 hours**, they are unemployed for half a day (0.5 person-day).
    *   **Out of Labour Force**: If they neither worked nor were available for work, they are classified as out of the labor force for that day.

*   **Comparative Level**: Historically, **CDS Unemployment Rate > CWS Unemployment Rate > UPSS Unemployment Rate**. The gap represents the magnitude of **underemployment** and intermittent seasonal unemployment.

### Comparison with The Decennial Census of India
The Decennial Census of India classifies workers based on a **180-day threshold** during the reference year:
*   **Main Workers**: Persons who worked for **183 days or more** during the reference year.
*   **Marginal Workers**: Persons who worked for **less than 183 days** during the year.

Because the Census does not probe daily time disposition, its "Main Worker" category is directly comparable to the **UPS** (Usual Principal Status) estimates of the NSO/MoSPI.

## Structural Profile, Trends and Paradoxes in Indian Labor Markets

### General Macroeconomic Labor Trends (2017–18 to 2025–26)
According to the **Annual PLFS reports** and the **Economic Surveys (2024–25 and 2025–26)**, the Indian labor market has experienced a significant structural transition:

*   **Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) UPSS (Aged 15+)**: Rose steadily from **49.8%** in 2017–18 to a peak of **60.1%** in 2023–24. In H1 FY26, under the Current Weekly Status (CWS) lens, the monthly LFPR stabilized in the **55.0%–56.1%** range.
*   **Worker Population Ratio (WPR) UPSS (Aged 15+)**: Improved from **46.8%** in 2017–18 to **58.2%** in 2023–24.
*   **Unemployment Rate (UR) UPSS (Aged 15+)**: Declined from a 45-year high of **6.0%** in 2017–18 to **3.2%** in 2022–23 and 2023–24. Under CWS, the UR declined from **8.7%** in 2017–18 to **4.9%** in 2023–24, and fluctuated between **4.7% and 5.6%** during H1 FY26.

---

### Sectoral Mismatches: "The Agriculture Trap" and Services Sector Anomaly
In standard economic development theory, labor transitions from low-productivity agriculture to high-productivity manufacturing and services. India exhibits a unique, structurally distorted transition:

*   **The Agriculture Trap**: While agriculture's contribution to India's GDP/GVA has declined to **15%–18%**, it still employs nearly **43%–45%** of the total workforce. This disproportionate concentration reflects massive **disguised unemployment** and low agricultural productivity.
*   **The Services Sector Anomaly**: The services sector contributes **54%** to India's GDP but absorbs less than **30%** of the workforce. Growth has been led by high-skill, capital-intensive sub-sectors (such as IT, software development, fintech, and advanced financial services) which cannot absorb low-skilled surplus labor from rural areas.
*   **The Construction Buffer**: Non-agricultural employment growth has been heavily concentrated in the **construction sector**, which has acted as a low-skill buffer absorbing workers leaving agriculture due to shrinking farm sizes and rural distress.

---

### The Quality of Employment Challenge: Informalization and Self-Employment
While headline unemployment rates have fallen, the structural quality of employment remains a challenge:

*   **Overwhelming Informalization**: Over **90%** of the total workforce in India is engaged in informal employment (lacking written contracts, paid leave, regular wages, or social protection). Even within the formal sector, a significant portion of employment is informal/contractual.
*   **The Self-Employment Surge**: The proportion of self-employed workers in the total workforce rose from **52.2%** in 2017–18 to **58.4%** in 2023–24.
*   **Unpaid Family Helpers**: This surge is driven primarily by **"unpaid helpers in household enterprises"** (contributing family workers who do not receive direct wages), rising to **19.4%** of the total workforce.
*   **Stagnant Regular Salaried Employment**: Regular wage/salaried employment has stagnated, dropping from **22.8%** in 2017–18 to **21.7%** in 2023–24.

---

### The Youth Unemployment and Employability Paradox
India is currently experiencing a historic demographic window, with youth (ages 15–29) making up a large portion of the population. However, youth employment exhibits a structural paradox:

*   **Youth Unemployment Rate (YUR) > Overall UR**: The overall UR is 3.2%, but the YUR is around **10%**. Historically, youth unemployment reached peak levels of **16.6%** for rural males and **25.7%** for urban females in 2018–19.
*   **The Education Paradox**: There is a strong **positive correlation** between education levels and unemployment rates in India. The higher the level of education attained, the higher the likelihood of being unemployed.
*   **Key Data (ILO India Employment Report 2024)**:
    *   Unemployment among **Graduates**: **29.1%**
    *   Unemployment among those with **Secondary or Higher Education**: **18.4%**
    *   Unemployment among **Illiterate** individuals: **3.4%**
*   **The Skilling Gap**: Despite government efforts, only **4.7%** of India’s workforce has formally certified skills, compared to over 90% in East Asian countries.
*   **The NEET (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) Challenge**: Approximately **38.1% of young females** fell into the NEET category in 2023, compared to **10.2% of young males**, reflecting societal norms and barriers to education and work.

---

### Gender Dimensions: Female Labour Force Participation (FLFPR)
India's FLFPR has historically been low and exhibits complex dynamics:

*   **Historical Decline**: Combined FLFPR declined from **42.7%** in 2004–05 to **31.2%** in 2011–12, and hit a low of **17.5%** in 2017–18.
*   **The U-Shaped Relationship**: Research shows FLFPR follows a U-shaped curve relative to household income and economic development:
    1.  At very low income levels, rural women work out of absolute economic necessity (survival employment).
    2.  As household incomes rise (**income effect**), women withdraw from manual labor to achieve higher social status.
    3.  At higher levels of education and income, women re-enter the labor market in high-productivity white-collar roles.

![[Pasted image 20260826105935.png|700]]

*   **The Post-2017 FLFPR Reversal**: Combined FLFPR rose from **23.3%** in 2017–18 to **41.7%** in 2023–24.
*   **Two Competing Academic Perspectives**:
    1.  **Development-Led Perspective**: Pushed by the Economic Survey, this argues that the rise is driven by rising female education and the **freeing up of women’s time** due to structural expansions in basic rural amenities (e.g., piped drinking water under Jal Jeevan, clean cooking gas under PM Ujjwala Yojana, and sanitation).
    2.  **Distress-Led Perspective**: Critics argue the increase is a household coping mechanism where rural women enter household enterprises as **unpaid family helpers** to support family incomes during periods of slow agricultural growth and stagnant real wages.

## The "Jobless Growth" Syndrome

**Jobless growth** is an economic phenomenon where an economy experiences robust GDP growth while maintaining or lowering its overall employment levels.

### The Employment Elasticity Collapse
Employment elasticity measures the responsiveness of employment growth to GDP growth:
$$\text{Employment Elasticity} = \frac{\% \ \Delta \ \text{in Employment}}{\% \ \Delta \ \text{in GDP}}$$
In India, employment elasticity has declined significantly:
*   In agriculture, the elasticity fell to **0.64%** and further declined to **0.60%**.
*   In manufacturing, employment elasticity remains extremely low. Consequently, even when output expands, industrial hiring remains flat.

### Rigidity of Labour Laws and MSME "Dwarfism"
*   **Regulatory Rigidity**: Historically, complex labor laws (such as the **Industrial Disputes Act 1947**) mandated that firms with more than 100 employees obtain prior government permission before laying off workers or closing operations. Since permission was rarely granted, formal firms avoided hiring permanent labor, opting instead for capital-intensive technologies or informal contract hiring.
*   **MSME Dwarfism**: Micro, Small, and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) are labor-intensive, yet they face credit constraints and compliance burdens. To avoid falling into the regulatory net, firms intentionally remain small (**"dwarfs"**—firms that are small and remain small even as they age), which limits their capacity to generate formal jobs.

### Skipping The Manufacturing Transition
Unlike East Asian economies that achieved rapid growth through high-volume, low-skill, export-led assembly (e.g., garments, footwear, electronics assembly), India transitioned directly from agriculture to high-skill services. This left a structural employment gap for low-skilled rural workers.

## Policy Architecture, Reforms and Government Initiatives

Over successive economic plans, India's policy response has evolved from relying on a post-independence **trickle-down effect** to implementing targeted wage employment and skilling programs.

### Major Wage Employment Schemes
*   **1. Viksit Bharat–Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) (VB–G RAM G Act, 2025)**
    *   **Legal Backing**: It is a **demand-driven, rights-based** rural employment framework established through an **Act of Parliament**, replacing the Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act (MGNREGA), 2005, with effect from **1 July 2026**.
    *   **Core Guarantee**: Guarantees at least **125 days of unskilled manual work** per financial year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to undertake such work - an increase from the **100 days** guaranteed under MGNREGA
    *   **Key Statutory Features**:
        *   **Women’s Participation**: At least **33% (one-third)** participation is mandated for women. Under the new framework, women’s participation has remained substantially above this threshold; women accounted for around **61.5% of person-days** generated under VB–G RAM G since its launch, according to latest reported government data.
        *   **Wage Rates**: Wage rates are notified separately for States/UTs under the new framework, with the revised rates applicable from **1 July 2026**. No notified wage rate is below **₹300 per day**.
        *   **Compensation Clause**: If employment is not provided within the prescribed period after a valid demand for work, the applicant is legally entitled to an **unemployment allowance payable by the State Government**. The right to unemployment allowance has been retained under VB–G RAM G.
        *   **Funding Ratio**: The programme operates as a **Centrally Sponsored Scheme**, with a **60:40 Centre–State funding ratio** for most States and **90:10** for North-Eastern and Himalayan States and specified Union Territories. The new framework also introduces **normative State-wise allocations**, with expenditure beyond the allocated amount to be borne by the concerned State
        * **Agricultural Season Provision**: States may notify up to **60 days in a financial year** during peak sowing and harvesting seasons when works under the programme may be temporarily suspended, with the objective of ensuring adequate availability of agricultural labour during these periods. 
        * **Social Audits**: **Social audits and transparency mechanisms** continue to form an important part of the rural employment framework, alongside strengthened digital monitoring and technology-enabled implementation.
        * **Asset Creation & Evolution**: The programme places greater emphasis on **durable, productive and climate-resilient rural assets**, with priority areas including **water security, core rural infrastructure, livelihood-related infrastructure, and mitigation of extreme weather events**. It seeks to integrate wage employment with rural infrastructure creation, livelihood enhancement and village-level development planning.
    *   **Achievements**: In **July 2026**, the first month of VB–G RAM G implementation, approximately **68.9 lakh rural households** availed employment, while reported person-days were substantially lower than under MGNREGA in the corresponding period of previous years. Available reports indicate that July 2026 employment generation was among the **lowest recorded in the last five years**, highlighting concerns regarding the actual utilisation of the expanded 125-day guarantee.
    *  **Criticisms**: Concerns include the shift from MGNREGA's earlier funding architecture to **normative allocations and greater State financial responsibility**, the possibility of reduced employment availability despite the higher statutory guarantee, increased dependence on digital systems, and the decline in rural employment generated during the initial month of implementation.

*   **2. State-Level Urban Employment Programmes (UEPs)**:
    Recognizing that MGNREGA is restricted to rural areas, nine states have launched urban employment guarantee schemes. These programs have shown high female participation rates, providing safe, localized, and flexible work.

---

### Self-Employment and Entrepreneurship Promotion
*   **DAY-NRLM (Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana - National Rural Livelihoods Mission / Aajeevika - 2011)**: Organizes rural poor women into Self-Help Groups (SHGs) and provides credit linkages, seed funding, and training to build self-employment livelihoods.
*   **DAY-NULM (National Urban Livelihoods Mission)**: Focuses on organizing the urban poor into SHGs, providing access to market-oriented skill training, and facilitating easy credit access to set up micro-enterprises.
*   **Prime Minister's Employment Generation Programme (PMEGP - 2008)**: A credit-linked subsidy program that helps individuals set up manufacturing and services micro-enterprises in non-farm sectors.
*   **Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana**: Provides collateral-free micro-loans up to ₹10 Lakhs across three categories (*Shishu, Kishor, and Tarun*) to micro-enterprises, supporting self-employment.
*   **PM-SVANidhi (Street Vendor's Atma Nirbhar Nidhi)**: A central sector micro-credit scheme providing working capital loans up to ₹50,000 with a 7% interest subvention to street vendors.

---

### Skilling, Employability and Targeted Initiatives
*   **PM Kaushal Vikas Yojana (PMKVY)**: The flagship skill certification scheme launched in 2015.
    *   **PMKVY 3.0 & 4.0**: PMKVY 4.0 focuses on **NSQF-aligned job roles** developed by industry-led Sector Skill Councils (SSCs) with courses delivered directly on industrial premises.
    *   **Recognition of Prior Learning (RPL)**: Assesses and certifies the skills of the unregulated, uncertified workforce.
    *   **Sectoral Focus**: Directs skilling investments toward emerging areas (digital technologies, green energy, advanced agriculture, and e-commerce).
*   **Deen Dayal Upadhyaya Grameen Kaushalya Yojana (DDU-GKY)**: A placement-linked skilling initiative for rural youth.
*   **PM Vishwakarma Scheme (2023)**: Provides basic (5–7 days) and advanced training, modern toolkits, and collateral-free concessional loans at 5% interest (with an 8% government subvention) to traditional artisans and craftsmen.

---

### Structural Labour Market Reforms: The Four Labour Codes
To simplify India's complex labor regulatory framework, the government consolidated **47 central and over 100 state labor laws** into **Four Comprehensive Labour Codes**:

1.  **The Code on Wages, 2019**: Rationalizes minimum wage systems across states, introduces a national floor wage, and ensures gender equality in wages.
2.  **The Industrial Relations Code, 2020**: Increases operational flexibility for enterprises by raising the employee threshold for mandatory government approval for layoffs, retrenchments, and closures from **100 to 300 employees**.
3.  **The Code on Social Security, 2020**: Mandates the extension of social protection and statutory benefits to unorganized, casual, gig, and platform workers.
4.  **The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020**: Standardizes safety, welfare, and health norms across industrial establishments.

### Digital Platforms and Database Integration
*   **e-Shram Portal (2021)**: Serves as a National Database of Unorganised Workers (including construction, migrant, gig, and agricultural workers). As of January 2026, it has registered over **31 crore unorganized workers**, with women making up **54% of registrants**. Registrants receive a **Universal Account Number (UAN)** linked to Aadhaar, ensuring the portability of benefits across states.
*   **National Career Service (NCS) Portal (2015)**: A job-matching platform integrated with the Skill India Digital Hub (SIDH), EPFO, ESIC, and e-Shram. It partners with private entities to offer free online career and digital skills training.
*   **Shram Suvidha Portal**: Provides a single-window interface for labor law compliance, inspections, and reporting, reducing administrative complexity.

## Comparison of Key Unemployment Concepts

To synthesize economic theory, the following table compares key types of unemployment:

| Analytical Dimension            | Frictional Unemployment                                    | Structural Unemployment                                      | Disguised Unemployment                                            | Underemployment                                                  |
| :------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Primary Cause**               | Imperfect information, transaction lags, and search times. | Skill mismatch, technological change, and sectoral shifts.   | Overcrowding in subsistence sectors due to lack of non-farm jobs. | Excess labor supply forcing acceptance of low-productivity work. |
| **Duration & Nature**           | Short-term, transitional, and largely voluntary.           | Long-term, persistent, and involuntary.                      | Long-term, chronic, and structural.                               | Ongoing, hidden, or structural.                                  |
| **Marginal Productivity (MPL)** | Positive (reallocates labor to higher-value jobs).         | Low or declining (skills are obsolete).                      | **Zero or Negative** $$MPL \approx 0$$                            | Positive, but significantly below full capacity.                 |
| **Typical Sector**              | Modern, dynamic urban sectors.                             | Declining industrial or evolving service sectors.            | **Rural subsistence agriculture** and family businesses.          | Urban informal services and unorganized trades.                  |
| **Policy Interventions**        | Digital search portals (e.g., NCS), job-matching engines.  | Skilling programs (PMKVY 4.0), educational curricula reform. | Structural shift to labor-intensive manufacturing (Lewis model).  | PMEGP, MUDRA loans, and promoting high-productivity formal jobs. |
