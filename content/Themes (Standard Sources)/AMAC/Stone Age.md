## INTRODUCTION

### Prehistory
The study of **Prehistory** refers to the vast span of human existence prior to the availability of written records. Because written texts do not exist for this period, our understanding of prehistoric societies is derived entirely from material remains, including stone implements, organic debris, animal bones, plant fossils, and early rock art. 

The birth of prehistory as a formal scientific discipline occurred in **1859**, when primitive stone implements found alongside the fossilized bones of extinct mammals in northern Europe were ratified before the Royal Society of London. In **1865**, the British polymath **Sir John Lubbock** coined the term "Prehistory" and formally divided the **Stone Age** into two distinct periods in his book *Prehistoric Times*: the **Palaeolithic** (Old Stone Age) and the **Neolithic** (New Stone Age). 

This classification was built upon the earlier **Three-Age System** proposed by the Danish archaeologist **Christian Jürgensen Thomsen** in the 1830s, which divided human technological progress into the **Stone Age**, **Bronze Age**, and **Iron Age** based on the primary materials utilized for manufacturing tools and weapons. By the late 19th century, archaeologists recognized that a transitional phase bridged the Old and New Stone Ages, leading to the introduction of the **Mesolithic** (Middle Stone Age).

### The Quaternary Period: Pleistocene and Holocene Epochs
The geological history of the Earth is divided into various eras, epochs, and periods. Hominin evolution and cultural development occurred during the **Cenozoic Era** (the Age of Mammals), specifically within the **Quaternary Period**, which is further divided into two key geological epochs:

1. **The Pleistocene Epoch (The Great Ice Age)**: Spanning from approximately 2 million years ago to 10,000 BCE, this epoch was characterized by global glaciations and extremely cold, dry climates. Large sheets of ice covered vast portions of the northern hemisphere. However, in tropical and subtropical regions such as the Indian subcontinent, glaciers melted into immense river systems, creating narrow hospitable zones where early hominins could survive.
2. **The Holocene Epoch (The Recent or Neo-thermal Phase)**: Beginning around 10,000 BCE (approx. 12,000 years ago) and continuing to the present day, this epoch marked the end of the last ice age. The climate became progressively warmer, more humid, and rainy. These massive environmental changes caused glaciers to retreat, altered the courses of major rivers, and brought about significant transformations in flora and fauna. Humans took advantage of the dense vegetation, extensive forests, and multiplying herds of smaller, faster grazing animals to spread into new ecological niches.

### Dating Methodologies and Stratigraphic Principles
To reconstruct the lifeways of prehistoric humans, archaeologists utilize a combination of geological and scientific methods:
* **Stratigraphy**: The study of soil and rock layers (strata) deposited over time. According to the law of superimposition, deeper layers are older, while layers closer to the surface are younger. Artifacts found within these stratified contexts can be dated relatively.
* **Typology**: Classifying stone tools based on their form, material, and manufacturing technique (e.g., handaxes, flakes, microliths). Tool refinement and size reduction generally correspond to technological advancement over time.
* **Scientific (Absolute) Dating**: 
  * **Radiocarbon (C-14) Dating**: Measures the decay of radioactive carbon in organic materials (bones, charcoal, wood) up to about 50,000 years old.
  * **Thermoluminescence (TL) and Optically Stimulated Luminescence (OSL)**: Used to date sediments, pottery, and burnt stone by measuring the trapped electrons released when exposed to heat (TL) or light (OSL).
  * **Electron Spin Resonance (ESR)**: Particularly useful for dating tooth enamel of animal fossils found in archaeological deposits.
  * **Potassium-Argon (K-Ar) Dating**: Measures the decay of potassium into argon in volcanic rocks, crucial for dating early hominin layers that are millions of years old.

## THE PALAEOLITHIC AGE (OLD STONE AGE)

### Chronology, Geological Context and Human Evolution
The **Palaeolithic** period derives its name from the Greek words *palaeo* (old) and *lithos* (stone). This stage represents the longest phase of human history, covering approximately 99% of the human past. In the Indian subcontinent, the Palaeolithic culture developed entirely within the **Pleistocene Epoch**.

On the basis of progressive developments in stone tool technology, raw material choices, and stratigraphic analysis, the Palaeolithic is divided into three distinct phases:
1. **Lower Palaeolithic**: ~2,000,000 BP to 100,000/60,000 BP.
2. **Middle Palaeolithic**: ~100,000 BP to 40,000 BP.
3. **Upper Palaeolithic**: ~40,000 BP to 10,000 BP.

### Hominin Remains in India: The Narmada Man & Hathnora Fossils
Unlike the African continent, where early hominin fossils are abundant, fossilized skeletal remains of human ancestors are exceptionally rare in India. 

* **The Hathnora Discovery**: In **1982**, the geologist **Arun Sonakia** made a breakthrough discovery in a boulder conglomerate deposit on the banks of the Narmada River at **Hathnora**, near Hoshangabad in Madhya Pradesh. He discovered a partial hominin skull cap (cranium), which was named the **Narmada Man** (or **Narmada Human**). 
* **Anthropological Classification**: The Hathnora skull cap is considered by anthropologists to represent **Archaic Homo Sapiens**, an intermediate evolutionary stage between **Homo erectus** and anatomically modern **Homo sapiens**. It is the oldest and only well-preserved hominin fossil in the Indian subcontinent.
* **Subsequent Discoveries**: Intensified excavations by the Anthropological Survey of India led by **A. R. Sankhyan** (2016) in the central Narmada valley (specifically at Hathnora and **Netankheri**) recovered a hominid collarbone (clavicle), along with fragments of a femur and a humerus. These fossil remains, associated with late Lower or Middle Palaeolithic tools, are dated between **300,000 and 75,000 years ago**, suggesting that at least two distinct hominin species coexisted in the Narmada valley.
* **Other Finds**: Another hominin fossil fragment was reported by **Robert Bruce Foote** from **Athirampakkam** in Tamil Nadu, but its exact whereabouts are currently unknown.

### Phase I: The Lower Palaeolithic Culture
The Lower Palaeolithic phase represents the earliest stage of human tool-making in India. The technology of this era relied on brute physical force and the manufacture of large, heavy implements.

#### Climatic Conditions & Raw Materials
Developing during the early-to-mid Pleistocene, the climate was extremely cold and dry. Vegetation was confined, and early humans were species of **Homo habilis** and **Homo erectus**. 
Early humans preferred **quartzite** (a hard, brittle metamorphic rock) for fashioning stone tools. Because of this, Palaeolithic humans are often referred to as **"Quartzite Men"** in early Indian archaeological literature. Where quartzite was not naturally available, hominins adapted by using other locally available stones:
* **Limestone** in the Bhima Basin (Karnataka).
* **Dolerite** and **Basalt** in the Deccan plateau (Maharashtra).
* **Petrified/Fossil Wood** in eastern India and Bengal (e.g., the Lalmai-Mainamati hills in Bangladesh and Chaklapunji in Tripura, showing technical affinities with the Irrawaddy valley of Myanmar).

#### Tool Technologies and Traditions
The Lower Palaeolithic in India is characterized by two distinct, parallel stone-tool traditions:

##### 1. The Soanian Pebble-Tool Tradition
* **Geographical Distribution**: Located in the northwestern part of the subcontinent, specifically along the **Soan River valley** (a tributary of the Indus) on the **Potwar Plateau** (modern-day Pakistan) and extending into the Sirsa valley of Haryana.
* **Technology**: Relied on heavy river pebbles.
* **Primary Tools**:
  * **Choppers**: Large flat pebbles flaked on only one side of the edge to create a cutting face (unifacial).
  * **Chopping Tools**: Heavy pebbles flaked on both sides of the edge to create a jagged, zig-zag cutting line (bifacial).

##### 2. The Peninsular Acheulian Tradition (Acheulian Culture)
* **Geographical Distribution**: Spread across peninsular India, central India, and southern Uttar Pradesh.
* **Technology**: Characterized by standardized, symmetrical, and increasingly refined bifacial tools. It is named after the French site of Saint-Acheul.
* **Primary Tools**:
  * **Handaxes**: Pear-shaped, almond-shaped, or oval core tools with pointed ends, bifacially flaked for digging, cutting, and piercing.
  * **Cleavers**: Heavy flake-tools with a broad, flat, and straight cutting edge, similar to a modern axe-head, used for skinning animals and splitting timber.
  * **Pebble Tools and Knives**: Used for general cutting and brute-force crushing.
* **Technique**: Used **stone hammer**, **soft hammer (wood/bone/antler)**, and **prepared core** techniques to flake and refine tools.

#### Key Lower Palaeolithic Sites
* **Pallavaram (Tamil Nadu)**: In **1863**, the British geologist **Robert Bruce Foote** discovered India's first Palaeolithic handaxe here, establishing the birth of Indian prehistory.
* **Attirampakkam (Tamil Nadu)**: Situated in the Kortallaiyar river basin, this is one of the richest Acheulian sites in India. Excavated by **Shanti Pappu** and **Kumar Akhilesh**, this site yielded quartzite tools, bovid teeth, and fossilised animal footprints (including horse and cattle). Luminescence dating places the Lower Palaeolithic levels here as old as **1.5 million years BP**, pointing to an early dispersal of hominins out of Africa.
* **Isampur (Karnataka)**: Located in the Hunsgi-Baichbal valley, this is the largest Lower Palaeolithic **stone tool factory site** discovered in India. Excavations by **K. Paddayya** revealed a dense concentration of limestone, chert, and quartzite tools. Electronic Spin Resonance (ESR) dating of bovid teeth enamel at Isampur yielded a date of **1.2 million years BP**, confirming its status as one of the oldest tool manufacturing hubs.
* **Didwana (Rajasthan)**: Located in the Hiran Valley, it has provided a continuous stratigraphic sequence showing the evolution of Lower Palaeolithic tools into Middle Palaeolithic forms.
* **Bhimbetka (Madhya Pradesh)**: Located in the Vindhyan range, these rock shelters were occupied as early as the Lower Palaeolithic, providing natural shelter from the elements.

### Phase II: The Middle Palaeolithic Culture
The Middle Palaeolithic represents a massive technological shift. The heavy core-tools of the Lower Palaeolithic were gradually replaced by smaller, sharper, and highly versatile flake-tools.

#### First Identification: The Nevasan Industry
In **1960**, the preeminent archaeologist **H.D. Sankalia** first identified and established the Indian Middle Palaeolithic culture along the Pravara River at **Nevasa** in Maharashtra. He named this cultural phase the **Nevasan Industry** based on the typical flake-tools found here.

#### Technological Innovation: The Flake Tool Industry
Hominins shifted from using the "core" of the stone to using the **flakes** detached from prepared cores. The tools were smaller, thinner, and required less physical mass but greater manual dexterity and cognitive planning to manufacture. Hominins also began utilizing fine-grained, siliceous rocks such as **chert**, **jasper**, and **agate** instead of quartzite.

#### The Levallois Technique
A defining marker of Middle Palaeolithic behavioral modernity was the use of the **Levallois Technique** (named after the Levallois-Perret suburb of Paris):
* **Prepared Core**: Hominins did not simply strike a stone to get a flake. First, they carefully prepared the rock (the core) by trimming its outer edges and systemically removing small flakes from the outer surface toward the center. This prepared core often resembled an inverted tortoise shell (known as a **tortoise core**).
* **Striking Platform**: Hominins then flattened the top of the core to create a striking platform.
* **Precise Strike**: By delivering a sharp, perpendicular blow to the striking platform (using a bone or stone punch), a single, large, highly standardized flake of pre-planned shape and thickness was detached, requiring minimal post-detachment trimming.

#### Middle Palaeolithic Tool Typology
* **Scrapers**: The most common tool type. Used for scraping animal skins to remove fat and flesh, and for scraping wood. Shapes included single-side, double-side, side-cum-end, straight, concave, convex, and core-scrapers.
* **Borers/Awls**: Tools with pointed tips used to drill holes in wood, bone, or animal skins to stitch clothing.
* **Points**: Unilateral or bilateral pointed flakes used as projectile tips, tanged points, or shouldered points, showing the early evolution of projectile weapons.

#### Key Middle Palaeolithic Sites
* **Nevasa (Maharashtra)**: The type-site of the Nevasan industry, located in the Godavari-Pravara basin.
* **Attirampakkam (Tamil Nadu)**: Luminescence dates indicate that the transition from late Acheulian to Middle Palaeolithic began here as early as **385,000 years BP**, and continued till **172,000 years BP**. This indicates that the Middle Palaeolithic emerged in India at a time comparable to its development in Africa and Europe.
* **Nandipalli (Kadapa District, Andhra Pradesh)**: Carbon-14 dating places the Middle Palaeolithic levels here as older than **23,000 years BP**.
* **Sanghao Cave (Peshawar, Pakistan)**: A famous cave site showing extensive Middle Palaeolithic hearths, ash, and flake tool assemblages.
* **Visakhapatnam Coast (Andhra Pradesh)**: Yielded tools made of quartz, chert, and quartzite using the Levallois technique.

### Phase III: The Upper Palaeolithic Culture
The Upper Palaeolithic represents the final subdivision of the Palaeolithic period, characterized by a rapid climax of human technological and cognitive achievements. It coincided with the last phase of the Pleistocene Ice Age, as the climate became increasingly arid.

#### Associated Human Species
For the first time in prehistory, this cultural phase is exclusively associated with **Anatomically Modern Homo Sapiens (AMHS)** (or *Homo sapiens sapiens*). Hominins now possessed a fully modern neural anatomy, allowing for abstract thought, symbolic art, and complex linguistic expression.

#### Blade-and-Burin Industry
The defining technological hallmark of the Upper Palaeolithic was the **Blade-and-Burin Industry**:
* **Parallel-Sided Blades**: A **blade** is defined as a stone flake that is at least twice as long as it is wide. These blades had straight, parallel sides and were systematically struck from cylindrical or prismatic cores using a **punch technique** (placing a bone or hardwood punch on the core and striking it with a hammer-stone).
* **Burins**: Chisel-like tools with a narrow, sharp, chisel-shaped cutting edge. They were used to engrave wood, bone, antler, and rock, and to cut deep grooves in organic materials to fashion tools.
* **Early Microliths**: Hominins began reducing tool sizes even further, creating miniature parallel-sided bladelets.

#### The Kurnool Bone Tool Industry
While stone was still widely used, the Upper Palaeolithic in India witnessed the extensive use of bone for tool manufacturing. The richest evidence comes from the cave complexes of Andhra Pradesh:
* **Billa Surgam Caves (Kurnool, AP)**: Excavated in the 1880s by **Robert Bruce Foote** and his son **Henry Bruce Foote**, these caves yielded bone tools in association with Late Pleistocene animal fossils. The tools included bone **awls**, **barbed and un-barbed arrowheads**, **daggers**, **scraper-knives**, **chisels**, **gouges**, **wedges**, and **axe-heads**. The technology was somewhat crude, reflecting the short-term, seasonal nature of cave occupations.
* **Muchchatla Chintamanu Gavi Cave (AP)**: Yielded bone **spatulas**, **scoops**, **chisels**, **perforators**, **shouldered points**, and **tanged points** alongside fine parallel-sided chert blades.

#### Art, Symbolic Behavior and Early Cults
* **Ostrich Eggshell Art**: Prehistoric humans in India hunted or gathered ostrich eggs (ostriches were indigenous to India during the arid Pleistocene). Hominins engraved concentric geometric lines on the shells and drilled holes to create **ostrich eggshell beads** and pendants. Important sites include **Patne (Maharashtra)**, **Ramnagar (MP)**, and **Chandresal (Rajasthan)**.
* **Mural Rock Paintings**: Prehistoric art emerged in the Upper Palaeolithic. Linear representations of massive animal herds (rhinoceroses, wild buffaloes, mammoths, bisons, boars) were executed on cave walls, particularly at **Bhimbetka (MP)**. Stick-like human figures were drawn in **green** (interpreted as dancers/ritual specialists) and **dark red** (interpreted as hunters). The pigments were prepared by grinding minerals like **ochre (geru)** and **green chalcedony** and mixing them with water or animal fat.

#### The Upper Palaeolithic Shrine at Baghor I
* **The Excavation**: Located in the Son valley of Madhya Pradesh, the site of **Baghor I** yielded fascinating evidence of an Upper Palaeolithic **shrine** dated between **9000 and 8000 BCE** (the transition from late Pleistocene to early Holocene).
* **The Structure**: Excavators discovered a roughly circular platform made of sandstone rubble, measuring about 85 cm in diameter. 
* **The Sacred Stone**: In the center of the platform was a naturally occurring triangular piece of sandstone showing concentric triangular laminations in colors ranging from yellowish-red to dark reddish-brown. Ten broken fragments of this stone were found scattered on and near the platform. When rejoined, they formed a perfect triangular stone measuring 15 cm high, 6.5 cm wide, and 6.5 cm thick.
* **Ethnographic Continuity**: Anthropologists have highlighted a striking parallel: the **Kol** and **Baiga** tribal communities living in the Kaimur hills today construct identical circular rubble platforms and worship similar naturally occurring triangular stones as a symbol of the **Female Principle** or as an icon of a local goddess (*Mai*). This represents one of the most remarkable examples of continuous **religious and symbolic continuity** in human history, spanning nearly 10,000 years.

#### Key Upper Palaeolithic Sites
* **Patne (Maharashtra)**: Provided a detailed stratigraphic sequence from Middle Palaeolithic to Mesolithic, yielding extensive ostrich eggshell beads and parallel-sided chert blades.
* **Baghor I and Baghor III (Madhya Pradesh)**: Son Valley sites yielding stone-working debitage, hearths, and the famous prehistoric shrine.
* **Kurnool Caves (Andhra Pradesh)**: Home to the Billa Surgam and Muchchatla Chintamanu Gavi bone-tool industries.
* **Renigunta (Chittoor District, AP)**: Located in the Rallakalava Valley, this site yielded a massive blade-and-burin industry.
* **Chopani Mando (Belan Valley, UP)**: Shows a clear cultural transition from the Upper Palaeolithic to the Mesolithic and Neolithic.
* **Rohri Hills (Sindh, Pakistan)**: Famous for massive chert quarrying and workshop sites.
* **Paisra (Bihar)**: Munger district, an open-air workshop site showing stone-tool working.

## THE MESOLITHIC AGE (HUNTERS AND HERDERS)

### Post-Glacial Environmental Adaptation & The Holocene Transition
The **Mesolithic** period (Middle Stone Age) represents an intermediate, transitional stage between the hunting-foraging Palaeolithic and the food-producing Neolithic. In the Indian subcontinent, the Mesolithic began around **10,000 BCE** (with the onset of the **Holocene Epoch**) and continued roughly until **4000/2000 BCE**.

With the end of the Pleistocene Ice Age around 12,000 years ago, global temperatures rose rapidly, glaciers retreated, and the climate became warm, wet, and rainy. Environmental adaptations varied across regions:
* **Rajasthan**: Sediment and pollen analysis from the dry salt lakes of **Didwana** indicates a marked increase in rainfall and the expansion of freshwater systems.
* **West Bengal**: Soil analysis at **Birbhanpur** suggests localized trends of increasing aridity.
* **Central India (Madhya Pradesh)**: The early and middle Holocene climate was highly humid and wet, characterized by heavy summer monsoon rains.

These climatic changes led to a flourishing of flora and fauna. Large, slow-moving Pleistocene animals were replaced by smaller, swift, and highly agile mammals (deer, antelopes, hares, wild boars) and aquatic resources (fish, mollusks, turtles). To hunt these fast animals, humans had to radically innovate their weapon technology, leading to the development of **microliths**. Hominins fanned out to occupy almost every ecological niche in India, colonizing areas such as the Indo-Gangetic plains, sand dunes, and high coastal zones for the first time.

---

### Microlithic Technology: Geometric and Non-Geometric Tools
The characteristic hallmark of the Mesolithic Age is the use of **microliths**-extremely small, highly standardized stone implements ranging from 1 to 5 cm in length. Hominins abandoned brute quartzite in favor of high-quality, fine-grained, crypto-crystalline silica-rich stones such as **chalcedony**, **chert**, **jasper**, **agate** and **quartz**.

Microliths are classified into two broad technological categories:
1. **Non-Geometric Microliths**: Earlier forms, including miniature parallel-sided blades, points, scrapers, awls, and borers.
2. **Geometric Microliths**: Later, highly advanced forms cut into precise shapes, including **triangles**, **trapezes**, **crescents (lunates)**, and rhomboids.

#### Hafting and the Composite Tool Revolution
Microliths were too small to be used directly by hand. Instead, they were designed as components of **composite tools**. Mesolithic humans carved grooves in shafts of wood or bone and **hafted** (fixed) multiple microliths into these slots using natural adhesives like **bitumen** or tree resin. 
* A series of microlithic blades hafted onto a bone handle created a serrated edge, forming the earliest **saws** and **sickles**.
* Triangles and lunates were hafted onto wooden shafts to serve as **arrowheads** and **spearheads**.
* This technological revolution introduced the **bow and arrow**, which dramatically increased hunting efficiency by allowing humans to kill fast-moving game from a distance.

---

### Subsistence Economy, Early Animal Domestication and Plant Cultivation
While Mesolithic communities remained primarily dependent on hunting, gathering, and fishing, their subsistence strategies became highly intensified. Rather than hunting at random, they became **species-specific hunters**, tracking and managing specific herds of animals.

#### Earliest Evidence of Animal Domestication
The Mesolithic period witnessed the critical transition from food-consuming hunter-gatherers to early animal keepers:
* **Bagor (Rajasthan)** and **Adamgarh (Madhya Pradesh)** have yielded the earliest undisputed evidence for the domestication of animals in the Indian portion of the subcontinent, dating to around **5000 BCE**.
* The **dog** was the first animal to be domesticated, used primarily for hunting and protection.
* This was followed by the domestication of **sheep**, **goats**, **cattle**, and **pigs**. These animals provided a stable source of milk, wool, and meat, buffering communities against seasonal food shortages.

#### Incipient Plant Cultivation
In the late stages of the Mesolithic, humans began manipulating plant species. Analysis of pollen and lake deposits at **Sambhar Salt Lake** in Rajasthan suggests that humans were clearing forests and practicing primitive, localized plant cultivation as early as **7000–6000 BCE**.

#### The Advent of Pottery
Although most Mesolithic sites are a-ceramic (lacking pottery), the late Mesolithic phase witnessed the early, independent manufacture of hand-made, poorly fired, and under-fired pottery. This early ceramic tradition, often featuring cord-impressed designs, has been excavated at **Langhnaj (Gujarat)**, **Chopani Mando (UP)**, and the Mirzapur caves (**Morahana Pahar**, **Baghai Khor**, **Lekhahia**).

#### Human Burials and Belief in Afterlife
Mesolithic sites have yielded numerous well-preserved human burials (e.g., at **Sarai Nahar Rai**, **Mahadaha**, and **Langhnaj**). 
* Humans were buried in a systematic **North-South** orientation, frequently within the habitation area or inside rock shelters.
* Grave goods, including microliths, animal bones, shell necklaces, and **bone ornaments**, were buried alongside the deceased.
* These elaborate mortuary practices indicate the development of abstract cosmological concepts, ritualized mourning, and a firm belief in an **afterlife**.

---

### The Flourishing of Rock Art
The Mesolithic period represents the golden age of prehistoric rock art in India. Prehistoric humans painted cave walls and rock shelters to record their daily lives, express ritual beliefs, and satisfy creative urges.

#### First Discovery of Rock Art
In **1867–68**, the archaeologist **Archibold Carlleyle** discovered prehistoric rock paintings at **Sohagighat** in the Kaimur hills of Mirzapur district, Uttar Pradesh. This discovery was made twelve years before the famous paintings of Altamira in Spain were found, making Sohagighat the **first recorded prehistoric rock art discovery in the world**. Today, over 150 Mesolithic rock art sites are known across India.

#### Artistic Techniques and Materials
* **Pigments**: The paints were prepared by grinding natural mineral pigments. **Iron oxide (geru or haematite)** was used for red, **limestone** for white, **charcoal or soot** for black, and **green chalcedony** for green. Yellow was produced through the hydration of iron oxides. Blue pigment was completely absent.
* **Binders**: Hominins mixed the ground mineral powder with water, animal fat, bone marrow, or egg white to make the paint long-lasting and water-resistant.
* **Brushes**: Brushes were made of soft wooden twigs chewed at one end, animal fur, squirrel tails, or the fibrous wood of the **semal (silk cotton)** tree.
* **Methods**: Paintings were executed inside caves (**petrographs**) or engraved/scraped onto rock surfaces (**petroglyphs**). Granite rocks in South India were also pecked or hammered (**rock bruisings**).

#### Themes and Subject Matter
* **Faunal Depictions**: Animals dominate Mesolithic rock art. Over 29 species are depicted with great anatomical accuracy, including **chital** (spotted deer, which occurs most frequently), bison, tiger, elephant, wild boar, rhinoceros, antelope, fish, lizards, frogs, and birds. **Notably, snakes are completely absent in Mesolithic paintings**.
* **Human Activities**: Hunting scenes predominate, depicting groups of hunters armed with barbed spears, pointed sticks, bows, and arrows chasing animals. Other scenes show humans setting traps and snares, gathering honey from beehives, fishing, herding, riding animals, dancing, playing music, and engaged in domestic tasks (women making food, children playing).
* **Gender Division of Labor**: Rock art clearly reflects early social structures. Men are usually depicted as hunters (stick-like figures drawn in red, sometimes wearing elaborate head-dresses or masks), while women are shown gathering food, cooking, and child-rearing.
* **Symbolic Art**: Complex geometric patterns such as spirals, rhomboids, concentric circles, and abstract signs (e.g., at **Jaora, MP**) point to the development of symbolic thought.

---

### Key Mesolithic Sites

#### Bhimbetka (Raisen District, Madhya Pradesh)
* **Discovery**: Discovered in **1957–58** by the eminent archaeologist **V.S. Wakankar**.
* **Geography**: Located 45 km south of Bhopal, the Bhimbetka hills are part of the Vindhyan range, covered in deciduous forests. Within an area of 10 square kilometers, there is a complex of nearly 800 prehistoric rock shelters, 500 of which contain paintings. It was declared a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** in 2003.
* **Excavation Findings**: While primarily famous for its Mesolithic paintings, excavations at Bhimbetka revealed a deep stratigraphic sequence from the Lower Palaeolithic through the Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic, and early Historic periods. 
* **Contact & Trade**: The Mesolithic levels yielded clear evidence of contact between hunter-gatherers and early settled agriculturalists. This includes copper tools, painted pottery, and beads made of steatite, faience, agate, carnelian, and terracotta.
* **The 'Zoo Rock Shelter'**: A famous shelter at Bhimbetka depicting a dense array of animal paintings, illustrating the rich biodiversity of the region during the early Holocene.

#### Bagor (Bhilwara District, Rajasthan)
* **Geography**: Located on a large sand dune on the bank of the **Kothari River**, about 25 km west of Bhilwara.
* **Significance**: Bagor is the **largest and best-documented Mesolithic site in India**, excavated by V.N. Misra. It represents continuous human occupation over 5,000 years.
* **Chronological Phases**:
  * **Period I (c. 5000–2800 BCE)**: Purely Mesolithic phase. Characterized by a highly distinctive and prolific microlithic industry (chert and quartz tools), paved stone floors, circular wattle-and-daub huts, and abundant bones of domesticated sheep, goats, and cattle.
  * **Period II (c. 2800–600 BCE)**: Chalcolithic phase, yielding copper tools and pottery.
  * **Period III (c. 600 BCE–200 BCE)**: Iron Age phase.
* **Sub-division**: Recent excavations have further subdivided the Mesolithic at Bagor into an **a-ceramic phase (5700–4500 BCE)** and a **ceramic phase (4500–3500 BCE)**.
* **The Chalcedony Trade**: A specialized study of chalcedony raw materials at Bagor by **Gurcharan S. Khanna** revealed that Mesolithic humans traveled long distances or engaged in complex regional exchange networks to procure high-quality chalcedony for making their microliths.

#### Adamgarh (Hoshangabad District, Madhya Pradesh)
* **Findings**: Located on a hill near the Narmada River, the upper layers of this site represent a highly significant Mesolithic level.
* **Tool Industry**: Thousands of microliths made of chert, chalcedony, jasper, and agate were found. Hominins gathered these raw materials from the riverbed of the Narmada, located 2 km away. Mace-heads, ring-stones, and hammer-stones were also recovered.
* **Domestication & Dating**: Radiocarbon dating of shells from the Mesolithic deposits yielded a date of **c. 5500 BCE**. The site has provided critical early evidence for the domestication of cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and dogs. Handmade pottery was also found in the Mesolithic levels.

#### Chopani Mando (Belan Valley, Uttar Pradesh)
* **Sequence**: Chopani Mando represents an invaluable habitation site showing a continuous cultural sequence from the Upper Palaeolithic through the Mesolithic to the Neolithic.
* **Excavated Layers**: Excavated by G.R. Sharma, the 1.55 m thick deposit is divided into three periods:
  * **Period I**: Epi-Palaeolithic stage.
  * **Period IIA (Early Mesolithic)**: Characterized by non-geometric microliths made of chert. Hominins lived in circular wattle-and-daub huts (the outlines of two round huts were excavated).
  * **Period IIB (Middle Mesolithic)**: Marked by the introduction of geometric microliths and five round huts.
  * **Period III (Late Mesolithic / Proto-Neolithic)**: Marked by a cluster of 13 round and oval huts built close together. The round huts had an average diameter of 3.3 m, while the oval ones measured 4.7 x 3.3 m. Outside the huts were three hearths, clay-and-bamboo storage bins, and handmade pottery with **cord-impressed patterns**. Crucially, carbonized seeds of **wild rice** were recovered from this late Mesolithic layer, showcasing the transition to plant exploitation.

#### Langhnaj (Mehsana District, Gujarat)
* **Excavated Layers**: Divided into three periods. Period I (Mesolithic) yielded microliths, human burials, bones of wild animals (including wild cattle and rhinoceros), and potsherds. It is one of the rare Mesolithic sites to show early hand-made pottery in association with microliths.

## THE NEOLITHIC AGE (THE FOOD-PRODUCING REVOLUTION)

### Defining the 'Neolithic Revolution'
The term **"Neolithic"** was coined by **Sir John Lubbock** in 1865 to denote the New Stone Age, characterized by the appearance of ground and polished stone tools. However, in the 1930s, the preeminent prehistorian **V. Gordon Childe** redefined the significance of this period by coining the term **"Neolithic Revolution"**. 

Childe argued that the transition from a food-consuming, nomadic lifestyle (hunting-gathering) to a food-producing, sedentary lifestyle (agriculture and animal husbandry) was one of the most profound revolutions in human history. It led directly to:
* **Sedentism**: The establishment of permanent village settlements.
* **Demographic Expansion**: A rapid increase in human population due to a stable food supply.
* **Pottery Making**: Fired ceramics to store grain surpluses, cook food, and boil liquids.
* **Social Complexity**: The emergence of property ownership, division of labor, craft specialization, chiefdoms, and long-distance trade.

While in the global context the Neolithic began around 9000 BCE (in the Fertile Crescent of West Asia), in the Indian subcontinent, its earliest manifestation dates to **c. 7000 BCE** at Mehrgarh, while in other regions (such as southern and eastern India) it emerged as late as **3000–1000 BCE**.

---

### Ground and Polished Stone Tool Technology
Neolithic technology represents a major advance over earlier Stone Age phases:
* **Raw Materials**: Hominins abandoned fragile quartzite and quartz in favor of tough, heavy igneous and metamorphic rocks, primarily **basalt**, **dolerite**, and **granite**.
* **Manufacturing Technique**: Tools were shaped using **pecking** (hammering the stone to create rough shapes), **grinding** (rubbing the tool against abrasive sandstone slabs), and **polishing** to create smooth, durable, and highly sharp cutting edges.
* **The Celt**: The defining Neolithic implement was the **celt**-a ground and polished stone axe head. Celts were fitted (hafted) into wooden handles to clear dense forests and till the soil. Other tools included stone **hoes**, **digging sticks** fitted with heavy **ring-stones** (used as weights to break soil), **querns** and **pestles/mullers** (for grinding grain), and **stone sickles** made of microlithic blades hafted onto wooden handles.

### Regional Neolithic Traditions in India

![[Pasted image 20260825135736.png|600]]

The Neolithic culture in the Indian subcontinent was not a single, uniform phenomenon. Instead, it was comprised of distinct regional traditions, each adapting to its local environment and ecological resources:

#### The Northwestern Neolithic (Mehrgarh & Baluchistan)
This represents the oldest and most advanced Neolithic tradition in the subcontinent, serving as the foundational cradle for the subsequent Indus Valley Civilization.

##### Mehrgarh (Balochistan, Pakistan)
* **Geography**: Located on the **Kacchi Plain** of Balochistan near the **Bolan Pass**, a major trade route to Iran. It lies along the Bolan River, on the fertile edge of the Indus plains, a region historically called the **"Breadbasket of Baluchistan"**. The site covers a massive area of 200 hectares.
* **Period I (c. 7000–5500 BCE) - Pre-Pottery (A-Ceramic) Neolithic**:
  * **Agriculture**: Hominins cultivated **six-row barley**, **emmer wheat**, and **einkorn wheat**, and gathered wild dates, jujube, and plums. Mehrgarh is recognized as a primary nuclear center for independent barley domestication.
  * **Animal Husbandry**: Hominins domesticated sheep, goats, and cattle. In the early levels, bones of wild animals (gazelle, nilgai, wild ass, swamp deer) predominated, but over time, bones of domesticated cattle grew rapidly, outnumbering sheep and goats.
  * **Architecture**: Built permanent **mud-brick houses** of rectangular or square shapes. These houses were divided into four or more compartments, some of which were used as **granaries** to store food surpluses.
  * **Burial Practices**: The dead were buried under house floors or in open spaces between houses. Grave goods were rich: bodies were adorned with ornaments of sea shells (indicating trade with the Arabian Sea coast, 500 km away), **turquoise** (imported from the Nishapur mines of Iran), **lapis lazuli** (imported from Badakshan, Afghanistan), and sandstone.
* **Period II (c. 6500–4500 BCE) - Ceramic Neolithic**:
  * **Inventions**: This period provides the **earliest evidence of cotton cultivation** in the world, as well as grapes. Hominins began manufacturing hand-made pottery.
  * **Crafts**: Glazed faience beads, ivory working, and female terracotta figurines were produced. Hominins manufactured refined sickles made by hafting microlithic blades onto wooden handles using **bitumen** as glue.
* **Period III (c. 4800–3500 BCE)**:
  * Characterized by the introduction of the **potter's wheel**, leading to a rapid multiplication of wheel-made, painted pottery featuring intricate human and floral designs. Early traces of copper working (metallurgy) have been recovered from this layer.

#### The Northern Neolithic (Kashmir Valley)
The Kashmiri Neolithic is highly distinct from all other traditions in the subcontinent. It is characterized by pit dwellings, a rich bone tool industry, and a complete absence of microliths.

##### Burzahom ("The Place of Birch")
* **Geography**: Situated 16 km northwest of Srinagar on a scenic lakeside terrace.
* **Pit Dwellings**: To survive the freezing, wind-swept winters of Kashmir, Neolithic humans lived in circular or rectangular **pit dwellings** dug deep into the natural soil (*karewas*). The pits were narrow at the top and broad at the base, plastered with clay, and reached using timber steps or ladders. Post-holes dug around the rims of the pits indicate they were covered with conical thatched roofs made of birch wood and reeds. Hearth-places found inside and outside the pits show that humans lived indoors during winter and outdoors during summer.
* **The Bone Tool Industry**: Burzahom has yielded the most prolific bone tool industry in India, including bone needles, scrapers, borers, chisels, awls, harpoons for fishing, and curved bone harvesters.
* **Mortuary Practices & Dog Burials**: Burzahom is unique in the subcontinent for practicing **joint Master-Dog burials**. Domesticated dogs were buried alongside their owners in graves, a practice otherwise unknown in Indian Neolithic cultures but suggesting cultural contacts with Neolithic China (Yangshao culture). 
* **Rock Art**: A famous engraved stone slab found at Burzahom depicts a hunting scene where a hunter spears a stag while a dog barks, set beneath two suns (or a sun and a moon).

##### Gufkral ("The Cave of the Potter")
* **Geography**: Located 41 km southwest of Srinagar.
* **Findings**: Shows a continuous sequence from an **a-ceramic Neolithic phase** to a ceramic Neolithic phase, followed by a Megalithic phase.
* **Subsistence**: The Gufkral people practiced both agriculture (wheat, barley, lentils, and peas) and herding (cattle, sheep, goats). 
* **Megalithic Phase Transition**: The Neolithic Gufkral was succeeded by a Megalithic phase, marked by the erection of **menhirs** (massive standing stones), large finished and unfinished ring-stones, copper points, and bone-marrow sockets used as tool handles.

#### The Ganges Valley & Central India (Belan & Vindhyan Regions)
This region is highly significant for providing some of the earliest global evidence for the transition from wild foraging to domesticated **rice cultivation**.

##### Lahuradewa (Sant Kabir Nagar District, Uttar Pradesh)
* **The Excavation**: Located in the trans-Sarayu plain of the middle Ganga valley.
* **The Breakthrough**: Recent multidisciplinary excavations at Lahuradewa yielded carbonized grains of domesticated rice dated as far back as **6500 BCE** (with some indicators suggesting wild/domesticated rice use up to 9000–8000 BCE). This is the **earliest evidence of agriculture and rice domestication in the entire Indian subcontinent**, challenging the traditional view that agriculture dispersed to India from West Asia.

##### Koldihwa (Allahabad District, Uttar Pradesh)
* **Findings**: Located on the Belan River. It historically yielded some of the earliest evidence of domesticated rice, dating to around 5500 BCE (though this chronology remains a subject of intense academic debate).
* **Technology**: Characterized by distinctive **cord-marked pottery** or cord-impressed ware ( handmade pottery with bowls and storage jars showing impressions of cords or reeds). The pottery clay was mixed with rice husk as a temper.

##### Chirand (Saran District, Bihar)
* **Geography**: Located on a large mound at the confluence of four major rivers: the Ganges, Sarayu (Ghagra), Son, and Gandak. This river junction provided fertile, open alluvial land receiving 100 cm of rainfall.
* **The Antler and Bone Industry**: Chirand is famous for its exceptionally rich **bone and antler tool industry**, second only to Kashmir. Hominins manufactured tools out of deer antlers, including bone celts, scrapers, chisels, needles, awls, diggers, pins, and bone ornaments (combs, pendants, earrings, and bangles).
* **Houses & Subsistence**: People lived in circular and semicircular houses with wattle-and-daub walls and clay-plastered floors. They practiced **double cropping**, cultivating rice, wheat, barley, moong, and lentils. Humped bull terracotta figurines, beads of semi-precious stone (agate, jasper, carnelian), and clay slingstones were also found.

#### The Eastern & Northeastern Neolithic
* **Geography**: Covers the hills of Assam, Meghalaya, Nagaland, and Arunachal Pradesh.
* **The Shouldered Celt**: This tradition is defined by ground and polished stone axes featuring a distinct "shoulder" (known as **shouldered axes** or shouldered celts). These tools show strong technological and stylistic affinities with the Neolithic cultures of Southeast Asia and southern China.
* **Pottery**: Dominated by crude, hand-made **cord-marked** and **paddle-impressed** pottery.
* **Daojali Hading (Assam)**: Located in the hills of Assam, this is the easternmost Neolithic site in India. It yielded shouldered axes, grinding stones, pestles, and corded ware pottery.
* **Lawnongthroh (Meghalaya)**: Calibrated radiocarbon dates place this site around **1220 BCE**.
* **Late Persistence**: Because of the remote, forested terrain, the Neolithic stone-tool technology persisted exceptionally late in the Northeast. At some sites, polished stone celts continued to be manufactured and used well into the historical period (e.g., Kanai Gaon in Assam is dated to the 6th century CE, and Chungliyimti in Nagaland is dated to **980–1647 CE**), long after copper and iron were introduced elsewhere.

#### The Southern Neolithic (Deccan Agro-Pastoralists)
The Southern Neolithic tradition, spread across Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Tamil Nadu, represents a highly extensive and unique pastoral landscape.

##### The Agro-Pastoral Economy
* **The Cattle Culture**: The economy was heavily centered on **pastoralism**. Cattle (**Bos indicus**) were the primary source of wealth and cultural identity. Communities also herded sheep, goats, pigs, and dogs. Terracotta figurines of humped bulls have been found in abundance.
* **Crops**: Due to the semi-arid climate of the Deccan, the primary crops were drought-resistant **ragi (millet)**, **horsegram (kulathi)**, green gram, and black gram.

##### The Problem of the Ashmounds
The most distinctive and puzzling feature of South Indian Neolithic sites is the presence of **Ashmounds**-massive mounds of accumulated, grey, vitrified ash layers.
* **Early Debates and Legends**: Local legends believed these mounds were the cremation grounds of Rakshasas (demons) from the *Mahabharata*, or the physical remains of mass *sati* (widow burning) performed during the medieval wars between Vijayanagara and the Delhi Sultanate. Early geologists classified them as volcanic ash or kankar formations, while others suggested industrial slag from ancient gold or iron smelting.
* **The Breakthrough**: The geologist **Robert Bruce Foote** was the first to notice their close stratigraphic association with Neolithic settlements and identified them as Neolithic.
* **F.R. Allchin's Utnur Excavation (1960s)**: Allchin excavated the ashmound at **Utnur (Telangana)**. He discovered wild cattle hoof impressions and post-holes representing stockade fences. He proved that the ashmounds were **cow pens (cattle stockades)**. Cow dung accumulated in large quantities inside these stockades over seasons. During seasonal migrations or pastoral festivals, this accumulated dung was intentionally set on fire (vitrified), leading to the formation of distinct ash layers. This was part of a **Neolithic fire cult** meant to promote the fertility of the cattle herds.
* **K. Paddayya's Budihal Excavation**: Paddayya conducted horizontal excavations at **Budihal (Karnataka)**. He challenged Allchin's rigid separation of ashmounds from human settlements. Paddayya proved that ashmounds were **full-fledged pastoral settlements** featuring residential quarters, cattle pens, garbage dumps, and sandstone-working chert workshops. Budihal functioned as a **congregational hub** (similar to modern-day cattle fairs) where multiple pastoral communities gathered for social, cultural, and trade transactions, creating these massive mounds over centuries.

##### Rock Bruisings and Granite Gongs
* South Indian Neolithic sites are surrounded by granite hills (such as **Kupgal** or Hiregudda in Bellary, Karnataka). Hominins pecked, hammered, or bruised the granite surfaces to create **rock bruisings** depicting humped cattle, hunters, ithyphallic figures, and heterosexual scenes.
* Granite boulders showing multiple cupule marks on their surfaces functioned as **rock gongs** or musical lithophones, used during ceremonies.
* Important southern sites include **Sanganakallu**, **Kupgal**, **Budihal**, **Piklihal**, **Brahmagiri**, **Maski**, **Hallur**, **Tekkalakota** (Karnataka); **Utnur** (Telangana); **Palavoy** (Andhra Pradesh); and **Paiyampalli** (Tamil Nadu).
