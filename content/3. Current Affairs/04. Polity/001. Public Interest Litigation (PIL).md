## Meaning

- **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** is a legal mechanism through which courts hear cases for the **protection and enforcement of public or general interest**, especially where affected persons are **poor, marginalised, voiceless or unable to approach courts themselves**.
- In **Janata Dal v. H.S. Chaudhary (1993)**, the Supreme Court described PIL as a legal action for enforcing **public or general interest** where the public or a class of the public has an interest affecting its **legal rights or liabilities**.
- **Public interest** means the interest of a **particular class of the community** affecting its legal rights or liabilities.
- Unlike ordinary litigation, PIL is **not based on personal injury alone**. A **public-spirited person or organisation** may approach the court on behalf of affected persons.
- PIL is **different from traditional adversarial litigation** between two parties. Courts assume a **more proactive role** in PIL.
- PIL has **neither been defined nor separately mentioned in the Constitution or any Indian statute**. It developed through **judicial interpretation**.
- The concept of PIL originated in the **USA in the 1960s**.

## Origin In India

- PIL developed mainly during the **late 1970s and 1980s**, alongside expansion of **Article 21** and relaxation of the traditional rule of **locus standi**.
- Earlier, only a person **directly affected by a legal wrong** could approach the court. PIL enabled others to approach courts on behalf of **disadvantaged groups**.
- It became particularly important for **bonded labourers, undertrial prisoners, pavement dwellers, children, women, prisoners, workers, tribal communities, victims of state negligence and environmental causes**.
- **Mumbai Kamgar Sabha v. Abdulbhai (1976):** Justice **Krishna Iyer** emphasised PIL's role in ensuring that the legal system serves the **poor and oppressed**. PIL was intended to **complement, not substitute, traditional litigation**.
- **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979):** first reported instance of PIL; highlighted the **inhuman conditions of prisoners and undertrial prisoners** and recognised **speedy trial as part of Article 21**.
- **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981):** ushered in a new era of PIL by expanding **locus standi**. An individual or **social action group** could invoke **Article 32 or Article 226** for persons unable to approach courts because of **social, economic or other disabilities**.

## Constitutional Basis

- **Article 32:** Right to move the **Supreme Court** for enforcement of **Fundamental Rights**.
- **Article 226:** Power of **High Courts** to issue writs for **Fundamental Rights and other legal rights**.
- **Article 21:** **Right to life and personal liberty**, whose scope was significantly expanded through PIL.
- **Article 39A:** **Equal justice and free legal aid**.
- PIL widened access to **Articles 32 and 226** by allowing public-spirited persons to approach courts on behalf of those unable to do so themselves.

## Key Features

- Filed for a **public cause, not private gain**.
- **Petitioner need not be personally affected**, reflecting relaxation of **locus standi**.
- **Any person or organisation** acting **in good faith and in public interest** may file a PIL, including **NGOs and consumer organisations**.
- Courts may accept **letters, postcards or newspaper reports as petitions** in suitable cases.
- Courts may also take **suo motu action** on matters of public importance.
- Used to protect **Fundamental Rights, human dignity and legal or constitutional rights**.
- Courts may appoint **commissions or expert committees** to investigate facts.
- Mainly used where **executive inaction, state failure or rights violations** affect vulnerable groups.
- PIL provides greater **procedural flexibility**, but must still follow **judicial procedure and principles**.

## Landmark Cases

- **Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979):** recognised **speedy trial as part of Article 21** and highlighted conditions of **undertrial prisoners**.
- **S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981):** expanded **locus standi** and gave wider acceptance to PIL.
- **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984):** concerned exploitation of **bonded labourers** and was the **first PIL filed by an NGO**. The Supreme Court ordered release of bonded labourers and **compensation** and strengthened judicial protection of **labour rights**.
- **Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1985):** addressed mining causing **environmental degradation, deforestation and displacement**. Recognised a **healthy environment as integral to Article 21**.
- **Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985):** connected the **right to livelihood** with **Article 21**.
- **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) and M.C. Mehta cases:** developed **environmental jurisprudence** concerning **pollution control, Ganga cleaning, vehicular pollution and industrial hazards**. The Court replaced the **strict liability principle with the absolute liability principle** to protect citizens' rights.
- **People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (1997):** dealt with **custodial deaths and legal aid**. Held that **Article 21** includes the right to live with **human dignity, free from torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment**.
- **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997):** laid down guidelines against **sexual harassment at the workplace** before legislation existed. It was followed by the **Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013**.

## Importance

- **Democratised access to justice**, particularly for marginalised and underrepresented communities.
- **Widened the scope of Article 32** and **expanded Article 21**.
- Protected rights of **bonded labourers, prisoners, slum and pavement dwellers and other marginalised groups**.
- Improved **prison and labour conditions**.
- Strengthened **environmental protection**.
- Increased **executive accountability** and addressed **governance failures**.
- Enabled courts to take **suo motu action** on matters of public importance.
- Contributed to **social and political change** and development of **new principles of constitutional law**.
- Made the judiciary a more active protector of **constitutional rights and social justice**.

## Areas Where PIL Has Been Used

- **Prison reforms**
- **Bonded labour and child labour**
- **Environmental protection and urban pollution**
- **Women's safety and sexual harassment**
- **Corruption and transparency**
- **Police reforms**
- **Food security**
- **Displacement and rehabilitation**
- **Rights of pavement dwellers**
- **Electoral reforms**

## Factors Responsible For The Increase In PIL Cases

- **Awareness and education:** greater knowledge of the legal system and **human rights** has increased use of PIL for social change.
- **Strengthening of the legal system:** improved access to courts and Supreme Court guidelines have made PIL procedures more **transparent and streamlined**.
- **Media coverage:** greater reporting has brought attention to issues that might otherwise remain unnoticed.
- **Increased activism:** more individuals and groups are using PIL to pursue public-interest issues.
- **Political support:** support from political leaders has also contributed to increased filing of PIL cases.

## Concerns

- **Misuse of PIL** for **personal or political gain** rather than public good.
- **Publicity-oriented petitions** and **private interest litigation disguised as public interest**.
- PIL may become **personal interest litigation, publicity interest litigation or political interest litigation**.
- **Interference in executive policy matters** and **judicial overreach**, particularly while resolving socio-economic or environmental issues.
- **Excessive burden on courts**, as PILs may require extensive research and investigation.
- **Vague petitions without proper research**.
- **Frivolous PILs** can delay genuine cases and contribute to the **backlog of cases**.
- Existing **judicial vacancies and pending cases** can also delay disposal of PILs.
- **Lack of effective implementation of court verdicts** can limit the success of PIL.

## Supreme Court Guidelines On The Applicability Of PIL Petitions

- Supreme Court guidelines issued in **1998 and 2003** generally permit PIL involving:
    - **Bonded labour**
    - **Neglected children**
    - **Exploitation of casual workers**
    - **Atrocities against women**
    - **Harassment of prisoners**
    - **Family disputes**
    - **Atrocities against Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes**
- Matters generally **not considered PIL** include:
    - **Landlord-tenant disputes**
    - **Service matters**
    - **Pension and gratuity**
    - **Admission to educational institutions**
    - **Early hearing of cases pending in lower courts**

## Judicial Approach To Misuse

- Courts have become **more cautious in admitting PILs**.
- Courts must **support legitimate PILs** while deterring petitions filed for extraneous purposes.
- Each High Court should establish **formal rules** to encourage genuine PIL and discourage misuse.
- Courts should **verify the petitioner's credentials and bona fide intention**.
- Courts must be satisfied that the matter involves **genuine public interest and real public harm or injury**.
- The petitioner must have **no personal, private or ulterior motive**.
- PIL may be rejected where:
    - the matter is **political or motivated**
    - it concerns a **private dispute**
    - it is filed for **publicity**
    - there is **suppression of facts**
    - the matter is better handled by the **executive or legislature**
- Courts may impose **costs or penalties on frivolous petitions** and discourage **busybodies** from filing PILs for extraneous reasons.

## Present Relevance

- PIL remains significant because governance failures continue to affect vulnerable citizens.
- Contemporary issues requiring judicial attention include **pollution, custodial violence, manual scavenging, prison overcrowding, displacement, women's safety, digital rights and climate risks**.
- The central challenge is preserving PIL as a **tool of social justice** while preventing its misuse for **politics, publicity or judicial overreach**.

## Way Forward

- PIL should remain focused on **genuine public injury and constitutional rights**.
- Courts should **screen petitions carefully** and prioritise cases involving **vulnerable groups**.
- **Publicity-driven and frivolous petitions** should be discouraged, including through **imposition of costs**.
- Courts should avoid entering **purely policy domains unless rights are clearly violated**.
- **Expert committees** should be used where technical issues are involved.
- Courts should ensure **time-bound monitoring in continuing mandamus cases**.
- The purpose of PIL should remain **access to justice, not parallel governance**.

## Conclusion

- **Public Interest Litigation is one of India's most important judicial innovations**, transforming courts beyond dispute-settling institutions into protectors of **constitutional rights and social justice**.
- Its major contribution has been giving a **voice to people unable to reach courts themselves**.
- Its future depends on maintaining a balance: **protecting the powerless without allowing PIL to become a substitute for governance or a platform for publicity**.
