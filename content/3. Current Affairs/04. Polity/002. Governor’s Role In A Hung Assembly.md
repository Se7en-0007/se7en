## What Is A Hung Assembly ?

- A **hung Assembly** arises when **no single party or pre-poll alliance secures a clear majority** in the Legislative Assembly.
- It triggers **coalition negotiations** and dependence on **smaller parties or independent MLAs** for majority support.
- In such a situation, the **Governor becomes the key constitutional authority** deciding whom to invite to form the government.

## Constitutional Provisions

- **Article 163:** Governor ordinarily acts on the **aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**, except in matters where the Constitution requires the exercise of **discretion**.
- **Article 163(2):** If a question arises whether a matter requires the Governor to act in his discretion, the **Governor's decision is final and cannot be called in question**.
- **Article 164(1):**
  - **Chief Minister is appointed by the Governor**.
  - Other Ministers are appointed by the Governor on the **advice of the Chief Minister**.
- When a party has a **clear majority**, the Governor ordinarily invites the **leader of that legislative party** to form the government.
- When **no party commands a majority**, the Governor exercises **discretion in appointing the Chief Minister**.
- The Constitution **does not prescribe a fixed or settled procedure** for selecting the Chief Minister in a hung Assembly.

## Governor’s Role In A Hung Assembly

- Governor's **primary objective** is the formation of a **stable and responsible government** capable of enjoying the **confidence of the House**.
- The Governor should:
  - **Explore all possibilities** with political parties, groups and **independent MLAs**.
  - Complete this process within a **reasonable time**.
  - Act **legally, impartially and constitutionally**, rather than according to **personal bona fide or ipse dixit**.
- The Constitution does **not define "reasonable time"**.
- Governor cannot wait **indefinitely**, as prolonged uncertainty may create opportunities for **horse-trading**.
- If government formation is possible, the Governor fixes a time for administering the **oath of office to the Chief Minister-designate**.
- A **pro-tem Speaker**, usually the **senior-most elected legislator**, may be appointed to administer oath to MLAs and conduct the **floor test**.

## Order Of Preference For Inviting A Party To Form Government

### Sarkaria Commission

- The **Sarkaria Commission** recommended an order of preference where no party commands an absolute majority:
  1. **Pre-poll alliance commanding a majority**.
  2. **Single largest party** staking a claim with support of others, including **independents**.
  3. **Post-electoral coalition**, with **all coalition partners joining the government**.
  4. **Post-electoral alliance**, where some parties join the government while others, including independents, provide **outside support**.
- If a **single party has an absolute majority**, its leader should automatically be invited to become the Chief Minister.

### Punchhi Commission

- The **Punchhi Commission** broadly endorsed the **Sarkaria framework**.
- Emphasised **constitutional neutrality** and that Governors should **not function as political agents**.
- Recommended that a **floor test should be conducted as early as possible** to determine majority.
- **Pre-poll alliances** should receive priority over post-poll arrangements because they represent a **clearer electoral mandate**.

### Committee Of Governors

- A **five-member Committee of Governors** was appointed by the President following the **Conference of Governors held in New Delhi in November 1970**.
- It concluded that the **test of confidence should normally be left to a vote in the Assembly**.

## Minority Government And Confidence Of The House

- **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994):**
  - The Constitution does **not require the political party forming the government itself to possess a majority**.
  - **Minority governments are constitutionally possible**.
  - What matters is whether the government **enjoys the confidence of the House**.
- Therefore, the central constitutional question is not whether a party independently possesses **50 percent plus one of the total membership**, but whether the government can **command legislative confidence**.

## Floor Test As A Measure Of Majority

- A **floor test**, also called a **trust vote**, determines whether a government enjoys the **confidence of the Legislative Assembly**.
- Successive Supreme Court precedents have treated the floor test as the **most objective and transparent means of determining legislative majority**.
- Majority should be established **on the floor of the House**, rather than through the Governor's **subjective satisfaction**.
- Supreme Court rulings have emphasised that the **electorate's mandate should not depend upon the personal discretion of the Governor**.
- **S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994):** held that the **floor of the House is the constitutionally ordained forum** for testing majority support.
- This principle was reiterated in **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)**.
- However, **S.R. Bommai** also contains a paragraph suggesting that a floor test applies to testing whether an **incumbent Chief Minister has lost majority**, rather than government formation immediately after elections.
- Despite this, subsequent Supreme Court precedents increasingly relied upon **floor tests even in government-formation disputes**.
- The Supreme Court has repeatedly maintained that the **House, not Raj Bhavan, is the place where democracy is in action**.

## Majority Of Members Present And Voting

- In the Tamil Nadu episode, the Governor's office initially sought support of **118 MLAs**, based on the **234-member full strength of the Assembly**.
- However, the constitutional requirement for sustaining a government is the support of the **majority of members present and voting in the Assembly**.
- Therefore, the majority required during a confidence vote may be **below 118 if members abstain**.
- The full-strength figure of **118 does not account for possible abstentions**.

## Supreme Court Judgments

### S.R. Bommai v. Union Of India, 1994

- Majority should ordinarily be established on the **floor of the House**.
- A ruling party need not independently possess a majority if its government **enjoys the confidence of the House**.
- Limited reliance on the **subjective assessment of the Governor**.

### B.R. Kapur Case, 2001

- Recognised the constitutional position relating to the Governor's powers when the formation of a viable government becomes impossible.
- Along with subsequent jurisprudence, recognised that the Governor cannot remain **indefinitely in limbo** without either enabling government formation or taking constitutionally permissible alternatives.

### Rameshwar Prasad v. Union Of India, 2006

- Warned against **premature dissolution**.
- Political parties should receive a **fair opportunity to form a government**.
- Reaffirmed the importance of determining majority through the **legislative process**.

### Uttarakhand Crisis, 2016

- Supreme Court underlined the **floor test as the ultimate option** for determining majority.
- Directed Chief Minister **Harish Rawat** to prove his majority on the **floor of the House**.

## Goa Political Crisis, 2017

- **Congress emerged as the single largest party**, but the Governor invited the **BJP-led post-poll alliance** headed by **Manohar Parrikar** to form the government.
- Supreme Court **declined to stay Parrikar's swearing-in**.
- Governor had originally provided **15 days** to prove majority.
- Supreme Court reduced this to **48 hours** and ordered a **floor test**.
- **Parrikar won the floor test**.
- The episode demonstrated judicial preference for an **immediate legislative determination of majority** rather than prolonged gubernatorial assessment.

## Manipur Political Crisis, 2017

- **Congress emerged as the single largest party**, but the Governor invited a **BJP-led post-poll alliance** to form the government.
- The government subsequently **proved its majority in the Assembly**.

## Karnataka Political Crisis, 2018

- Governor invited the **BJP**, the single largest party, to form the government despite the claim of the **Congress-Janata Dal (Secular) post-poll alliance**.
- **B.S. Yediyurappa** was originally given **15 days** to establish majority.
- Supreme Court reduced the period to **24 hours**.
- Directed that the floor test:
  - Be conducted on **live camera**.
  - **Not be held through secret ballot**.
- **Yediyurappa resigned before the trust vote** after being unable to muster the required majority.

## Maharashtra Political Crisis, 2019

- Governor appointed a **BJP-led coalition government** amid uncertainty about whether it possessed majority support.
- The Chief Minister subsequently **resigned after being unable to muster the required majority**.

## Power To Dissolve The Assembly

- **Article 174(2)(b)** provides for dissolution of the Legislative Assembly.
- **B.R. Kapur (2001)** and **Rameshwar Prasad (2006)** recognised that an Assembly may be dissolved even **before its first meeting** when political parties are unable to stake a viable claim for government formation and constitutional machinery cannot otherwise function.
- Governor cannot remain **in limbo**, unable either to appoint a government or exercise constitutionally available alternatives.

## President’s Rule As A Last Resort

- If **all reasonable possibilities of forming a stable government fail**, the Governor may initiate the process for **President's Rule under Article 356**.
- It is an **extreme measure and last constitutional option**.
- Constitutional machinery in the State should, **as far as possible, be maintained**.
- Concerns have arisen over Governors allegedly using discretionary powers to recommend President's Rule to **promote the political interests of the party in power at the Centre**.

## Issues

- Recommendations of commissions and constitutional conventions require Governors to act in a **bipartisan and constitutionally neutral manner**.
- Yet Governors have on different occasions followed **different preferences**:
  - **Goa and Manipur, 2017:** post-poll alliances preferred over the single largest party.
  - **Karnataka, 2018:** single largest party preferred over a post-poll alliance.
  - **Maharashtra, 2019:** government appointed despite uncertainty regarding majority.
- This inconsistent exercise of discretion has generated concerns about:
  - **Political bias**.
  - **Subjective gubernatorial discretion**.
  - **Horse-trading during prolonged uncertainty**.
  - Governors functioning as **agents of the Union government rather than impartial constitutional heads**.
- Judicial pronouncements have attempted to regulate discretionary powers, but **differences in judicial interpretation have resulted in inconsistent application of principles**.

## Way Forward

- The Governor's discretion exists to facilitate a government that is **stable and enjoys majority support in the Assembly**, not to independently determine political legitimacy.
- Majority claims should ordinarily be settled through an **early floor test**.
- Governors should exercise discretionary powers in a **bona fide, impartial and constitutionally neutral manner**.
- **Justice Kurian Joseph Committee on Union-State relations**, constituted by the earlier Tamil Nadu government, recommended incorporating a **new Schedule into the Constitution** to codify rules governing the Governor's exercise of **discretionary powers**.
- Such codification could provide a clearer **constitutional basis for gubernatorial discretion** and reduce inconsistent application.
- The fundamental principle remains that the question of **who commands the confidence of the legislature should ultimately be determined on the floor of the House, rather than through the Governor's subjective assessment**.
