- Since **2020**, **Unconditional Cash Transfer (UCT)** schemes have become an important electoral strategy in India, particularly to attract **women voters**.
- Examples include **Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thittam** in Tamil Nadu, **Lakshmir Bhandar** in West Bengal, and **Gruha Lakshmi Yojana** in Karnataka.
- Despite increasing cash amounts before the **2026 elections**, some governments implementing these schemes were defeated. One possible reason is the **political cost associated with UCTs**.

## Financial Support To Women

- UCT schemes provide **financial support to women** and partially advance **SDG 5.4**, which calls for recognition of women’s **unpaid domestic and care work**.
- States were expected to spend about **$18 billion** on UCTs in **2025-26**, much of it targeted towards women.

## Electoral “Freebies”

- Critics describe UCTs as electoral **“freebies.”**
- Financing them often requires **expenditure switching** or **larger fiscal deficits**, reducing resources for productive investments such as **employment generation** and **self-employment programmes**.
- Once households become dependent on UCTs, withdrawing them becomes difficult because political parties promote **competitive welfarism**.

## Targeting Challenges

- The fundamental challenge in targeted UCT programmes is **beneficiary identification**.
- Governments cannot directly observe the incomes of most informal-sector workers and therefore use proxy indicators such as **land ownership**, **electricity consumption**, and **household assets**.
- This inevitably creates:
    
    - **Inclusion errors** - benefits reaching ineligible households.
    - **Exclusion errors** - eligible households being left out.
- Whether real or perceived, these targeting errors can impose significant **political costs**.

## Kalaignar Magalir Urimai Thittam

- Before the **2021 election**, the ruling party promised **₹1,000 per month** to all **women-headed households**.
- Fiscal constraints led the government to restrict eligibility based on **income**, **land ownership**, and other criteria when the scheme began in **September 2023**.
- Initially, about **1.13 crore women** were covered.
- After widespread complaints from women who believed they met the criteria, another **16.94 lakh beneficiaries** were added in **December 2025**.
- The scheme cost **₹13,807 crore** in **2025-26**.
- Dissatisfaction continued despite the expansion. Women who felt unfairly excluded reportedly became more aggrieved when eligible beneficiaries received:
    
    - An **advance payment of three months’ entitlement**.
    - A **special summer relief payment**.
- Although its precise electoral impact cannot be measured, even modest changes in voter preferences can affect outcomes in **closely contested constituencies**.

## Other Experiences

- **Lakshmir Bhandar**, introduced in **2021** to provide monthly assistance to women, faced allegations of including **non-residential citizens**.
- Although its benefits were increased before the **2026 election**, the incumbent government lost power.
- Maharashtra’s **Mukhya Majhi Ladki Bahin Yojana** and Karnataka’s **Gruha Lakshmi Scheme** also faced **inclusion** and **exclusion errors**, respectively.

## Actual And Perceived Errors

- Political costs arise not only from **actual errors** but also from **perceived errors**.
- People who do not meet official eligibility criteria may still believe they have been treated unfairly.
- Households that legally qualify may be considered undeserving if they appear relatively affluent.
- Such **perceived targeting errors** can be as politically consequential as actual administrative mistakes.

## Economics And Politics

- There is an inherent tension between **economics** and **politics**:
    
    - Economics favours **targeted programmes** so scarce public resources reach those most in need.
    - Politics rewards **broader inclusion**, because voters judge governments not only by benefits received but also by benefits they believe were unfairly denied.
- Therefore, targeted UCT programmes carry an unavoidable **political cost**.

## Conditional Cash Transfers And Other Incentive-Based Welfare Programmes

- **Conditional cash transfers** and other **incentive-based welfare programmes** offer a more sustainable alternative.
- Linking benefits to **socially desirable outcomes** produces broader developmental gains.
- Tamil Nadu’s **Midday Meal Scheme** illustrates this principle: because participation depends on **school enrolment**, **self-selection** reduces grievances.
- Welfare programmes linked to **education** or other desirable behaviours can achieve developmental objectives with lower political costs.
- In contrast, UCTs remain inherently vulnerable to **inclusion**, **exclusion**, and **perceived targeting errors**.
- These political costs should be recognised alongside UCTs’ **economic and social benefits** when designing future welfare policies.
