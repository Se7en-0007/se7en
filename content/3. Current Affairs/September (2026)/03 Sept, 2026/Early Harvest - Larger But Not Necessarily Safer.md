- The **25th round of talks between the Special Representatives** on the **India-China boundary question** was held in **Beijing on August 25, 2026**, between **Ajit Doval** and **Wang Yi**.
- The initial readouts did not mention **“early harvest”**, but the next day’s **“Eight Points of Outcomes and Consensus”** stated that:
    
    - The **Expert Group on Boundary Delimitation** and the **Working Group on Border Management**, established under the **Working Mechanism for Consultation and Coordination (WMCC)** a year earlier, would advance discussions on an **“Early and Substantial Harvest”** of **boundary delimitation** and **Border Management**.
    - Their first task is to agree on their **terms of reference**, indicating that the process remains at an **early stage**.

## **An Uncertain Early Harvest**

- **“Early and Substantial Harvest”** is an **Indian coinage**.
- Around **2019**, after Beijing proposed settlement of the **Sikkim Sector alone**, India counter-proposed an **“Early and Substantial Harvest”** comprising:
    
    - The **Sikkim Sector**.
    - The **entire Middle Sector**.
    - Boundary delineation along the **watershed**.
- **China rejected** this proposal.
- In later discussions, China reportedly accepted only:
    
    - The **Sikkim Sector**.
    - Some **undisputed areas of the Middle Sector**.
- This was **unacceptable to India**.
- The return of the exact Indian formulation in the joint document could imply:
    
    - **Beijing has accepted India’s terms**, which would require China to give up claims to approximately **2,450 sq km** in the **Middle Sector**, south of the watershed boundary.
    - These areas include **Barahoti, Giu-Kaurik, Nilang-Jadhang, Sangcha Malla, and Lapthal**.
    - Alternatively, it could amount to an **“agreement with holes”**: a nominal settlement across both sectors restricted to stretches where claims already coincide, while leaving **trijunctions** and **contested pockets** of the Middle Sector undefined.
- Such an arrangement would reproduce the asymmetry of a **Sikkim-only settlement**:
    
    - India would concede where its position is strong.
    - There would be no progress in the **Eastern** and **Western Sectors**.
    - China would retain its hardline formulation of **“dong tiao xi rang”** - **meaningful adjustments by India in the East and corresponding concessions by China in the West**.
- It would also abandon **Article III** of the **2005 Agreement on Political Parameters and Guiding Principles for the Settlement of the India-China Boundary**, which envisages a **“package settlement” covering all sectors**.
- The package clause recognises that the boundary’s **four sectors are strategically interlinked** and require **cross-sector give and take**.

## **No Open-And-Shut Case**

- The **Sikkim Sector** is not an **open-and-shut case**.
- India and China agree that the **Anglo-Chinese Convention of 1890** forms the **“basis of the alignment”** of the boundary in this sector.
- However, **Article I** contains two mutually contradictory stipulations:
    
    - Its first sentence makes the **watershed crest** the boundary.
    - Its second sentence identifies **Mount Gipmochi** as the starting point on the Bhutan frontier.
- **British Indian maps of 1907 and 1913** placed the trijunction about **6.5 km north of Gipmochi**, on the true watershed crest.
- India and Bhutan interpret the **watershed principle** as controlling and locate the trijunction at **Batang La**.
- China relies on the literal reference to **Gipmochi** to shift the trijunction southwards to the **Jampheri Ridge**.
- A Sikkim delimitation based on the **1890 text**, unless it repudiates **Gipmochi**, would be regarded by Beijing as conceding the southern trijunction.
- Even a settlement reserving the trijunction would weaken India’s standing to advise Bhutan against a bilateral deal after India itself had settled an adjacent sector.
- China’s **“package proposal” to Bhutan** involves:
    
    - Relinquishing roughly **495 sq km** of claims in the north.
    - In exchange for Bhutan ceding around **269 sq km** in the northwest, including the **Doklam Plateau**.
- This could deepen China’s presence in the **Chumbi Valley** and provide access to the **Jampheri Ridge**.
- From the Jampheri Ridge, the entire **Siliguri Corridor** - India’s sole overland link to its northeastern States - would come under **direct observation**.
- Since the **2017 Doklam standoff**, China has built **roads, villages, and military facilities** across western Bhutan, gaining **de facto control over Doklam**.

## **A Genuine Early And Substantial Harvest**

- A genuine **“Early and Substantial Harvest”** requires:
    
    - **Delineation along the highest-watershed principle** across the entire stretch in both sectors, rather than selectively in areas where positions already converge.
    - Explicit exclusion of **trijunction points**, including **Batang La** and comparable Middle Sector points adjoining third countries, from the Expert Group’s mandate.
    - Settlement of such trijunctions only in consultation with the concerned country, as required by the **2012 Common Understanding** between the two Special Representatives.
    - Terms of reference that extend India’s **2019 proposal in good faith**, rather than dilute it.
- Without acceptance of the **highest-watershed principle**, silence on the trijunction could be filled on the ground in **Beijing’s favour in Bhutan**.

## **Unanswered Questions**

- China’s August 25 readout reiterated commitment to a **“package” settlement**, but its compatibility with a **piecemeal settlement approach** remains unclear.
- **Article X** of the **2005 Agreement** directs the Special Representatives to continue consultations to reach an **agreed framework for a boundary settlement**.
- This agreed framework is meant to provide the basis for later **delineation and demarcation** by civil and military officials and surveyors.
- Yet, there is **no agreed framework**, while an **Expert Group on Delimitation** is proposed.
- China uses **“delimitation”** interchangeably with **“demarcation”**, whereas the **2005 Agreement does not use the term “delimitation” at all**.
- The issue is whether Special Representatives will continue pursuing an agreed framework or leave it in abeyance while officials undertake a **partial delimitation exercise** without such a framework.
- Other elements of the **Eight Points** are useful but incremental **confidence-building measures**:
    
    - Additional meeting points and hotlines for **General Level Mechanisms** in the **Eastern and Middle Sectors**.
    - Continued **pilgrimage** and **border trade**.
    - A **September meeting** on **trans-border rivers**.
- These measures are on a different track from **boundary settlement** and should not be conflated with it.

## **Medog County Project**

- The Eight Points do not mention the **Medog County project** on the **Yarlung Tsangpo River**.
- They only refer to maintaining communication on **trans-border river issues**, including **hydrological data sharing** and renewal of relevant **MoUs**, without reporting progress.
- The September meeting should convey India’s concerns about:
    
    - The **world’s largest hydropower plant** being constructed near the border in the **Eastern Sector**.
    - Its location in a **highly earthquake-prone zone**.
    - The risk highlighted by the **flash floods in Nepal on August 26**.

## **Avoid Optics**

- The **2005 Agreement**, the only substantive outcome of **23 years of Special Representative talks**, must not be diluted.
- China has sought to **reinterpret and undermine** the agreement since its conclusion.
- Indian negotiators must avoid projecting **contrived progress**.
- **Diplomacy that trades long-term strategic interests for short-term optics is self-defeating.**
