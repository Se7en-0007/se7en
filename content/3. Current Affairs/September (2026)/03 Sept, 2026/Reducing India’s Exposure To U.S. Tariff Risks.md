- The **U.S. Senate** has passed the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia And Iran Act Of 2026**, which imposes sanctions and authorises additional tariffs and other restrictions related to **Russia**.
- A key provision authorises tariffs of up to **100%** on countries that are among the **five largest importers of Russian crude oil or natural gas** if they knowingly make new purchases after the Act’s enactment.
- The Act still awaits **House approval**, but its enactment could have serious implications for **India’s economic interests**.

## The Russian Oil Dilemma

- India has diversified its energy supplies to reduce its **crude import bill** and gain **strategic advantage** amid global uncertainty, resulting in a sharp rise in imports of **Russian crude**.
- Before the **Russia-Ukraine conflict**, Russian crude accounted for only **2%** of India’s imports; it now accounts for **roughly half**.
- In **2026**, imports nearly doubled from **4.54 MMT in January** to **8.96 MMT in May**.
- While this strategy has helped secure supplies, it has created diplomatic costs, particularly in managing relations with the **United States**, which seeks to strongly discourage these imports.
- On **July 24**, before the bill’s introduction, the U.S. imposed **forced-labour tariffs** on **60 countries**, including India, under **Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974**.
- India faced an additional **10% tariff**, replacing the expired **10% duty** under **Section 122**.
- If the Russia Sanctions Act becomes law, India’s cumulative tariff could reach **110%**, making it one of the most heavily tariffed countries.
- Such tariffs could undermine India’s **price competitiveness** in the U.S., a major export market, and lead to economic losses.
- **China’s cumulative tariff** could reach **112.5%**, as China and India are major importers of Russian crude.

## Trade Simulations And Results

- Two global trade simulations were conducted using the **GTAP dataset and model**, a global general equilibrium model that captures **global linkages** and **country-level shocks**.
- The **sanction scenario** models a **110% U.S. tariff on India**, with other countries facing forced-labour tariffs and China facing **112.5%** tariffs.
- The **diversification scenario** applies the same tariffs while India pursues export diversification, proxied by a full **India-European Union Free Trade Agreement (FTA)**.

### The Sanction Scenario

- Proposed U.S. sanctions could have adverse consequences for the Indian economy.
- India’s welfare declines by nearly **$47 billion**.
- **GDP, output, domestic demand, exports, and imports** all contract.
- Aggregate exports fall by **5.1%** and imports by **5.2%**, reflecting disrupted trade flows and weaker economic activity.
- A prolonged tariff-based confrontation with the U.S. could impose substantial costs on India’s growth and trade performance.

### The Diversification Scenario

- With a functional **India-EU FTA**, the economy turns around despite the same tariff environment.
- Welfare improves by **$26.3 billion** and **GDP turns positive**.
- Sectoral output and domestic demand recover by around **1%**.
- Aggregate exports increase by **3.1%**, while imports rise by a moderate **2.6%**.
- This indicates stronger production and trade integration with **alternative markets**.

## The Strategy To Pursue

- Even if India continues procuring crude from Russia to strengthen its **energy security**, the adverse economic effects of U.S. tariffs can be mitigated to a large extent through **export diversification**.
- The **India-EU FTA**, used as a proxy for diversification, shows that India must increasingly look beyond the U.S. and expand its presence in alternative markets, even though the U.S. remains one of India’s largest export destinations.
- Diversification is not a **panacea** because it depends on the capacity of other markets to absorb additional Indian exports.
- Without adequate **external demand**, diversification may remain limited.
- Export diversification must be complemented by sustained **domestic reforms**, including:
    
    - **Trade facilitation**
    - Removal of **non-tariff barriers**
    - Improved **logistics and standards**
    - Movement up the **goods quality ladder**
- This strategy can enhance India’s resilience to future **geopolitical shocks** and strengthen its long-term **export competitiveness**.
