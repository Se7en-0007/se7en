- **The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI), prompted by the Supreme Court, has proposed bold red front-of-pack warnings for packaged foods high in fat, salt, or sugar.**
- The warning would make it immediately clear that products such as **instant noodles, breakfast cereals, and “health drinks”** may not be as wholesome as their advertising claims.
- This is a **welcome but insufficient** step on its own.

## What They Are Being Sold

- The number of children presenting with **morbid obesity and diabetes** has risen sharply within a few years.
- According to the **World Obesity Atlas 2026**, **41 million Indian children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 are overweight or obese**.
- The issue is not merely that children are eating more, but **what they are being sold**.
- **Breakfast cereals, sweetened yoghurts, and “health drinks”** are marketed to parents as filling gaps in children’s diets, highlighting **“energy” and vitamins** while saying little about their sugar content.
- In **2024**, a leading multinational was found adding sugar to **infant food sold in India and other lower income countries**, while the same product in **Europe** contained no sugar.
- A popular health drink was found to be little more than **flavoured sugar syrup**; a **social media storm**, rather than a regulator, led the company to prescribe a **15% cut in added sugar**.

## Around Schools And Colleges

- Studies show that around **schools and colleges**, the cheapest and most easily available snacks are also the **least healthy**.
- A popular “energy drink” among teenagers, priced at **₹20**, contains close to **17 grams of sugar**, along with **caffeine and artificial colour**, in a single bottle.
- Its label says it is **not meant for children**, but nothing prevents a child from buying it.
- The market is **pricing unhealthy calories within the range of a child’s pocket money**.

## Need For Enforcement

- The central problem is **lack of enforcement**.
- The **FSSAI** and the **Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE)** have long recommended what schools should not sell, but optional rules are treated as optional and canteens stock whatever sells cheapest.
- A **red warning label** can work only if it is **actually enforced**.
- The proposed rule applies only to **organised retail**.
- Much of India’s sugar, salt, and trans fat is consumed unbranded through **street stalls, dhabas, and sweet shops** in the **unorganised sector**, which are not required to declare anything.
- A red label on a biscuit packet does nothing about a **jalebi sold loose beside it**.
- Regulation confined to packaged food leaves the **unregulated half of the plate untouched**.

## A Tax On Sugar

- India must address the issue it keeps avoiding - **a tax on sugar**.
- After the **U.K. introduced its soft drinks industry levy**, sugar consumption among both children and adults fell.
- Manufacturers reformulated drinks to fall below the tax threshold instead of raising prices.
- A well-designed sugar tax does not only discourage purchases; it also pushes industry to make products **less harmful**.

## India Already Taxes Sugary Drinks Heavily

- Since **September 2025**, aerated and sweetened beverages, including **sugar-free versions**, have been placed in a single **40% GST slab**.
- Thus, a normal cola and its **zero-sugar counterpart** pay the same tax, giving manufacturers **no incentive to reduce sugar**.
- The U.K.’s levy taxed drinks in **tiers based on sugar content**, allowing companies to reduce their tax burden by reformulating products.
- India could use a similar approach to give manufacturers a reason to **cut sugar**.

## The Standard Objection

- The concern that a sugar tax would affect the poor most is valid, but it is only **half the argument**.
- Cheap, unregulated sugar already imposes a heavy cost on poorer people, who bear the brunt of the **diabetes, hypertension, and childhood obesity epidemic** driven by sugar-heavy diets.
- They also have the least means to treat these diseases.
- Doing nothing is not neutral; it is a **slower, costlier tax paid in ill health rather than rupees at the till**.
- The tax should be **calibrated to sugar content** to encourage reformulation, and part of the revenue should be used to make **healthy food cheaper**.

## What India Needs Now

- The proposed **red label** is a genuine, though belated, first step.
- India needs:
- **Mandatory food standards**
- **Limits on marketing unhealthy food to children**
- **Rules that reach the unorganised sector**
- **An honest debate on taxing sugar** in a way that protects families with the least capacity to absorb either the cost of the tax or disease
