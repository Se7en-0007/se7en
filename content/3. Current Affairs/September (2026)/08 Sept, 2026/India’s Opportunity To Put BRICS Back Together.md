- **BRICS has moved far from its original purpose.** The **18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi on September 12-13, 2026** offers India an opportunity to restore coherence and direction to the expanded grouping so that it can effectively shape global events.
- BRICS was created to give **Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa** - emerging economies - a greater voice in **global governance**, especially in **financial and economic institutions**, and to pursue a **more equitable multilateral order**.
- India initially viewed BRICS as a vehicle for genuine reform of multilateral institutions. The **fourth BRICS Summit**, hosted by India in **2012**, centred on **“Global Stability, Security and Prosperity.”**
- India’s presidency helped establish the **BRICS Bank - the New Development Bank**. India also advanced initiatives such as integrating a **counter-terrorism architecture** into BRICS’s work.

## The Emergence Of The China Challenge

- China saw BRICS as a **counterpoise to western domination** and as a platform to amplify its global ambitions. In **2010**, BRICS countries accounted for **nearly 20% of world GDP**.
- China sought to use **BRICS-Plus** and **BRICS Outreach** to engage the **Global South** and consolidate its profile as a **second pole in a future bipolar world**.
- India consequently had to pursue its reform agenda while also acting as a **counterweight to China’s larger designs on BRICS**.
- India, Brazil and South Africa found China’s support for reform of multilateral institutions to be selective. China, for example, resisted supporting their bids for **permanent seats on the United Nations Security Council**.
- India therefore focused more on **BRICS consolidation** and **intra-BRICS matters** to resist Chinese grandstanding.
- India’s 2026 chairship has four pillars: **Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability**. It draws on the vision of **“Humanity First”** and a **“people-centric” approach to BRICS cooperation**.
- There was a danger that the **principle of consensus**, which had sustained BRICS cohesion, would be bypassed and that BRICS would resemble the **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation**, where a majority can bulldoze its views through.
- India and Brazil resisted this trend. The **2019 BRICS Summit in Brasilia** had **no invited guest countries**, involving only the **five original members**.
- In **2020**, under the Russian presidency, a **non-consensus chair’s statement on COVID-19** was issued, bypassing the principle of consensus.
- At the **BRICS Foreign Ministers’ meeting in New Delhi in May 2026**, a non-consensus outcome emerged because new members could not agree. Increasing use of non-consensus documents would make BRICS ineffective.

## Membership Expansion Tests BRICS Cohesion

- China pushed for expansion of the **New Development Bank** and then of BRICS itself. India and Brazil resisted, but BRICS has now expanded to **11 members** with the addition of **Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Indonesia**, along with **10 partner countries**.
- The new members have begun fighting among themselves. While the original five have serious differences, they have addressed them with maturity for the group’s greater good.
- The new members are subverting BRICS through **bilateral conflicts**. BRICS should not go the way of **SAARC**, where bilateral disputes have paralysed the grouping.
- India has tried to preserve BRICS as a **“non-West” group**, against pressure from some members to make it an **anti-West group**.
- Avoiding an anti-West tilt is becoming difficult because:
    
    - **China** is competing with the **United States** for global leadership.
    - **Russia** is at war with Ukraine with Europe’s full backing.
    - **Iran** is being bombarded by the **U.S. and Israel**.
    - **Brazil and India** have faced **punitive tariffs** by U.S. President **Donald Trump**.
    - The **U.S. Congress** is considering legislation allowing the President to impose punitive tariffs on countries importing **Russian oil**.
    - The **Quad** is being emasculated by the U.S., **India-U.S. relations** are under pressure, Pakistan is being courted by the U.S. at India’s expense, and global institutions are being made dysfunctional by the West.
- The more erratic the U.S. is vis-à-vis BRICS, the greater the chance that BRICS will move in the wrong direction.
- Many BRICS members share India’s effort to prevent an anti-Western drift because they seek engagement with multiple actors across different camps in their national interest. They seek **change**, not geopolitical realignment with **China and Russia**.

## Parallel Visions Of The World

- Both the **U.S. and China** are advancing parallel visions of the world and following their own rules instead of internationally negotiated rules.
- Both challenge existing international norms and global institutions while developing parallel structures and standards in:
    
    - **Artificial Intelligence**
    - **Digital and Internet governance**
    - **Data ownership**
    - **State control**
    - **5G/6G and telecom**
    - **Satellite navigation**
    - **Electric vehicles**
- Neither seeks reform of existing institutions.
- China has established the **World AI Cooperation Organisation in Shanghai**, alongside the **Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank**, the **Belt and Road Initiative**, and the **Digital Silk Road**. These challenge the **Bretton Woods institutions** and their governance and financing models.
- China sees BRICS as a vehicle to pursue these broader goals.
- The push for **de-dollarisation** through a parallel **BRICS currency** has received a **lukewarm response**. Many members are uncomfortable with a **renminbi-dominated currency** and prefer:
    
    - **Interlinking payment systems**
    - **Central bank digital currencies**
    - **National-currency transactions**
- Following a successful pilot project, China is formally launching **mBridge**, an alternative financial payment system. India does not want an alternative **Bretton Woods system dominated by China**.

## India Can Course Correct BRICS

- The **2026 Delhi Summit** presents an opportunity for course correction. India has worked to persuade new members to overcome their political differences.
- India must revive the original **raison d’être** of BRICS and shape the expanded grouping into a vehicle for **reform and stability**, while preventing others from hijacking it.
- At the **2018 BRICS Summit in South Africa**, the vision of **“reformed multilateralism”** was first articulated at the leaders’ retreat.
- By **2019**, this vision had entered the **BRICS Summit document in Brasilia**, pushed by **India, Brazil and South Africa**.
- BRICS should revive this agenda and become a strong voice for **“non-West” middle powers** and, by extension, the **Global South**.
- A **Global South middle power** is distinct from broadly West-centric middle powers. BRICS is the credible, though imperfect, organisation of Global South middle powers.
- China is hardly a middle power and has disproportionate influence within BRICS. India and China need greater synergy on emerging global issues while containing their bilateral differences.
- Some middle powers that ought to be represented are absent from BRICS. Yet, despite its imperfect composition, BRICS holds considerable **geopolitical and geo-economic potential**.
- As BRICS’s profile rises and more countries seek membership, India should proactively embrace the **BRICS reform agenda** to advance its larger interest of **multi-alignment**. Otherwise, others will take BRICS in a different direction.
