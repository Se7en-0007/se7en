## India’s Rejection

- The **Permanent Court of Arbitration**, adjudicating a dispute raised by **Pakistan** under the **Indus Waters Treaty**, passed **two more orders against India**.
- It ruled that India’s decision to keep the Treaty in **“abeyance”** was **“not permissible under the Treaty or other applicable rules of international law”**.
- The Court held that the Treaty, governing the sharing of waters of **six transboundary rivers** between India and Pakistan, remained **“fully in force”**, and that India continued to be bound by it.
- The Court also directed India to **halt construction work at the Ratle Hydroelectric Project** until the technical dispute is finally settled.
- India had anticipated these orders and has not participated in the arbitration process, which has been underway for **four years**.
- India maintains that the Court was constituted in violation of the Treaty because a **neutral expert** had already been appointed to adjudicate the same issue, while the Treaty does not permit **parallel resolution processes** for the same dispute.
- India therefore considers the Court to lack legitimacy, refuses to recognise its existence or proceedings, and did not appoint its **two arbitrators** to the Court.
- Nevertheless, the Court proceeded with **two arbitrators appointed by Pakistan** and **three “umpires”** nominated by institutions named in the Treaty.
- India stated that the “so called Court” was constituted by the **World Bank** in a **“patent breach”** of the Treaty, rejected its “so-called award”, and reiterated that its decision to keep the Treaty in **abeyance remains in force**.
- The latest rulings were the **fourth “award”** of the Court of Arbitration, with all awards going in Pakistan’s favour.
- The Court was constituted in **September 2022**. In earlier awards, it declared itself competent to adjudicate the dispute, rejected India’s objections conveyed to the World Bank in **December 2022**, and held that its decisions were **final and binding**.
- India rejected all earlier awards, including the most recent one in **May**.
- With the latest order, the Court has likely completed its work by addressing all issues raised by Pakistan.
- The Court has **no means to enforce its orders**. Therefore, given India’s position, the orders are not expected to materially alter the prevailing situation.
- For Pakistan, however, the orders are valuable for raising the matter at relevant international forums, building domestic hysteria against India, and exploring further international legal options.

## Road Ahead For Pakistan

- Pakistan’s best-case scenario would be a **UN Security Council resolution against India**, framing the Treaty dispute as a **security issue**.
- Pakistan is likely to pursue this during the remaining **four months** of its tenure as a **non-permanent member** of the Security Council.
- Another option would be to seek a reference of the matter by the **UN Security Council** or the **UN General Assembly** to the **International Court of Justice**.
- These scenarios are described as **far-fetched** and unlikely to unfold as Pakistan wishes because India has greater clout at the UN and can get such moves scuttled.
- Pakistan’s other option is to construct a narrative portraying India as a country that does not respect international law or comply with treaty provisions.
- Pakistan is already pursuing this narrative and these efforts are likely to intensify.
- India has chosen to absorb the repercussions, build a diplomatic counter-narrative, and prepare for a legal battle if required.
- India believes it has a legal case and is confident of defending its decision to place the Treaty in abeyance.

## Road Ahead For India

- India’s principal focus is the **expeditious completion** of projects enabling full use of its rights over the three western rivers - **Indus, Jhelum and Chenab** - allocated to Pakistan under the Treaty.
- The Treaty permits India only **limited use** of these waters, but Pakistan’s repeated objections to Indian projects have prevented this.
- India is pursuing **eight infrastructure projects** on these rivers in **Jammu and Kashmir**, collectively worth **Rs 50,000 crore**.
- At least one project, which seeks to divert water from the **Chenab** to the **Beas**, goes beyond Treaty provisions because the Treaty does not allow **inter-basin transfer of waters**.
- For India, the Treaty is effectively **“as good as dead”**.
- India uses the term **“abeyance”** deliberately because the Treaty contains no provision allowing unilateral termination or suspension, although India considers that the Treaty does not exist.
- India disengaged last year from the **neutral expert process** that had been established at its own request.
- India continues to share **hydrological data** with Pakistan, but only through its **High Commission in Islamabad**, not through the **Permanent Indus Commission** established under the Treaty.
- Continuing the neutral-expert process or using the Permanent Indus Commission could be seen as relying on Treaty provisions, contradicting India’s position that the Treaty does not exist.
- The likelihood of the Treaty being revived in its original form is **next to none**.
- India has said the Treaty will remain in abeyance while Pakistan continues to support **cross-border terrorism**.
- India has suggested renegotiating the Treaty, which Pakistan is likely to reject because it is unlikely to secure a deal as favourable as the existing one.
- India is also likely to insist on a strictly **bilateral agreement**, without a third party such as the **World Bank**, which Pakistan is unlikely to accept.

## Sharing Without A Treaty

- The treaty deadlock appears **unresolvable in the foreseeable future**.
- The transboundary rivers between India and Pakistan are likely to continue flowing without a formal water-sharing agreement.
- This is not unique: there are **hundreds of instances** worldwide of transboundary rivers without a water-sharing agreement.
- India shares rivers with **China, Bhutan, Nepal, Bangladesh and Myanmar**, and most of these rivers have no formal water-sharing arrangement.
- Keeping the Treaty in abeyance does **not** mean India has stopped river-water flows to Pakistan.
- India cannot stop these flows even if it wanted to, and Pakistan continues to receive water as it did earlier.
- Pakistan would continue to receive river waters even after India completes all projects on the western rivers.
- Despite declining per-capita water availability due to **population pressures** and **climate change**, the **Indus river system** still has sufficient water to meet the needs of people living in its basin in India and Pakistan.
- Pakistan’s water problem is attributed not to India stopping or disrupting flows, but to **inefficient water-management practices**.
- The uncertainty created by keeping the Treaty in abeyance could force Pakistan to adopt better practices, create storage facilities, and use water more judiciously.
