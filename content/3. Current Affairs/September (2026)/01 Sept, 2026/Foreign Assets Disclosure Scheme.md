- The **Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers - Disclosure Scheme (FAST-DS)** was **launched on August 16** to address practical issues faced by **students, young professionals, tech employees, relocated NRIs**, and other small taxpayers.
- Concerns have arisen because individuals face **high fees and penalties** for participation, including where there has been **no actual gain** from the foreign asset.
- The problem is especially acute for salaried employees who received **restricted stock units (RSUs)** or **employee stock ownership plans (ESOPs)**, included them in salary taxation, but did not separately report them in **Schedule FA (foreign assets)** of the income tax return.

## Instances Of Non-Disclosure

- A salaried individual invested **Rs 90,000** in **US-listed stocks** that are now trading at a loss. Although the overseas remittance appeared in the person’s **Annual Information Statement (AIS)**, it was not separately disclosed in **Schedule FA**. Under FAST-DS, the individual must pay **Rs 1 lakh upfront** to disclose the loss-making investment.
- An employee of a foreign company operating in India had **ESOP vesting details** included as salary but did not declare them separately in the foreign-assets schedule. The employee must pay **Rs 1 lakh** merely as disclosure charges.
- Tax officials state that including **ESOPs/RSUs in salary taxation** does not itself amount to disclosure of the foreign asset in the prescribed **Schedule FA**.
- FAST-DS applies irrespective of whether the undisclosed foreign asset is currently in **profit or loss** because the scheme addresses **non-disclosure of the asset itself**, not whether income or gain has arisen from it.

## Change In Status

- Experts say disclosure schemes must account for a taxpayer’s change from **non-resident** to **Indian resident**.
- Many employees received ESOPs from global companies while they were **NRIs**, were subsequently deputed to India, and became Indian residents. Their earlier foreign-asset details may not have been disclosed, leading to questions about how they acquired those assets once they disclose them.
- Experts also flag cases in which people who have been permanent residents of countries such as **Singapore for decades** receive tax notices for bank accounts opened there during their non-resident status.

## ESOPs And India’s Net Outflows

- **ESOPs** are an important salary component, particularly in the **technology sector, startups, and foreign companies**.
- Under the Balance of Payments category, **“financial derivatives (other than reserves) and employee stock options”**, net outflows stood at **just under $24 billion in 2025-26**, up **8%** from around **$22 billion in 2024-25**.
- The figure in **2024-25** had almost tripled from nearly **$8 billion in 2023-24**.
- Reserve Bank of India BoP data does **not provide a separate breakup** for flows relating to **ESOPs** and **financial derivatives**.

## Two Broad Categories

- FAST-DS applies to:
    
    - **Undisclosed and never-before-taxed foreign income or assets located outside India up to Rs 1 crore**.
    - **Foreign assets up to Rs 5 crore** that were already offered to tax or acquired when the assessee was a non-resident, but were not declared in the relevant income-tax-return schedule.
- Under the first category, the assessee must pay:
    
    - **30% of the fair market value (FMV)** of assets or **30% of undisclosed income** as tax.
    - An additional **30% as income tax in lieu of penalty**.
- Under the second category, the disclosure fee is a flat **Rs 1 lakh**.

## Disproportionate Penalty

- Experts argue that the **Rs 1 lakh** fee can be disproportionate when the undisclosed amount is insignificant.
- In the example of US-listed stocks purchased for less than **Rs 1 lakh**, the fixed disclosure fee can erase the entire investment.
- Experts emphasise **materiality** - the maximum error in financial statements that can be allowed before they are considered wrong.
- A taxpayer may have sold shares but retained a small fraction of shares that was not disclosed. Experts argue that authorities should assess the materiality of such omissions, including cases where only a small part of a disclosed holding remains unreported.
- Critics question whether a combined **30% tax plus 30% penalty**, amounting to **60%**, is too high. They argue that the fixed levy particularly affects salaried employees, who are described as the most honest taxpayers because taxes are deducted by default rather than choice.
- They contend that enforcement should focus on **big defaulters and evaders**.

## Updated Return And Scrutiny

- Some employees are considering filing an **updated return** to correct such anomalies rather than using FAST-DS, particularly where the asset value is low and concerns about greater scrutiny remain.
- Tax officials state that the **Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act** does not recognise updated returns for foreign income that was never previously taxed or disclosed.
- Therefore, even after filing an updated return, the taxpayer would remain liable to tax and penalty under the **Black Money Act**.
- Officials say disclosure under **FAST-DS** is therefore preferable and maintain that there is **no intention of more scrutiny** for declarations made under the scheme.

## Fixed Date Of Valuation

- The valuation date is fixed as **March 31, 2026**, irrespective of changes in the **exchange rate** or the value of assets.
- The FMV of assets declared under the scheme must be calculated as on this date.
- FMV will be the higher of:
    
    - **Cost of acquisition**.
    - The price the asset would ordinarily fetch if sold in the open market on the valuation date.
- The market valuation should ideally be supported by a report from a valuer recognised by the government, or its agency, of the country where the asset is located.
- If no market valuation is undertaken, the **indexed cost of acquisition** will be treated as the FMV.

## Tax Officials’ Position

- Tax officials distinguish between **taxation of income** and **reporting of foreign assets**, treating them as separate compliance requirements.
- The scheme covers foreign assets up to an aggregate value of **Rs 5 crore**, while imposing a uniform **Rs 1 lakh** fee.
- Officials describe the charge as a fixed **regularisation fee**, not a penalty linked to the asset’s value or the tax payable.
- The fee is intended as a simple and uniform cost for regularising past non-disclosure, regardless of whether the asset has **appreciated or depreciated**.
- Officials argue that the **Rs 1 lakh** fee should be seen against the **Rs 10 lakh penalty** that non-disclosure would otherwise have attracted without the scheme.

## Suggested Solutions

- Experts suggest a **discrepancy threshold** below which the Income Tax Department should not issue notices.
- One proposed approach is that if a taxpayer holds an overseas asset of **$100,000**, a discrepancy up to **$100,000** should not trigger a notice.
- Such a threshold could make disclosures almost voluntary while ensuring that the Department retains records of overseas assets.
- Experts also suggest maintaining an **updated database of NRIs** so that the Tax Department can appropriately identify persons who remain non-residents and avoid issuing notices for foreign accounts opened during their non-resident status.
