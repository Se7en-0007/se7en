- The **U.S.-Canada trade dispute** offers **sharp lessons for India**.

## The U.S.-Canada Economic Relationship

- The **U.S. and Canada** have been deeply economically integrated since at least **1965**, when they established **free trade in automobiles and their parts**.
- This widened into a **full-fledged free trade agreement in 1989** and was expanded into the **North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA)** about **five years later**.
- Their economic integration has **steadily continued** and has, by all accounts, **worked well for both economies**.
- For the much smaller Canadian economy, the objective was to achieve **economies of scale** by producing **vast amounts of a few products**.
- Canada accounts for **70% of the oil refined in the American Midwest** and supplies **60% of the nation’s aluminium**.
- Canada also supplies **nearly all the types of lumber used in U.S. residential construction**.

## A New Low

- Despite this **long and fruitful trade partnership**, the economic relationship has fallen to a **new low**.
- Canada withdrew from negotiations over a **new tariff deal**, citing **last-minute insertions by the U.S. side**.
- The U.S. has made a **similar allegation** against Canada.
- Canada imposed **reciprocal tariffs of up to 50%** on the U.S. in response to the **50% tariffs** imposed by the U.S. on imports from Canada.
- From **September 29**, the U.S. will **outright ban** certain **Canadian alcoholic spirits**, some **dairy goods**, and **motorcycles**.
- Statements by **U.S. President Donald Trump** are **hardly conciliatory in nature**.

## The First Lesson For India

- If the U.S. treats a **next-door neighbour**, **alliance member**, and **long-standing trade partner** in this manner, India should not take for granted any **favourable treatment** it might receive from the U.S.

## The Second Lesson

- **Barrelling ahead with trade deals** might not always be the best plan.
- Before Canada, **Malaysia** also backed out of a deal it had signed with the U.S.
- Malaysia argued that once the U.S.’s **reciprocal tariff system** was deemed **illegal**, the benefit from a trade deal no longer outweighed the costs of **opening up to the U.S.**
- Despite the success of its recent trade pacts, India should weigh the same **pros and cons** in dealing with the U.S., especially because a deal might not end **tariff pressure**.

## India Has Faced This Before

- Despite the **February 2026 agreement** of **18% tariffs on imports from India**, the U.S. has continued with its **forced labour** and **excess capacity investigations**.
- These investigations could result in tariff levels **exceeding the 18% limit**.
- India has done well to insist that a deal will not be struck until its **advantage over its competitors is clear**.

## The Third Lesson

- This **advantage can vanish even after a deal is struck**.
