## At The Pinnacle Of Its Power

- At the time of the **September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks**, the **United States** was at the **pinnacle of its power**.
- A decade earlier, the **Soviet Union** had disintegrated; **Russia** was struggling to emerge from **post-Soviet chaos**, while **China** remained a close economic partner of the U.S.
- In **eastern Europe**, Washington had undertaken military interventions and **NATO** had completed its first round of expansion into **Russia’s neighbourhood**.
- The U.S. maintained a formidable military presence in **West Asia**.
- As the world’s **sole superpower**, the U.S. launched a **global war on terrorism**, first targeting **Taliban-run Afghanistan**, which had hosted **al-Qaeda**, the perpetrators of the 9/11 attacks.
- The war went far beyond fighting terrorism. **Twenty-five years later**, the U.S. remains bogged down in West Asia, its rivals are more assertive elsewhere, and terrorism is more dispersed across **West and South Asia and Africa**.

## Regime-Change Wars

- Though conceived as a transborder fight against terrorist organisations, primarily **al-Qaeda**, the war evolved into a series of **regime-change wars**.
- These wars sought to reshape **Afghanistan, West Asia and North Africa** according to American interests and worldview.
- **George W. Bush** and his neoconservative allies believed that a **“deficit of democracy”** bred terrorism; spreading democracy therefore became a theoretical imperative of the war on terror.
- In Afghanistan, U.S. forces toppled the Taliban regime and established the **Islamic Republic**.
- The war forced al-Qaeda to disperse into autonomous cells and retreat, but did **not defeat** the organisation.

## Three Consequences

- In less than **two years**, the U.S. invaded **Iraq**. Besides its disastrous humanitarian cost, the invasion had three lasting consequences.
- **First**, it diverted U.S. attention from Afghanistan and al-Qaeda, enabling the Taliban to regroup and establish an insurgency that eventually defeated the U.S. **20 years after the 9/11 attacks**.
- **Second**, the destruction of the Iraqi state and ensuing chaos created a new haven for al-Qaeda.
- Before 9/11, Afghanistan hosted **Osama bin Laden’s al-Qaeda**. After the Iraq war, **Abu Musab al-Zarqawi** built the deadliest branch of the jihadist organisation in Iraq.
- **Al-Qaeda in Iraq (AQI)** survived Zarqawi’s death in a U.S. airstrike in **2006** and transformed into the **Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS)** in the early **2010s**, after Syria also descended into civil war.
- **Third**, the collapse of the **Saddam regime** removed a historical buffer between Iran and the U.S.-backed **Persian Gulf monarchies**.
- Saddam had fought Iran for **eight years in the 1980s** and, despite later becoming an outcast in the U.S.-Sunni axis, remained a staunch enemy of Tehran.
- After Saddam’s fall, Iraq’s Shia movements, historically tied to Tehran and suppressed under Saddam, became the new centres of power in Baghdad.
- **Iran** steadily expanded its influence in Iraq, strengthening its regional position while trapping the U.S. in Iraq’s morass.

## Cutting The Losses, Geopolitical Chain Effects

- In **2011**, amid the **Arab Spring protests**, NATO launched an extensive bombing campaign in Libya to protect the Libyan people from **Muammar Qaddafi’s forces**.
- The war, which Barack Obama said he had **“led from behind”**, brought down the Qaddafi regime but destabilised Libya, once among Africa’s most stable states.
- The collapse of the Libyan state opened the floodgates to terrorism across Africa as weapons were smuggled across borders to militias and terrorist groups.
- Over the past **15 years**, jihadist groups spread their influence across the **Sahel**, while western influence diminished on the continent.
- By the end of the Obama presidency, Iraq was unstable with growing Iranian influence; the Taliban were strengthening in Afghanistan; Libya was split between rival governments and armed militias; and Russian intervention in Syria had, though briefly, turned the tide towards the **Assad regime**.
- While the U.S. struggled with wars in Afghanistan, West Asia and North Africa, China, its key systemic rival, continued rising economically and militarily.
- The U.S. began cutting its losses and repositioning itself across the wider region.

## The U.S. Withdrawal From Afghanistan

- The first move was to accept that the U.S. had lost the war in Afghanistan, its first post-9/11 regime-change war.
- Donald Trump’s first administration began direct talks with the Taliban, and in **February 2020**, the U.S. signed an agreement committing to withdraw American troops from Afghanistan.
- On **August 15, 2021**, two weeks before the deadline, the Taliban entered **Kabul**.
- The post-9/11 paradox is that America emerged from the Cold War as the world’s unrivalled power, but attempts to consolidate primacy through military interventions eroded the strategic freedom provided by unipolarity.
- Washington regarded withdrawal from Afghanistan as swallowing an inevitable bitter pill: it had to trim strategic overreach and prepare for the looming contest with China.

## Geopolitical Butterfly Effect

- The pullback from Afghanistan was interpreted as weakness by rivals, especially Russia, which had long sought to alter the **post-Soviet security architecture** in its rimland.
- In **February 2022**, less than a year after the U.S. withdrawal, Russia invaded Ukraine, testing the U.S. commitment to Europe.
- The **October 7, 2023 Hamas attack on Israel** shook all of West Asia.
- Israel announced its own **“war on terror”**, launched a full-scale attack on Palestinians in Gaza, and extended it into a mini-regional war.
- This chain of developments led to the **February 2026 U.S.-Israeli bombing of Iran**.
- America’s failure to learn from failed regime-change wars, inability to strategically decouple from Israel, hubris, and refusal to accept that the world has changed contributed to this folly.
- The **American-Israel axis** sought to topple the **Islamic Republic** and reshape the region, but the war has trapped the U.S. in another West Asian quicksand.

## The Changing Global Order

- **Twenty-five years after 9/11**, terrorist groups have survived the war on terror and evolved into localised tentacles of a transnational jihadist project spanning multiple geographies.
- Despite U.S. commitment to Europe through NATO, a widening chasm has emerged in the **transatlantic alliance**.
- Traditional U.S. allies are increasingly frustrated with Washington’s newfound **transactionalism**.
- Israel, America’s closest ally in West Asia, is dragging Washington deeper into a regional vortex.
- While the U.S. remains bogged down in West Asia, China has emerged as the **second pole** of an increasingly fluid **multipolar order**.
- Russia is violently challenging the U.S.-led security architecture in Europe.
- The U.S., as indicated by its ill-conceived Iran war, continues to act as a **unilateral hegemon**.
- Its unilateralism is increasingly at odds with the emerging realities of the **post-unipolar world**.
