- **BRICS has promised an alternative to the western-dominated global financial order** through a **new bank rivaling the World Bank**, a **reserve fund independent of the International Monetary Fund (IMF)**, and a move away from **U.S. dollar dominance**.
- **Seventeen years into the BRICS project, this has not materialised.**
- The central reason is structural: **BRICS countries remain deeply enmeshed in the system they claim to challenge**, and their institutions reflect this reality.

## The Same Bank, Different Nameplate

- The **New Development Bank (NDB)**, launched in **2015**, was BRICS’s flagship institution, intended to provide developing countries:
    
    - **Loans without political strings**
    - **Governance without western dominance**
    - **Financing in local currencies rather than dollars**
- A decade later, **half of the NDB’s outstanding bonds are denominated in U.S. dollars**, while the **Chinese yuan makes up most of the rest**.
- The **South African rand accounts for only 1%**.
- The NDB leadership had set a target for **local-currency lending to reach 30% of its portfolio by the end of 2025**, but it stood at **roughly 22% by mid-2025**.
- The NDB’s **first rupee-denominated bond** was still at the planning stage in **September 2025**, despite the bank having operated for a decade.
- The NDB relies on **S&P, Fitch and Moody’s**, the same western credit-rating agencies that BRICS governments criticise as biased against developing countries.
- In **March 2022**, shortly after Russia invaded Ukraine, the NDB **froze all Russia-related operations** to protect its credit standing in New York.
- Russia is a **founding member** and holds a **20% shareholding** in the NDB.
- Russia subsequently **relinquished its turn at the NDB’s rotating presidency**, extending the Brazilian incumbent’s term until **2030**.
- This indicates that the NDB does not operate outside **western financial discipline**.
- By the end of **2024**, the NDB’s total project approvals had reached **$39 billion**.
- The **World Bank Group commits roughly $100 billion every year**.
- Thus, in a full decade, the NDB approved **less than what the World Bank Group disburses in six months**.
- The NDB also **co-finances projects with the World Bank and IMF**, making the relationship **complementary rather than competitive**.
- Although this may be smart banking, it falls far short of the NDB’s founding rhetoric of being a genuine alternative.

## The Safety Net Nobody Uses

- BRICS established the **Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA)** in **2015**.
- The CRA is a **$100 billion pool of foreign-exchange reserves** intended to help member countries manage financial crises without turning to the IMF.
- In principle, it was the most radical BRICS proposal because it could have weakened the IMF’s monopoly on emergency lending to the **Global South**.
- In practice, the CRA has **never been activated, not once in a decade**.
- A member country seeking to draw **more than 30% of its allotted share** must first enter an **IMF programme**.
- Therefore, the CRA’s escape route leads back to the institution it was meant to escape.
- The CRA has:
    
    - **No permanent staff**
    - **No independent surveillance capacity**
    - **No research wing**
- It is effectively a **promissory note dressed up as an institution**, unable under its own rules to function independently of the IMF.

## De-Dollarisation

- **De-dollarisation** is the BRICS idea that most captures public imagination: reducing reliance on the **U.S. dollar** and shifting global trade towards **local currencies**.
- Media coverage of BRICS summits amplifies this promise, but the reality is more limited.
- The **126-point declaration** issued at the **Rio summit in July 2025**, BRICS’s most comprehensive statement to date, **does not use the word “de-dollarisation” even once**.
- In **November 2024**, **Russian President Vladimir Putin** stated that BRICS had **“not sought to abandon the dollar”**.
- **India opposes a common BRICS currency**, fearing trade reprisals from Washington.
- **South Africa considers the idea too risky**.
- **China prefers the gradual internationalisation of the yuan on its own terms**.
- When **U.S. President Donald Trump** threatened a **10% tariff surcharge** on countries aligning with “anti-American” BRICS policies, **the bloc gave no collective response**.
- This showed how little it takes to deter BRICS even from the appearance of challenging the dollar system.

## IMF Reform

- At the **Kazan summit in 2024** and the **Rio summit in 2025**, BRICS declarations called for a **“quota-based and adequately resourced” IMF**.
- BRICS is therefore **not calling for the IMF to be replaced, restructured, or fundamentally reformed**.
- Instead, it seeks **a bigger say within the IMF**.
- The **United States holds 16.49% of IMF voting rights**.
- Major IMF decisions require an **85% supermajority**.
- This gives Washington an **effective veto over every significant IMF decision**.
- Despite knowing this for decades, BRICS countries continue to seek more representation within a system whose rules ensure that the final outcome is determined by someone else.

## What This Tells Us About BRICS

- Defenders point out that the NDB has financed real infrastructure and that its expansion to include **Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the United Arab Emirates** reflects a genuine Global South desire for alternatives to western-led institutions.
- The BRICS agenda is driven by frustration with:
    
    - **IMF conditionality**
    - **Dollar hegemony**
    - A financial system designed in **1944** by and for the **industrialised West**
- However, the gap between rhetoric and record remains significant.
- **BRICS countries have not built an alternative financial architecture.**
- Instead, they have built institutions operating within the existing system by:
    
    - **Lending in dollars**
    - **Deferring to western rating agencies**
    - **Designing safety nets that require IMF approval**
    - **Pledging loyalty to governance structures they publicly denounce**
- The key question is not whether BRICS can reform the global financial order, but whether its governments have any material interest in doing so, or whether securing a **better seat at the existing table** has always been the real objective.
