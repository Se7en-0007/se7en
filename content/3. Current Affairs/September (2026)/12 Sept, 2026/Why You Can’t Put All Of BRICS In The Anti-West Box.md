- **India’s challenge at the BRICS summit in Delhi** is two-fold: to convey the forum’s shared objective of **democratising the international system** and to dispel the impression that it seeks to **overthrow the US-led order**.
- BRICS is commonly viewed through an **anti-Western lens**. Supporters portray it as a vehicle for resisting **Western dominance**, while critics see it as a **China- and Russia-led counter-bloc**.
- Both readings are **simplistic** because BRICS members have different national aims: **accommodation with the West, autonomy from it, parity within it, or direct confrontation**.

## India And Brazil

- Within BRICS, **India and Brazil** share a strong instinct against turning the grouping into an **anti-West alliance**.
- They do not seek to undermine an international system in which they want a **larger role**, **more trade**, **access to technology and investment**, and **greater diplomatic standing**.

## A Spectrum Of Approaches

- The political orientation of BRICS towards the West is best understood as a **spectrum of approaches**, rather than a single anti-Western position.
- **Iran** is closest to the **anti-Western pole** because resistance to **US power** is central to its **state ideology** and recent history.
- At the other end are countries with deep **economic, security and institutional ties with the West**, even while seeking greater autonomy.
- **Russia and China** lie between these extremes. Both are in **strategic competition with the West**, but both also seek **status, influence and a seat at the Western high table**.

## Brazil Offers The Clearest Challenge

- **Brazil** offers the clearest challenge to the easy opposition between **“the West”** and **“the Global South.”**
- Brazil is **Latin America’s largest power** and a strong advocate of reforming global institutions.
- Yet, in broad historical and political terms, Brazil is also part of **the West**: its **geography, language, religion, legal tradition, political institutions and elite reference points** are rooted in the **Iberian and Euro-Atlantic worlds**.
- It is a **constitutional democracy** whose diplomatic tradition privileges **international law and multilateralism**.
- Brazil is not seeking to overthrow the West from outside. It is a **Western power dissatisfied with how authority is distributed within the order it inhabits**.

## Non-Western Is Not Anti-Western

- **Being non-Western is not synonymous with anti-Western.**
- The newer members further weaken the anti-Western image of BRICS:
- **Egypt** remains tied to **Washington on security**.
- The **UAE** has extensive **commercial and strategic links** with the **US and Europe**.
- **Indonesia’s “free and active” diplomacy** is intended to preserve manoeuvring room among major powers.
- These members seek **autonomy**, closer ties with **China**, and a stronger **Global South voice**, but not membership in a unified anti-Western camp.

## The Anti-Western Reading

- The anti-Western reading of BRICS is **wrong as description** but **useful as politics**, which explains its persistence.
- It allows a critic in **Washington** to treat different national calculations as one bloc, and allows a member at the confrontational end to claim the collective weight of the other members.
- The forum has **no rule** that prevents either use.

## For India

- For India, BRICS is useful precisely because of its **diversity**.
- BRICS would cease to serve Indian interests if it became a **China-led alliance against the West**.
- Delhi’s political burden is to ensure that BRICS does not conflate **autonomy from America** with **antagonism towards it**.
- India needs to strengthen the perception of BRICS as a forum seeking **international reform**, rather than a **rupture with the West**.
