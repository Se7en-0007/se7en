- Several **ISRO employee associations** questioned the private sector taking over the space agency’s **core processes** after **IN-SPACe Chairperson Pawan Goenka** stated that ISRO would eventually stop manufacturing **all launch vehicles** and operating the **spaceport under development**.
- **IN-SPACe** - the **Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre** - is an **independent and autonomous nodal agency** under the **Department of Space**. It acts as an interface between **ISRO** and **non-governmental entities (NGEs)** and assesses how India’s space resources can be better utilised.
- ISRO has already begun transferring several key technologies to industry. **V Narayanan** maintained that this shift is meant to **expand India’s space sector**, **not privatise ISRO itself**.

## What Does The Space Policy Say?

- After the space sector was opened up and **IN-SPACe** was established in **2020**, the **Indian Space Policy - 2023** was drafted to define the roles of **ISRO, IN-SPACe, NewSpace India Limited (NSIL), and private entities**.

### ISRO

- ISRO was to adopt a **research-oriented role**:
    
    - Develop newer applications and systems.
    - Share technologies with industry.
    - Store and provide satellite data.
    - **Demonstrate human spaceflight capability** and develop a **long-term road map for sustained human presence in space**.
- ISRO was to develop newer technologies but **transition out from the existing practice of being present in the manufacturing of operational space systems**.

### IN-SPACe

- IN-SPACe was meant to:
    
    - Promote private space activities and hand-hold industries.
    - Create an enabling environment for private participation.
    - Authorise activities while considering **national security** and **international and foreign policy obligations**.
- Its authorisation role covers:
    
    - Establishment of satellites.
    - Launch services.
    - Setting up launch pads.
    - Establishment of ground stations.
    - Proper use of orbital resources.
- It was also expected to ensure access to government testing and other facilities, transfer technologies, build **industry-academia bridges**, and create **manufacturing hubs**.

### NSIL

- **NSIL**, a public sector undertaking under the Department of Space, is **ISRO’s commercial arm**.
- It was to commercialise publicly developed space technologies and service space-based needs of users, creating some overlap with IN-SPACe’s role.
- NSIL now mainly provides **commercial launches** to Indian and international clients.

### Private Entities

- **NGEs** are allowed to undertake **end-to-end activities** in the space sector through establishment and operation of:
    
    - Space objects.
    - Ground-based assets.
    - Services such as **communication, remote sensing, and navigation**.

## What Key Technologies And Processes Are Being Transferred?

### SSLV

- The **Small Satellite Launch Vehicle (SSLV)** was operationalised in **2024**, following **three development flights**.
- It was developed with industry in mind:
    
    - Can be assembled in **three days** by **six people**.
    - Costs around **Rs 30 crore**.
    - In comparison, the **PSLV** takes **70 days**, requires **60 people**, and costs **Rs 100 crore**.
- In **2025**, **Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL)** won the bid for **Rs 511 crore**.
- HAL will make at least **two SSLVs end-to-end** under ISRO procedures during a **two-year technology-transfer phase**.
- After **August 2027**, HAL can modify the vehicle’s design and suppliers.
- After the two-year period, it can manufacture and commercialise SSLV independently, aiming for **six to 12 launches a year**.
- During the technology transfer, **NSIL plans to conduct at least 15 launches**.

### PSLV

- In **2022**, the **PSLV** became the first launch vehicle to be commercialised.
- A consortium of **HAL and Larsen & Toubro (L&T)** received a contract to manufacture **five PSLVs** for ISRO.
- ISRO later issued an **expression of interest (EoI)** for PSLV technology transfer:
    
    - ISRO will hand-hold the selected company for **36 months** or until the **end-to-end manufacture of two launch vehicles**.
    - After this period, the company will be free to manufacture and offer launches.
- The first of the five launch vehicles made by the HAL-L&T consortium is likely to be launched **later this year or early next year**.

### LVM3

- ISRO began commercialising **LVM3**, its heaviest launch vehicle, in **2024**, but the bid was not awarded.
- The initial plan was for NSIL to support industry for **two years**, after which industry would manufacture **four to six LVM3 rockets annually** for the next **12 years**.
- A new **EoI** was issued in **2026**:
    
    - ISRO will support the selected industry partner for **42 months** or until at least **two vehicles** are realised.
    - Thereafter, the industry will be able to create its own market.

### Satellite Launch Complex In Kulsekarapattinam

- IN-SPACe called for an EoI to run the upcoming spaceport for **small satellite launches** at **Kulsekarapattinam**, in Tamil Nadu’s **Thoothukudi district**.
- IN-SPACe, through ISRO, will facilitate handing over the **Satellite Launch Complex (SLC)** facilities to the selected party for **operation and management**.
- ISRO will provide technical guidance, operational support, training, and hand-holding for **one year** or until completion of **three launches**.
- ISRO has also transferred technologies for several other **launch vehicles, satellite systems, and subsystems**.

## Why Is This Being Done?

- Opening the sector to private players aims to expand **India’s share in the global space economy**.
- Although India is among the foremost space-faring countries, its current global space-economy share is **less than 2%**, because ISRO’s main focus was on **national priorities** rather than commercial services.
- The private sector is expected to take over routine services and provide reliable, cost-effective launch services to Indian and international customers.
- The plan is to expand India’s space sector from **$9 billion in 2026** to **$40 billion-$45 billion over the next decade**.
- Investment in the private space sector rose **sixfold**, from **$100.5 million in 2021-22** to **$618.5 million by March 31, 2026**.

## Civilian Space Activities

- The stated objective of opening the space sector was always **commercialisation**, with launch-vehicle commercialisation beginning in **2022**.
- The expansion discussed in relation to the private sector is largely restricted to **civilian space activities**.
- For India’s strategic requirements, the article argues that **in-house expertise must be preserved**:
    
    - The replacement satellite for **EOS-03** could be launched only recently, **five years after the initial failure**.
    - Such expertise is needed for major ISRO projects including **Chandrayaan 4 and 5, Gaganyaan, and Bharatiya Antariksh Station**.
- The private space sector remains at a **nascent stage** and cannot yet compete with ISRO, but expertise must be preserved even when some private companies become capable of competing.

## What Are ISRO’s Big-Ticket Missions?

- **Gaganyaan** is India’s first **human spaceflight programme**; even its first uncrewed flight is yet to take place.
- The programme will involve at least **six uncrewed flights** and **two crewed flights**.
- It includes launching the first module of the **Bharatiya Antariksh Station**, India’s planned indigenous modular space station similar to the **International Space Station**.
- ISRO plans to build a **permanent astronaut corps** and achieve a **sustained human presence in low earth orbit** in its own space laboratory.
- ISRO plans at least **two lunar sample-return missions** - **Chandrayaan 4** and **Chandrayaan 5** - before sending a human to the Moon by **2040**.
- The agency also plans another **Mars mission** and a **Venus mission**.
