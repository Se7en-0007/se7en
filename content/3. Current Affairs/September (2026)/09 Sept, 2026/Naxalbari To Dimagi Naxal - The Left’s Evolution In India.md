- Prime Minister Narendra Modi again used the term **“Dimagi Naxal”**, meaning those with a **“Naxalite mindset”**, in an address to students of **Shri Ram College of Commerce**.
- He said he had used the term in his Independence Day speech as a **“homeopathic pill”** and that the **“Dimagi Naxals”** revealed themselves through their reactions.
- Kiren Rijiju clarified that **“Dimagi Naxal”** did not refer to Opposition leaders, but to people who **stood with Maoists and separatists, supported Article 370 and Northeast separatism, and rejected the Constitution**.

## Who Is A Naxal?

- The term **“Naxal”** comes from **Naxalbari**, a village in **Darjeeling district, West Bengal**, where a **peasant uprising** occurred in **May 1967** on the lines of the **Chinese Communist Revolution led by Mao Zedong**.
- In mainstream understanding, the Naxal movement has undergone a substantial transformation and is now largely associated with **armed revolt against the State** and, more broadly, **mindless violence**.
- Its origins, however, lay in questions of **justice and inequality**, captured by the popular Maoist slogan **“land to the tillers”**.
- The demand for **“land to the tillers”** was also part of the **Indian national movement**, though it did not retain it.

## How Are Naxals, Maoists, And Marxists Different?

- Under **Charu Mazumdar**, the **Communist Party of India (Marxist-Leninist) - CPI(ML)** was formed on **April 22, 1969**.
- The CPI(ML) became one of the principal political formations of the **Naxalite movement**, which is also broadly called the **Maoist movement in India**.
- **“Maoist”** and **“Naxal”** are often used interchangeably.
- Although Naxalbari is often considered the birthplace of Indian Maoism, a faction of the **Communist Party of India** had formally adopted the ideology of **Mao Zedong’s “Chinese liberation struggle”** earlier, in **Telangana in 1948**.

### Telangana Peasant Revolt

- The genesis of Maoist insurgency is traced to the **1946-1951 Telangana peasant revolt**, in which Communist-backed villages fought the **Nizam’s regime** and **oppressive landlords**.
- The movement intensified when the princely state of **Hyderabad refused to join the Indian Union**.
- After the **1948 Army operation** that led to Hyderabad’s accession to India, the Congress-led Union government subjected these cadres to **“heavy-handed” suppression**.
- Many cadres fled to the hills, where **tribal rebellion met Maoist insurgency**.
- The armed struggle continued even after the CPI, seeking to contest the first elections of the new nation, called it off in **1951**.

### CPI Split And CPI(M)

- In **1964**, the CPI split and the **Communist Party of India (Marxist) - CPI(M)** was formed.
- The CPI(M) was closer to the **Chinese communist approach** - emphasising **peasant mobilisation** and **revolutionary struggle** - than to the Soviet model of **centralised, state-led development**.
- In the Indian context, CPI(M) cadres are specifically called **Marxists**, although both the CPI and CPI(M) derive ideology from **Marxist theory** and remain integrated within the **Indian democratic framework**.
- The CPI(M) pushed for sharper positions against the then-dominant **Congress**.

### Spread Of Naxalite Uprisings

- After Naxalbari, the late **1960s** and **1970s** saw similar uprisings in **Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha**, and parts of present-day **Jharkhand and Maharashtra**.

### Central Bihar

- In central Bihar, Naxalites resorted to **land grabs and assassinations**.
- This occurred in the backdrop of the **Bihar Abolition of Zamindari Act, 1948** and the **Land Ceiling Act, 1961** largely failing to transfer land to agricultural labourers.
- Agricultural labourers were left **“highly vulnerable”** to oppression, including oppression arising from **caste hierarchy**.
- During the late **1970s** and **1980s**, landowners formed **private caste-based militias** to crush the uprising, portraying Naxalite activities as a lower-caste movement against the interests of other castes.

### Chhattisgarh And Odisha

- In **Chhattisgarh** and **Odisha**, then Orissa, exclusion of **tribal communities** from development plans created conditions for the spread of Naxalism.
- The later **protracted war** came under criticism even within the communities where it had found space.
- The rise of **Salwa Judum**, a militia formed in Chhattisgarh to fight Naxals, was linked to **local backlash**.

### CPI (Maoist)

- The CPI(ML) gradually split into many factions.
- The **People’s War Group**, a major armed branch in undivided Andhra Pradesh, and the **Maoist Communist Centre of India**, which operated mainly in Bengal-Bihar and had earlier absorbed the Punjab-based **Revolutionary Communist Centre of India (Maoist)**, formed the **CPI (Maoist)** in **2004**.
- The CPI (Maoist) pledged to **overthrow the State**.
- Among factions that entered India’s electoral democracy, **CPI(ML) Liberation** gave up arms in **1992** and is represented by **two Lok Sabha MPs**, both from **Bihar**.

## How Does The State See Naxalism?

- The larger public largely no longer associates Naxalism with a response to **“prevailing, persisting inequalities that are only getting worse, becoming aggravated”**.
- The narrowing meaning of Naxalism is significant as similar-sounding words, stripped of its **historiography**, enter public vocabulary.
- This occurs at a time when Home Minister **Amit Shah** claims that the ideology has been **“completely eradicated”**.

### Law-And-Order Framework

- The State has mainly viewed Naxals through a **law-and-order framework**.
- In **2004**, civil society brought the State and Maoists to negotiations aimed at a **“just peace”**, but talks collapsed because the Centre insisted that Maoists should first **lay down arms**.
- In **2010**, Maoists agreed to a **ceasefire** demanded by the State, but the killing of **Azad**, the CPI (Maoist) leader leading the peace talks, halted the process.
- Talks between the **Trinamool Congress** and Naxalites later ended after **Kishenji** was killed in an alleged **fake encounter** in Bengal in **2011**.
- The TMC had supported the **2008 Lalgarh protests** by Naxals against the police and their camps, but switched its framework after coming to power.

### Silence On Historical Roots

- As security forces rout Naxals in their strongholds and the term acquires different connotations in public discourse, the historical roots of the movement - fighting **inequality and injustice** - receive little articulation.
- The Congress is described as silent because, even if not in terms of exact military tactics, it largely shares the **BJP’s opinions** on the issue.
- The Communists may have strategic reasons for silence, or the Maoists may have isolated themselves by treating everyone else as a **betrayer to the cause of revolution**.
- The Left’s silence is also attributed to **internal fragmentation** and a **state of paralysis in sufficiently articulating the problem**.
