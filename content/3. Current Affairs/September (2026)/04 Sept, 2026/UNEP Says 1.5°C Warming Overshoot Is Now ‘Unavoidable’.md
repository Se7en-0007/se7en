- **UNEP’s report, “Limiting Overshoot,” states that the world will likely “overshoot” - temporarily breach - the 1.5°C target above pre-industrial temperatures within the next few years.**
- **Crossing the 1.5°C threshold is described as “unavoidable.”**
- Even under an **“optimistic scenario”**, in which governments fully implement present climate plans and **net-zero pledges**, global temperatures are expected to **peak at about 1.8°C above pre-industrial levels**.
- The required approach is to keep the **peak as low as possible**, remain above the limit for the **shortest possible period**, and subsequently **bring temperatures down**.

## First, Why Is The 1.5°C Target Significant?

- The **1.5°C target** was a key outcome of the **2015 Paris Agreement**, signed by **193 countries and the European Union**.
- Under **Article 2 of the Paris Agreement**, countries must keep global average temperature rise **well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels**, while pursuing efforts to limit it to **1.5°C**.
- **Climate risks increase with every additional fraction of a degree of warming.**
- **1.5°C was never considered completely “safe”**, but remaining closer to it reduces the severity of risks to **people and ecosystems**.

### 2°C And 1.5°C

- For years, **2°C** was the threshold widely used to assess **dangerous climate change**.
- Economist **William Nordhaus** suggested that warming beyond **2°C** would push living conditions outside the range experienced by **human civilisation**.
- The **Cancun Agreements**, signed at **COP 16**, formally included both **2°C and 1.5°C** in an international climate accord.
- The **Paris Agreement** established **2°C as the upper limit** and **1.5°C as the more desirable goal**.
- The risks of **extreme heat, drought, water stress, and other climate impacts** are substantially lower at **1.5°C** than at **2°C**.

### Warming Beyond 1.5°C

- Climate impacts intensify as warming rises, increasing the risk of irreversible changes such as **Amazon dieback, permafrost thaw, and ice-sheet collapse**.
- The further temperatures rise above **1.5°C**, the harder and more expensive it becomes to bring them down.
- This increases dependence on **carbon dioxide removal** in addition to **emissions cuts**.
- Exceeding **1.5°C** for a few months, or even for one or two years, does **not automatically mean** that the Paris Agreement target has been breached.
- The **1.5°C goal** refers to **long-term warming measured over decades**, not the temperature average of an individual calendar year.

## Shift Strategy, Do Not Abandon 1.5°C

- The world must not abandon the **1.5°C target** despite exceeding it.
- Countries need to approach **1.5°C “from above rather than below”**.
- This requires **massively reducing emissions** and minimising the **severity of the overshoot pathway**.

## Some Damage Cannot Be Undone

- Overshooting **1.5°C** will cause **harmful and long-lasting damage**, even if temperatures are later brought down.
- The higher temperatures rise and the longer they remain above **1.5°C**, the greater the risk of:
    
    - **Extreme heat**
    - **Wildfires**
    - **Glacier loss**
    - Damage to ecosystems, including **coral reefs**
- Some changes could be difficult or impossible to reverse, including risks involving:
    
    - **Greenland ice sheet**
    - **West Antarctic ice sheet**
    - **Amazon rainforest**
    - **Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation**, a major ocean-current system that helps regulate climate

### Bringing Temperatures Back Down

- **Carbon dioxide removal** will be necessary, but even optimistic deployment is unlikely to reverse more than **a few tenths of a degree of warming this century**.
- At current rates, **afforestation and forest management alone** would require **more than 100 years** to reduce warming by just **0.1°C**, even assuming **no residual CO₂ emissions**.
- Returning below **1.5°C** would not reverse all damage caused during the overshoot.
- **Sea-level rise, extinctions, and ecosystem shifts** may continue even when temperatures decline.

## Extent Of Overshoot

- The **extent of overshoot** matters.
- A **smaller and shorter overshoot** would cause less damage and improve the prospects of bringing warming back below **1.5°C**.
- Immediate cuts in **methane** and other **short-lived climate pollutants** can slow warming relatively quickly.

## Mitigation

- The report outlines a **three-step response to overshoot**:
    
    - **Immediate future:** Slash emissions and protect vulnerable people and ecosystems.
    - **At peak warming:** Reach at least **net zero** while coping with severe impacts.
    - **After peak warming:** Sustain **net-negative CO₂ emissions** - removing more carbon dioxide from the atmosphere than is emitted - to bring temperatures down.
- **Mitigation and adaptation must happen together.**
- Weak emissions cuts make adaptation more difficult and expensive.
- Poor adaptation leaves people and infrastructure exposed to climate risks.

## Uneven Burden

- The consequences of overshoot will be **unequally shared**.
- Countries that have contributed the least to global warming are often among those most exposed to its effects.
- At **COP30 in Belém, Brazil**, countries agreed on the need to sharply scale up **adaptation finance for developing countries**.
- Adaptation needs of developing countries could exceed **$310 billion annually by 2035**.
- This requirement is about **12 times current international public-finance flows**.
