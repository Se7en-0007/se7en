- **Gloria Steinem**, among the **most well-known feminist activists of the 20th century**, died on **September 3** at the age of **92**.
- She was a **pioneering figure of the Second Wave of the Feminist Movement** of the **1960s and 1970s**, which fought for **women’s workplace rights** and their right to make **reproductive choices**.
- The **First Wave**, led by feminists in the **late 19th century**, focused on **voting rights**, a key step toward women being considered **full citizens**.
- The **Second Wave** expanded feminism into areas previously treated as part of **private life**, especially **reproduction**.
- Coming from **humble beginnings**, Steinem became a journalist and went **undercover as a Playboy employee** to expose the **exploitative inner workings** of the clubs run by the men’s entertainment enterprise.
- She visited India in **1957**, in her early twenties, under a fellowship at **Miranda House**, the women’s college of **Delhi University**.
- Steinem brought out the **first women’s magazine in the US** on her own. Her work included commitment to **anti-child trafficking in India** and **feminist criticism of pornography**.

## What Are The Waves Of Feminism?

- Steinem grew up when women in the **United States lacked legal safeguards in public life**.
- By the **1960s**, several civil-rights movements emerged, including movements for **women** and **Black Americans**.
- **Betty Friedan’s** 1963 work, **The Feminine Mystique**, identified women’s desire to move beyond the **domestic sphere** and seek something more than **husband, children, and home**.

### Second Wave Feminism

- Steinem and her longtime Indian collaborator, **Devaki Jain**, were openly **second-wave feminists**.
- Their focus was on women gaining rights in the **public sphere** and questioning the **public-private divide**, particularly to stress the importance of **reproductive rights** and related concerns.
- During her India visit, Steinem travelled through southern states with **Christian missionaries**. She later credited her experiences in **Indian villages** with teaching her how to **organise people politically**.
- In the US, women’s associations led by activists such as Steinem, along with writings such as Friedan’s, helped shift the momentum.
- Important legal developments included:
    
    - The **Equal Pay Act of 1963**, which banned discrimination based on sex for doing the same work.
    - **Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964**, which prohibited employment discrimination based on **race, colour, religion, sex, and national origin**.
    - Court rulings that expanded access to **contraception**, enabling women to plan their careers better.
    - **Roe v. Wade (1973)**, in which the US Supreme Court gave women a **constitutional right to abortion**.
- Steinem challenged **gender-based stereotypes** and advocated women becoming **self-sufficient**, while also questioning why men did not take on tasks traditionally assigned to women.
- In her satirical article **“If Men Could Menstruate”** for **Ms. magazine**, she argued that if men menstruated, menstruation would become an **enviable, boast-worthy, masculine event**.
- Her larger argument was that the traits of the **powerful** are treated as better than those of the **powerless**, irrespective of logic.

## Steinem’s Criticism And Legacy

- The Second Wave’s approach was criticised because it did not adequately acknowledge the **double burden** faced by Black women, who were simultaneously fighting for more basic rights.
- These gaps shaped the **Third Wave of Feminism** in the **1990s**.
- Lawyer and scholar **Kimberlé Crenshaw** developed the concept of **intersectionality**, examining how overlapping identities such as **race, class, gender, sexuality**, and in India, **caste**, shape people’s social experiences.
- This broadened awareness of **harassment in the workplace**.
- The **Fourth Wave**, emerging in the **2010s**, built upon these gains. The #MeToo movement was a major example, while the wave also widened the scope to include **LGBTQ identities**.

### Gender As A Critical Category

- Second-Wave feminists often framed gender in binary terms: **man and woman**.
- Steinem saw this binary as important for speaking for women, resembling **strategic essentialism**.
- Although she was open to understanding **intersectionality**, she considered gender a **critical category** for advancing understanding of **reproductive rights** and achieving **policy change**.
- Feminists have always disagreed with one another. While newer concerns such as **LGBTQ rights** require a more dynamic approach, it is also important to recognise what earlier feminism has built.

### India, Caste, And Intersectionality

- Steinem returned to India in the **early 2010s**.
- She recognised the role of **caste alongside gender**, comparing caste in India with **race in the US**, reflecting an evolving understanding of **intersectionality**.
- She argued that the restriction of women from powerful groups and exploitation of women from less powerful groups helps keep visible differences of **race or caste** alive, and that these differences can only be uprooted together.
- Steinem and Devaki Jain shared concerns over **employment**, **greater control over women’s bodies**, and **childcare**.
- Their work reflected a **transversal practice** and **dialogic citizenship** among women across the world, despite differing contexts.

### Continuing Relevance

- With **reproductive rights** being curtailed again in the US and **Roe v. Wade overturned four years ago**, Steinem’s ideas have gained renewed relevance.
- Her work was **accessible** and brought together people who might otherwise never have come together.
