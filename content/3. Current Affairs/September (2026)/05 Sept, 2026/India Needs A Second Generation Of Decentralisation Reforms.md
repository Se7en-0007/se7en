- **Against the backdrop of the 16th Finance Commission recommendations** and **prolonged delays in municipal elections across major Indian cities**, local governance has acquired renewed urgency.
- Since the **1970s**, a global **devolutionary trend** has increased the role of local and regional governments in stimulating economic development.
- The rationale for decentralisation shifted from an **identity discourse** based on cultural and linguistic factors to an economic discourse of achieving **greater economic and social change**.

## India’s Local Governance Experiment

- India’s local governance experiment is among the most important, yet most unfulfilled, **post-Independence constitutional reforms**.
- The **73rd and 74th amendments** promised to make **panchayats and municipalities real units of self-government**, bringing democracy closer to citizens and development closer to local needs.
- Although local bodies exist, elections are held, and constitutional language is in place, the real powers of government - the **3Fs: funds, function, and functionaries** - are often retained at higher levels.
- Consequently, India is **decentralised on paper but centralised in spirit**.

## Why Decentralisation Matters

- **Local governments** are closer to people’s everyday problems and are better placed to identify bottlenecks, prioritise spending, and adapt policy to specific **social and ecological conditions**.
- Panchayats and municipalities determine whether **water reaches a neighbourhood, roads are repaired, and waste is collected**.
- Local government is not a decorative layer of democracy - it is where **governance becomes visible**.
- Decentralised governments can provide services that reflect **diverse local needs**.
- Better matching of **public goods and services provisioning** with **individual preferences** maximises both **collective and individual welfare**.

## State-Citizen Synergy

- Decentralisation shortens the distance between the **citizen and the state**, making it easier to question power and ensure accountability.
- Citizens can more readily confront a **ward councillor or sarpanch** than a Minister or senior bureaucrat.
- While this does not automatically guarantee good governance, it makes **accountability more tangible**.
- Decentralised governance bridges the gap between citizens and a state often viewed as **remote and impersonal**.

## Developmental Argument

- Local bodies can shape **local economic growth**, not merely deliver services.
- **Roads, drainage, markets, sanitation, and other basic infrastructure** are forms of social investment that determine whether a local economy grows or stagnates in the long run.
- Decentralisation is therefore a practical strategy for **sustainable development**, not merely a democratic ideal.
- When local institutions possess real authority, they can adapt public spending to local **geography, livelihoods, and environmental stress**.

## Kerala’s Experience

- **Kerala** is often cited as a leading example of decentralisation.
- Studies of gram panchayats show that fiscal success depends not only on transfers but also on **administrative preparedness, planning capacity, and institutional support**.
- Research by **Parvathy Sailesh and Padmini Ram (2022)** indicates that decentralisation works best when local bodies are treated as **genuine governing institutions**, rather than delivery arms of higher tiers of government.
- Kerala’s lesson is not that it is perfect, but that decentralisation can work when the State commits to it **in practice, not merely in principle**.

## Services To Benefit

- A **2013 comparative study of Odisha and Gujarat** by **Raut and Sekher** found notable differences in health-system decentralisation, with **Gujarat performing better on decentralisation measures**.
- Public health is a sector where **local responsiveness, coordination, and problem-solving** can make a real difference.
- Decentralisation shapes how services are organised, who has authority, and how quickly public institutions respond to **local needs**.

## Weaknesses Of India’s Decentralisation Framework

- Responsibility is often devolved without **genuine power**.
- Local bodies are asked to deliver services without adequate **funds, functions, or functionaries**.
- Many local governments face **limited budgets, inadequate staff, and overlapping control from higher levels of government**.
- Modern urban schemes increasingly rely on **special purpose vehicles run by bureaucrats**, which bypass elected municipal bodies and further erode the **74th amendment**.

## Fiscal Dependence

- Local bodies in many States rely heavily on transfers from above, often with **limited discretion and little predictability**.
- This makes long-term planning difficult and weakens **local accountability**.
- A decentralised system cannot function properly when local institutions depend on **irregular or tightly controlled funding**.
- Decentralisation must enable local governments to **decide, plan, and adapt**, rather than merely shift responsibilities downward.

## Capacity

- Local representatives may be elected and accountable but often lack adequate **technical support or training**.
- **Budgeting, procurement, record-keeping, and project monitoring** require skills that many local bodies do not possess.
- Capacity constraints are compounded by social hurdles, including the power wielded by the **spouses of elected woman representatives**.
- In urban centres, real financial power is often concentrated with **unelected municipal commissioners** rather than elected Mayors.
- Without addressing these issues, decentralisation risks remaining a **formal exercise rather than a functional one**.

## Second Generation Of Decentralisation Reforms

- India needs a **second generation of decentralisation reforms** that moves beyond symbolism to substance.
- **The 3Fs must be genuinely devolved**: local governments should have clearly assigned responsibilities, finances, and administrative staff to fulfil their mandates.
- **State Finance Commissions** must become more effective and consequential, ensuring transfers are **transparent, regular, and aligned with local needs**.
- **Local planning must become real rather than ceremonial**: district and local plans should guide actual spending decisions, not merely record them.
- **Capacity building** should be treated as core infrastructure for governance, with routine training in **budgeting, procurement, planning, and social audit**.
- Accountability should be strengthened through **transparent reporting, public dashboards, social audits, and stronger citizen participation** at ward and village levels.

## The Unfinished Promise

- India does not need to invent decentralisation - it needs to **complete it**.
- The **constitutional framework exists**, the democratic case is established, and the developmental logic is strong.
- What is missing is the **political will to trust local institutions with genuine power**.
- Decentralisation is not a nostalgic idea from the 1990s but a central requirement for India’s future, including **more accountable governance, more resilient public services, and more sustainable local development**.
- The unfinished promise of the **73rd and 74th amendments** must be delivered on the ground.
- The **16th Finance Commission** has a crucial window to act by explicitly tying State grants to the **genuine empowerment of local bodies**.
