- **India is revising its model bilateral investment treaty (BIT)**, and the revised text is expected to be placed before the **Union Cabinet** soon.
- The process was initiated after the **Finance Minister stated in the 2025 Union Budget speech** that India was considering revamping its **2015 Model BIT**.
- The **2015 Model BIT** emerged from a broader appraisal triggered after several foreign investors sued India for **BIT breaches**.
- The two principal outcomes of that appraisal were:
    
    - **Unilateral termination of BITs**
    - Adoption of a **new model BIT** as the basis for fresh negotiations

## A Model That Is Out Of Balance

- Over the last decade or so, India has concluded only **a handful of BITs** based on its **2015 model**.
- This reflects the limitations of the **2015 Model BIT**.
- Investment treaties seek to balance two competing objectives:
    
    - **Investment protection**
    - **The State’s right to regulate**
- The Indian model BIT tilts heavily towards **the State’s right to regulate**, rather than investment protection.
- This produces doubts among countries that export capital to India regarding the **legal protection** available to their investments.
- Such doubts are intensified by:
    
    - **High regulatory risks**
    - **Not-so-well-developed governance models**
    - **An agonisingly tardy judicial system**
- India’s decision to revisit the model is intended to pull the pendulum back towards **the centre**.

## Review Of BITs

- A BIT review has **two core components**:
    
    - Determining the **substantive and procedural changes** required in the law
    - Determining the **processes** needed to ensure a robust outcome
- The first component has received considerable attention. Proposed legal reforms include:
    
    - Making it easier for foreign investors to use **international arbitration** for treaty claims
    - Enhancing **substantive protections** for foreign investment
    - Introducing more **investment facilitation measures**
- The second component - the process for ensuring the robustness of the outcome - has received comparatively little deliberation.

## Concerns About Democratic Deficit

- International economic treaties, including those concerning foreign investment, have a conspicuous impact on citizens and therefore invoke the **all-affected principle in democracy**.
- The central question is whether persons affected by an investment treaty should have a right to participate in decision-making.
- When affected persons cannot effectively participate through their **elected representatives** or **civil society organisations**, it raises concerns of a **democratic deficit** in treaty-making.
- Originating in European debates, **democratic deficit** means insufficient oversight of:
    
    - **Technocrats and bureaucracies**
    - The **executive**, including the political executive
    - Those who negotiate and develop treaty frameworks behind closed doors
- This lack of oversight can take forms such as:
    
    - Lack of, or inadequate, **parliamentary supervision** of treaty-making
    - Absence of an external consultative process involving **subject-matter experts** and **civil society organisations**

## Wider Consultation

- Several countries seek to overcome the democratic deficit by placing negotiated treaty texts before Parliament before ratification:
    
    - The **United Kingdom** and **Australia** mandatorily place treaty texts before Parliament, enabling it to express its views.
- In relation to model BITs:
    
    - **Norway** conducted two rounds of public consultations on an updated draft, in **2008** and **2015**.
    - **Colombia** released its model BIT for public consultation.
    - In **March 2015**, India circulated the draft **2015 Model BIT** for public comment.
- India’s public consultation enabled the **Law Commission of India (LCI)** to constitute a team of experts to examine the draft.
- In its **260th report**, the LCI recommended ways to improve the draft Model BIT.
- India adopted the revised version in **December 2015**, although it did not incorporate all LCI recommendations.

## Have A Consultative Process

- The government is presumed to have already conducted widespread **internal consultations**, namely intra-governmental deliberations.
- For external consultation, the following steps may be considered:

1. **Form a core team of external experts** with demonstrated proficiency in **international investment law** or **foreign investment**.
    
    - It should ideally include **international lawyers** and **economists** from universities, research institutions and think tanks.
    - The team could function as a **sounding board** for the government.
2. **Invite views from stakeholders**, including **industry bodies, arbitrators, law firms and other civil society organisations**.
3. **Prepare a draft and place it in the public domain**, inviting comments from the public at large, as India did in **March 2015**.
4. **Place the draft Model BIT before Parliament for discussion**, either following or simultaneously with public consultation.
    
    - Relevant **department-related parliamentary committees** can also be involved.

- The consultative process must not become a **box-ticking exercise**.
- It should be a meaningful activity that upholds the spirit of **participatory democracy**, necessarily including engagement with **dissenting views**.
