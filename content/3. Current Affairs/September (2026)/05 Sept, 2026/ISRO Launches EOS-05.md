## EOS-05 And Geosynchronous Orbit

- **ISRO launched India’s first dedicated imaging satellite, EOS-05, to operate in geosynchronous orbit at about 36,000 km from Earth.**
- India already has several active **Earth observation satellites** in **lower Earth orbits**, which circle Earth and periodically capture imagery over India.
- In **geosynchronous orbit**, a satellite remains **stationary relative to Earth**, enabling it to watch the **same region continuously without gaps**.

## What Are The Different Types Of Orbit?

- An **orbit** is the **curved path** followed by an object in space around another object owing to **gravity**.
- Earth’s orbits are broadly categorised into **three primary altitude classes**, each serving a different purpose:
    
    - **Low Earth orbit (LEO)**
    - **Medium Earth orbit (MEO)**
    - **Geosynchronous orbit (GSO)**

## Low Earth Orbit (LEO)

- **Low Earth Orbit (LEO)** is the zone immediately surrounding Earth, between **160 km and 2,000 km above Earth’s surface**.
- Its proximity to Earth reduces **signal latency**.
- LEO is densely populated by **communications and observation satellites**, **satellite constellations**, and **mega-constellations** - large groups of up to **thousands of satellites working together**.
- The **International Space Station** is also located in this zone.
- LEO includes subsets such as:
    
    - **VLEO - Very Low Earth Orbit**
    - **SSO - Sun-synchronous orbit**
- **VLEO satellites** can provide **higher image resolution** because they are closer to Earth, but they face much more **atmospheric drag**, increasing their **fuel requirements**.
- Satellites in LEO generally take **90-120 minutes** to circle Earth, but do not necessarily need to follow a permanent path.
- As of **September 4**, there were **15,562 active payload satellites in LEO**.
- India had **22 operational satellites** in LEO.

## Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)

- **Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)** extends from **2,000 km to 35,786 km**, just below geosynchronous orbit.
- Like LEO satellites, MEO satellites are **not confined to following a specific path** around Earth.
- MEO houses global navigation satellite systems such as **Galileo**, a constellation of **30 satellites**.
- It provides a balance between **low signal latency** and a **large coverage area**.

## Geosynchronous Orbit (GSO)

- Satellites in **Geosynchronous Orbit (GSO)** are positioned above roughly **35,786 km**.
- They fly over Earth’s **equator**, move from **West to East**, and exactly match Earth’s rotation.
- Therefore, their position relative to Earth appears **stationary**.

### Geostationary Orbit (GEO)

- **Geostationary orbit (GEO)** is a subset of GSO.
- A satellite appears stationary relative to Earth when it is placed in a:
    
    - **Circular orbit**
    - **Directly over the equator**
    - **Zero-degree inclination**
    - **Orbital period matching Earth’s rotation**
- Satellites in GEO cover **large portions of Earth**.
- Just **three evenly spaced satellites** can provide **near-global coverage**.
- India has **31 operational satellites in GEO**.

## Why Can’t Rockets Place Satellites Directly In Geosynchronous Orbits?

- **EOS-05 weighs 2,367 kg**, making it a heavy satellite.
- Direct launch towards geosynchronous orbit would require **immense amounts of fuel**.
- ISRO generally takes satellites bound for GEO to a **Geosynchronous Transfer Orbit (GTO)** or, in some cases, a **lower-energy sub-GTO**, and leaves them there.
- The satellite then uses its **own propulsion**, rather than the rocket’s, to:
    
    - **Raise and circularise its orbit**
    - **Reduce its inclination**
    - Reach its **operational orbit**
- The **GSLV - Geosynchronous Satellite Launch Vehicle** deposited EOS-05 in **sub-GTO**, at an altitude of **more than 190 km from Earth**.
- ISRO used its **second most powerful rocket**, whose published **GTO capacity was below the mass of EOS-05**, instead of the **LVM3**.
- After release, EOS-05 uses its own propulsion system to reach the final geosynchronous orbit.

## Why Do We Need Satellites In Geosynchronous Orbit?

- Traditionally, most imaging and observation satellites operate in **lower Earth orbit**.
- Because LEO satellites are close to Earth’s surface, they must travel at **breakneck speeds** to counteract Earth’s gravitational pull.
- As a result, they can observe a specific patch of Earth only for a **brief window** before moving past it, creating **significant gaps in coverage**.
- Satellites in geostationary orbit appear fixed relative to Earth, providing:
    
    - **Constant persistence**
    - **Large area of coverage**
    - **Persistent, high-frequency observation of large regions**
    - Observation without relying only on **periodic satellite revisits**

### Trade-Off

- The trade-off is lower **image resolution** because of the satellite’s distance from Earth’s surface.
- Satellites also experience perturbations from:
    
    - The gravitational influence of the **Sun and Moon**
    - Earth’s **non-uniform gravity field**
    - **Solar-radiation pressure**
- These perturbations increase the need for **station-keeping** and the satellite’s **fuel spend** to stay on path.

## EOS-05 Satellite

- EOS-05’s geosynchronous imaging capabilities can enable persistent, high-frequency observation of large regions.
- This can be strategically valuable for:
    
    - Tracking rapidly evolving **weather systems**
    - Monitoring **floods** and **forest fires**
    - Monitoring **agriculture** and **natural resources**
    - Strengthening situational awareness across India’s **land and maritime domains**
- The key opportunity is to translate the rich, frequent satellite data into **actionable intelligence** through India’s **private space and geospatial ecosystem**.
