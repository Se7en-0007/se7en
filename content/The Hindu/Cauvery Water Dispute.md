## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of the Dispute</mark>

The Cauvery river dispute is one of India's longest-running interstate water conflicts, involving Karnataka (upstream riparian state) and Tamil Nadu (downstream riparian state). Karnataka controls the river's flow through its reservoirs, while Tamil Nadu's delta agriculture depends heavily on water releases, especially during the kharif season. The current crisis (2026) stems from a deficient southwest monsoon in its first half, reviving the familiar pattern of distress and dispute between the two states.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Institutional Mechanisms</mark>

- Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal: adjudicated water-sharing shares between riparian states, with allocations upheld by the Supreme Court.
    
- Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA): the apex body responsible for implementing Tribunal/Supreme Court orders and overseeing water releases.
    
- Cauvery Water Regulation Committee (CWRC): a technical committee that issues specific directives on daily release quantities from Karnataka to Tamil Nadu.
    
- Billigundulu: the inter-state border measuring point where actual water flow into Tamil Nadu is gauged against stipulated targets.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Crisis Details (2026)</mark>

Karnataka's Congress government withheld irrigation water releases, citing anticipated drinking water shortages amid poor monsoon forecasts. This resulted in Billigundulu recording only about 1,000 million cubic feet (tmc ft) against a stipulated 31 tmc ft target for July, normally the wettest month. Tamil Nadu similarly received only around 3.6 tmc ft between June 1 and end of July, roughly 10 percent of the approximately 40 tmc ft prescribed by the Tribunal and Supreme Court for that period.

On July 30, the CWMA ratified a CWRC directive instructing Karnataka to release 3,500 cusecs daily to Billigundulu for 15 days starting July 29. Even though this quantum would not fully meet Tamil Nadu's irrigation needs, it was meant to provide ecological support to the delta region. Heavy rainfall reviving inflows into four major Karnataka reservoirs has since eased tensions somewhat, though it is premature to conclude the water shortage crisis is resolved.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Way Forward and Analysis</mark>

The two states last attempted to negotiate a distress-sharing formula nearly 25 years ago, and no durable mechanism has since been institutionalized for low-rainfall years. A distress-sharing formula would allocate proportionate cuts to both states during deficient monsoon years rather than triggering ad hoc standoffs each time. The editorial's normative position is that Karnataka must first comply with existing institutional mechanisms (CWMA and CWRC) even while it can propose alternatives, since these bodies operationalize Tribunal and Supreme Court verdicts safeguarding riparian rights.
