## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

- The Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969 governs registration of vital events in India.
    
- The 2023 amendment made birth certificates the principal, near-conclusive proof of date and place of birth for admissions, voter rolls, passport, Aadhaar, driving licence, and government job applications.
    
- The 2023 amendment also empowered the Centre and States to build digital databases of births and deaths and mandated data sharing with the Registrar General of India.
    
- As certificates gained gatekeeping power over access to services, incentives for fraudulent registration also increased, prompting the 2026 amendment.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What the 2026 Bill Changes</mark>

- The Bill amends Section 13(3) of the 1969 Act, which deals with delayed registration of births/deaths.
    
- For delays up to two years: registration still requires an order from a district magistrate, subdivisional magistrate, or authorised executive magistrate (unchanged from earlier).
    
- For delays exceeding two years: a judicial magistrate must now verify the event and pass an order (new requirement), shifting authority from the executive to the judiciary.
    
- Evidentiary rules and documentation standards for registration remain unchanged despite the added judicial layer.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legislative Process Concerns</mark>

- The Bill was passed in the Lok Sabha without debate.
    
- This occurred because the Opposition was protesting the July 20, 2026 police crackdown on Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) protests.
    
- The Rajya Sabha has yet to debate the Bill.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Rationale and Government's Stated Logic</mark>

- Moving contentious registration cases to courts is meant to insulate the administration from accusations of arbitrary grant or denial of documentation.
    
- Identity documents have become increasingly politically sensitive, making this insulation strategically important for the government.
    
- However, the government has not published evidence justifying why the two-year threshold specifically triggers judicial intervention, nor has it explained why the risk of fraud rises after two years.
    
- It also does not appear to have examined less burdensome alternatives to judicial referral.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Concerns and Critique</mark>

- **Rectification difficulty**: While judicial scrutiny distances decisions from the executive, erroneous judicial decisions may be harder for disadvantaged applicants to challenge or reverse than executive ones.
    
- **No improvement to evidentiary standards**: If suspicious applications were being approved due to lax evidentiary standards, the government could have tightened documentation requirements instead of adding a judicial layer.
    
- **Valid reasons for delay ignored**: Registration delays often occur for legitimate reasons, such as events occurring in remote areas, or undocumented migrants realising the need for educational certificates only later.
    
- **Policy philosophy critique**: The government's consistent instinct has been to raise the assurance level of documents rather than make them more accessible, potentially treating wrongful exclusion of genuine citizens as an acceptable cost to minimise wrongful inclusion.

|Aspect|Delay up to 2 years|Delay beyond 2 years|
|---|---|---|
|Authority required|District/Subdivisional/Executive Magistrate|Judicial Magistrate|
|Nature of authority|Executive|Judicial|
|Evidentiary standard|Unchanged|Unchanged|
|Rationale given by government|Administrative feasibility|Verification of event (rationale for threshold not evidenced)|
