## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Core Contradiction</mark>

Meghalaya, home to Sohra (Cherrapunji) and Mawsynram - among the wettest inhabited places on Earth with over 11,000 mm annual rainfall - faces acute drinking water shortages every winter. From November to March, at least 40% of residents in Sohra constituency buy water from private contractors, with 500 litres costing close to a thousand rupees. The paradox: abundance of rainfall has never translated into water security, because the state never built infrastructure to store what falls.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cause 1: Declining and Erratic Rainfall</mark>

- Long-term decline: a joint IIT Kharagpur–IITM Pune study of rain-gauge data (1901–2019) found Sohra losing about 27.15 mm of rainfall per year over the last four decades.
    
- Sohra has already lost its "wettest place on earth" title to nearby Mawsynram (just 15 km away), which is now also recording a decline.
    
- Drivers include rising sea-surface temperatures in the tropical Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea (altering pre-monsoon moisture transport) and loss of vegetation cover as forests give way to croplands.
    
- The monsoon pattern itself is shifting from prolonged, relentless rain to shorter, sharper bursts.
    
- A recent shock: Meghalaya recorded an ~80% rainfall deficit in June (normally peak monsoon) under El Niño influence, prompting Chief Minister Conrad K. Sangma to call it an "existential crisis."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cause 2: Geology, Slope and Runoff</mark>

- Sohra sits on a high limestone plateau; rainfall drains away almost immediately, sheeting down hillsides toward the plains of Bangladesh instead of being retained locally.
    
- Deforestation has stripped soil of its water-holding capacity, worsening unharvested runoff.
    
- Unlike arid regions that invest heavily in storage because they expect scarcity, Meghalaya's economy and settlements were historically built on an assumption of endless water, so no dry-season storage systems were ever developed.
    
- The Mawphlang dam, a key reservoir supplying drinking water to Shillong, has reportedly dried up during deficit spells, with its catchment further degraded by stone quarrying.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cause 3: Governance and Infrastructure Failure</mark>

- Under the Jal Jeevan Mission (launched 2019), Meghalaya lags behind national progress - rural tap-water coverage was around 43.57% in 2023, versus a national average of roughly 55.32%.
    
- In the Garo Hills, taps have been installed with no water supply behind them, or left entirely unconnected to any pipeline.
    
- Of roughly 14,600 schools in the state, only about 6,720 have functional drinking water - a serious gap as climate-driven heatwaves raise dehydration risks among children.
    
- Drinking water has become a recurring but broken election promise across successive Assembly elections for more than 25,000 voters in the Sohra constituency alone.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Who Bears the Burden</mark>

|Group|Impact|
|---|---|
|Women and children|Walk several kilometres daily to natural springs, hauling water uphill by cart, bicycle, or bamboo pole|
|Poorer households|Cannot afford private contractor water, forced to walk long distances|
|Wealthier households|Buy water from private contractors at rising seasonal prices|
|Schoolchildren|Face dehydration risk due to non-functional water supply in thousands of schools|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Path Forward</mark>

- The Meghalaya Water Foundation argues people must be central to solutions, not passive recipients of pipes.
    
- The draft Integrated State Water Policy (Department of Water Resources) is built on sustainable management and community participation, potentially serving as a model for other water-stressed states/cities like Chennai and Bengaluru.
    
- Practical priorities include: harvesting rainwater currently running off to Bangladesh, reforesting critical catchments like Mawphlang, curbing quarrying and unplanned land-use change, and investing in storage and distribution infrastructure.
    
- The core takeaway: this is fundamentally a crisis of management and political follow-through, not merely a meteorological one.
