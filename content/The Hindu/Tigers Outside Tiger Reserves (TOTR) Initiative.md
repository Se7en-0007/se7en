## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Tiger Conservation in India</mark>

- Project Tiger launched in 1973 marked the beginning of India's tiger conservation programme.

- Protected area network expanded from 9 reserves (18,278 sq km) in 1973 to 58 reserves (84,488 sq km) today, covering nearly 2.6% of India's landmass.

- Tiger population rose sharply from 1,411 (2006) to 3,682 (2022), making India home to over 70% of the world's wild tigers.

- This success created a new challenge: a growing share of tigers now living outside protected areas.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why TOTR Was Launched</mark>

- As per the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA), 35-40% of India's tiger population currently lives outside designated Tiger Reserves.

- Tigers are territorial carnivores requiring large home ranges; when core habitats reach saturation, tigers naturally disperse into human-dominated fringe areas.

- Increased tiger presence outside reserves has led to more human-tiger interactions - fatalities, livestock depredation, accidental encounters, prolonged rescue operations, and public anxiety.

- These incidents carry ecological, social, and political consequences, straining forest departments and district administrations.

- In response, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change launched the TOTR initiative.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Objective and Approach</mark>

- Establish a scientifically grounded, landscape-based framework for conserving tigers outside protected areas while safeguarding human lives and livelihoods.

- Shift the governance model from reactive crisis management to proactive, science-driven governance.

- Built on three pillars of implementation: technology, preparedness, and community participation.

- Launched as a targeted pilot programme rather than a nationwide rollout, so that long-term policy can be based on evidence from real field conditions.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scope of the Pilot</mark>

- Based on the All-India Tiger Estimation (2022), which recorded tiger presence across 212 forest divisions.

- 40 forest divisions across 9 States selected for Phase-I implementation.

- Participating States: Uttarakhand, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, and Assam.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Two Pillars of TOTR</mark>

**Pillar 1: Conflict Reduction and Field Protection**

- Upgrading communication systems for faster response.

- Deploying rapid response teams for conflict situations.

- Improving rescue infrastructure and capacity.

- Ensuring coordinated action during emergencies across departments.


**Pillar 2: Long-Term Coexistence**

- Awareness programmes for local communities.

- Structured community engagement, treating villagers as partners rather than bystanders.

- Timely compensation mechanisms for loss of life, injury, or livestock.

- Building trust to sustain conservation efforts on shared landscapes.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Role of Compensation Mechanisms</mark>

- Streamlined, timely compensation is identified as critical to the initiative's success.

- Though States have their own compensation frameworks, delays often erode public trust and fuel resentment among affected communities.

- States are urged to simplify procedures and invest in sustained outreach to build confidence among rural families living alongside tigers.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Technology and Institutional Capacity</mark>

- TOTR promotes modern conservation tools: camera traps, GPS-enabled monitoring systems, drones, and AI.

- These tools aim to strengthen surveillance, improve early warning systems, and enable evidence-based decision-making.

- However, technology alone cannot resolve human-tiger conflict - effectiveness depends on institutional strength.

- Requires well-trained forest staff, veterinarians, and rescue teams capable of rapid response.


## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Broader Significance</mark>

- TOTR reflects a shift in conservation philosophy - from focusing solely on protected areas to recognising a wider mosaic of reserve forests and non-reserve lands that now support dispersing tiger populations.

- Highlights the broader national need to strengthen governance of "shared landscapes" where ecology and human livelihoods intersect.

- If executed effectively, TOTR has the potential to become a landmark conservation initiative, similar in stature to Project Tiger, demonstrating that humans and large carnivores can coexist safely.
