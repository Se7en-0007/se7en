## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background</mark>

The Government of India is considering a standalone AI legislation, but immediate pressure is already coming from the EU's AI Act. This Act entered into force in August 2024 and became applicable on August 2, 2026. It follows a risk-based regulatory approach, meaning different categories of AI systems face different levels of scrutiny.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Risk-Based Classification Under EU AI Act</mark>

- Prohibited AI systems - certain uses banned outright.
    
- High-risk AI systems - subject to strict regulation (e.g., hiring, education decisions).
    
- Limited-risk AI systems - face lighter compliance checks.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Core Mismatch: India's Business Model vs. EU Assumptions</mark>

- The EU Act assumes software is built, approved, and sold as a finished, static product.
    
- India's tech industry (IT services, global capability centres) operates on continuous, responsive adaptation and bespoke client-driven updates - a fundamentally different model.
    
- This mismatch, rather than the law's extraterritorial reach, is the real concern for Indian firms.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Conformity Assessment: Key Mechanism</mark>

- Article 43 requires "high-risk" AI systems to undergo a conformity assessment before entering the European market.
    
- This proves compliance on testing, documentation, and human oversight standards.
    
- Most providers self-assess and self-declare; only a narrow set (mainly certain biometric tools) need independent third-party assessment.
    
- Once approved, the system can operate freely - unless "substantially modified."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Counts as Substantial Modification</mark>

- A change not contemplated during the original assessment.
    
- One that affects compliance or alters the system's intended purpose.
    
- Triggers a requirement to repeat the entire conformity assessment process.
    
- Distinction matters: foreseen/planned changes examined at the original assessment do NOT trigger a fresh review; unanticipated changes likely do.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Revised Compliance Timeline (2026 Update)</mark>

|Category|Deadline|
|---|---|
|Standalone high-risk AI systems|December 2027|
|High-risk AI embedded in regulated products|August 2, 2028|
|Grandfathering clause|Systems already on market before deadline exempted until substantially modified|

This easing package received final EU approval in June 2026.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Business Model Implications</mark>

- Favours firms with predictable, standardised product roadmaps who can pre-assess future planned upgrades.
    
- Disadvantages firms offering bespoke, client-adaptive services - harder to prove future changes fall within original assessment scope.
    
- Liability risk: firms that substantially modify someone else's high-risk AI system may be treated as the "provider," inheriting all original obligations.
    
- This directly challenges India's core value proposition - "improvement on demand" - since unplanned changes could trigger fresh regulatory burden.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Opportunity for India</mark>

- High-risk compliance work is fundamentally paperwork and proof-driven: governance measures, technical documentation, testing regimes.
    
- This compliance work must be done at volume, against harmonised technical standards still being drafted.
    
- Indian professional services firms already have experience in adjacent fields - data protection, financial regulation, technical assurance - that can be extended to AI Act compliance.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Longer-Term Strategic Vision: Treaty Pathway</mark>

- The EU AI Act allows conformity assessment bodies from third countries to be recognised as "notified bodies," provided the EU has an appropriate agreement with that country.
    
- The India-EU Free Trade Agreement (concluded January 2026) contains standing machinery and regulatory cooperation provisions.
    
- If India secures institutional recognition under this FTA, Indian conformity assessment bodies could eventually perform official EU-recognised compliance functions - not just supply compliance services, but participate directly in the EU's assessment ecosystem.
