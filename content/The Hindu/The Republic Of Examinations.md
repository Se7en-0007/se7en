- **Public examinations shape how citizens experience the modern state** by allocating not merely **marks or jobs**, but **the future**.
- They embody **fairness** and connect citizens across social classes to the state.
- Families organise aspirations of **education, livelihood, marriage, honour, and prestige** around examinations.
- For students and parents, examinations create a **Damocles sword** of anxiety.

## When Failures Of Educational Legitimacy Become Crises Of Political Legitimacy

- Examination-related disputes have repeatedly expanded into wider political conflicts:
    
    - **China**: The abolition of **imperial examinations in 1905** shut a principal route to social mobility under the **Qing dynasty**, eroded its legitimacy, and contributed to the **1911 Revolution**.
    - **Sri Lanka**: Changes in university admissions convinced many **Tamils** that **equal citizenship** was slipping away.
    - **South Africa**: The **1976 Soweto uprising** began with students protesting the imposition of **Afrikaans** as the language of instruction and examinations, followed by brutal repression.
    - Similar conflicts have occurred from **Bangladesh and Chile** to **Ethiopia and France**.
- Although the immediate examination crisis appears to have passed, with the **Education Minister’s resignation**, a **high-powered task force**, and stronger penalties for fraud, it would be wrong to conclude that the crisis has ended.

## A Sense Of Systematic Exclusion

- Administrative failures may be addressed through **stronger encryption, tighter invigilation, and better technology**.
- However, the deeper question is whether public outrage also reflects a **sense of systematic exclusion** perpetuated through the education system, of which examinations are the **crowning glory**.
- **2.2 million students** took **NEET** this year.
- Their experience is being watched by:
    
    - **17 million students** sitting for secondary and higher secondary **Board examinations** every year.
    - More than **30 million students** enrolled in the final two years of schooling.
    - Roughly **125 million Indians aged 15-19** awaiting their own encounter with the examination system.
- Thus, the grievance of one cohort becomes the **looming nightmare** of the next.

## Markedly Different Educational Systems

- India’s school system is deeply segregated across:
    
    - **Elite English-medium schools** affiliated with national Boards.
    - **Low-cost private schools** of varying quality.
    - **Under-resourced government schools**, mostly following State Board curricula in regional languages.
- A clear hierarchy exists, with **government schools occupying the lowest rung**.
- Parents compensate for educational deficiencies through **private tuition**.
- These distinct educational worlds converge in national entrance and recruitment examinations such as **NEET, JEE, and civil services examinations**, which claim to rank students by a **common standard**.

## Seeking Upward Mobility

- For a **Class 12 student in a Hindi-medium government school in eastern Uttar Pradesh or Bihar**, an examination is an effort to cross the divide between **one India and another**.
- Each examination holds out the possibility, however remote, of:
    
    - Escaping **precarious work**.
    - Entering **higher education**.
    - Acquiring credentials and **English-language skills** required by much of the organised economy.
    - Securing **dignified employment**.
- In India’s high-growth economy, the fastest-growing jobs are in **construction, informal and domestic services, delivery, and ride-sharing**.
- **Barely one in five Indian workers** has a **regular salaried job**.
- Only a **tiny minority** has written contracts and employment benefits.
- Examinations thus promise incorporation into a **better world**.

## The Promise Of Impartial Rules

- Public examinations derive extraordinary legitimacy from the belief that, despite unequal educational systems, **the rules are impartial**.
- Their legitimacy does not rest on erasing inequality, but on sustaining the belief that inequalities can be overcome.
- Citizens need not believe that everyone will succeed; they must believe that **“someone like me” could succeed**.
- The more unequal a society is, the stronger the appeal of public examinations, provided they are seen as fair.
- Whether a state is formally **democratic** or **authoritarian** matters less because citizens encounter the state primarily through **schools, universities, examinations, and recruitment Boards**, rather than elections.
- China’s imperial examination system survived for centuries because it convinced people that public office was allocated according to standards.

## Towards An Open Society

- Independent India transformed a colonial institution into one of the Republic’s principal foundations.
- Colonial examinations introduced the political imagination that advancement could be acquired through **education**, rather than being denied by **birth**.
- After Independence, public examinations, together with **reservations**, became the Republic’s commitment to opening society and the economy to all.
- The promise was not that everyone would succeed, but that **every citizen possessed a legitimate claim to compete**.
- India became a **republic of examinations**.
- Parents criticise examination stress yet enrol children in stressful pre-schools and coaching centres because they trust that rules will ultimately be fairly applied.
- Every education policy since Independence has promised to reduce the **tyranny of examinations**, but no government has seriously displaced them because no alternative commands equivalent confidence.
- During the **COVID-19 pandemic**, India kept schools closed for **over two years**, longer than China, most of Latin America, or many African countries, yet public examinations went ahead.

## Educational Disappointment Becomes Political Grievance

- Examinations ordinarily individualise disappointment: **“I failed.”**
- Examination scandals collectivise disappointment: **“We were cheated.”**
- Students perceive betrayal by the system that asked them to work hard, sacrifice, wait their turn, and trust that competition would be fair.
- Educational disappointment therefore becomes **political grievance**.
- The promise of access to a better future begins to appear hollow.
- Being dismissed as **“cockroaches”** resonated because it captured the reality that those who fail to climb the examination ladder and secure good jobs are seen as **individually responsible and socially disposable**.

## Address Inequality And Injustice

- The republic of examinations begins to unravel when public examinations lose authority as **impartial arbiters of the nation’s future**.
- Their authority also produces resistance to fundamental educational reforms among both the **elite** and the **masses**.
- Reforms must address inequality and injustice in a **hierarchically organised system of institutions**.
- Indicators of educational failure include:
    
    - More than **half of Class 5 children** cannot read a **Class 2 text**.
    - Fewer than **one-third** can perform **basic division**.
    - Fewer than **half of graduates** are judged employable by employers.
- Technology and tighter supervision may restore confidence in procedure, but the deeper issue is whether they can restore faith in the **constitutional promise**.
- Fundamental problems in the education system and broader **economic inequalities** must also be addressed; failure may pave the way for a **deeper crisis**.

## Arbitrariness, Exclusion And Domination

- Educational legitimacy can unravel through:
    
    - **Arbitrariness** - rules no longer appear fair.
    - **Exclusion** - unequal access to education is experienced as unequal citizenship.
    - **Domination** - education becomes an instrument through which one group dominates another.
- **Imperial China** illustrates arbitrariness: abolition of the examination system without a credible alternative weakened confidence in a principal source of Qing legitimacy.
- **Sri Lanka and Chile** illustrate exclusion:
    
    - In Sri Lanka, university admissions policy symbolised unequal citizenship between linguistic communities.
    - In Chile, a deeply marketised education system came to embody entrenched social inequality rather than equal opportunity.
    - Students acted not as disappointed investors of private capital, but as citizens invoking **fairness and justice**.
- **Soweto** illustrates domination, where Apartheid used schooling and examinations as instruments of **racial domination**.
- In each case, education, particularly the public examination that culminated it, ceased to represent legitimate membership in the political community.

## Constitutional Compact

- India’s examination crisis is not merely an administrative failure to be solved through **digital safeguards** or harsher penalties.
- Examinations became part of a **constitutional compact** promising fairness in an unequal society.
- The more unequal the education system, the more public examinations embody **hope**.
- They not only select students but also:
    
    - Organise aspirations.
    - Confer legitimacy.
    - Mediate the relationship between citizens and the state.
- The Republic must confront inequalities that precede examinations - schools divided by **language, curriculum, and resources**.
- It must also address narrowing opportunities after examinations in a labour market with too few **secure and dignified jobs**.
- Together, these conditions must sustain the political imagination that every child, regardless of where they begin, can reasonably believe that **education, ability, and effort** can still lead to **dignity, self-worth, and meaningful work**.
