## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background</mark>

The Western Ghats span six coastal states of India and are internationally recognised for their exceptional biodiversity, containing 39 biodiversity hotspot areas including national parks, reserved forests, and wildlife sanctuaries. UNESCO declared parts of this region as World Heritage Sites (WHS) in 2012, reinforcing global recognition of its ecological value.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Origin of the ESA Proposal</mark>

- Western Ghats Ecology Expert Panel (WGEEP) was constituted in 2010, chaired by ecologist Madhav Gadgil
    
- WGEEP recommended declaring 142 talukas across 44 districts of the entire Western Ghats as Ecologically Sensitive Area (ESA)
    
- The proposal sub-divided the region into three Ecologically Sensitive Zones (ESZ) to regulate human activity levels
    
- Once notified under the Environment Protection Act, 1986, the declaration would carry legal force
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Kasturirangan Revision</mark>

Following strong local and political opposition from concerned states, the central government set up a high-level working group under Krishnaswamy Kasturirangan in 2012 to review the WGEEP report.

- Restrictions were limited only to ecologically sensitive natural landscapes, not all human-inhabited areas
    
- The area under regulation was reduced to just 37% of the Western Ghats (from the much larger WGEEP proposal)
    
- Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEF&CC) issued the first draft notification based on this report in 2014

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current State Positions</mark>

States have taken differing stances on finalising the ESA notification:

- Gujarat and Goa appear to have agreed to finalisation of the notification
    
- Maharashtra has sought a fresh review of the proposal
    
- Karnataka, Kerala, and Tamil Nadu remain in ongoing discussions with the Centre
    
- A "phased rollout" or "state-wise finalisation" approach is being considered, following inclusion of this clause in the 2024 draft notification
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Karnataka Case Study</mark>

Karnataka illustrates the complexity of implementation on the ground. The state has 10 districts within the Western Ghats, with 23.4% of its population residing there, and around 20,668 square kilometres identified for ESA declaration. Regardless of which party governs the state, Karnataka has consistently opposed the proposal citing impacts on agriculture, plantations, mining, and infrastructure development.

A field study covering villages near 10 World Heritage Sites across Chikkamagaluru, Kodagu, Shivamogga, and Udupi districts, plus sites in Uttara Kannada (including Kali Tiger Reserve, Mundigekere Bird Sanctuary, and Mookambika Wildlife Sanctuary), revealed several ground-level concerns:

- Some residents near Kali Tiger Reserve and Kudremukh National Park have accepted or considered rehabilitation packages
    
- Villages with granted forest rights still face restrictions on minor forest produce collection and agricultural practices
    
- Forest rights claims of long-term non-tribal residents (some over 150 years, e.g., near Balahalli Reserved Forest) have been rejected in certain cases
    
- Local officials and communities support regulation of harmful activities like stone quarrying and unplanned tourism projects (e.g., forest ropeways)
    
- Widespread fear exists that buffer zones extend 10 km from core areas, risking eviction
    
- Stakeholders criticised reliance on satellite imagery, arguing it cannot distinguish plantation crops (arecanut, shade-grown coffee, rubber, coconut) from natural forest cover
    
- Concerns were raised that no committees physically visited affected villages
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Protecting Indigenous Communities</mark>

A key policy recommendation is including indigenous communities living deep within forests under the ESA framework, since they naturally follow conservation principles and maintain sustainable relationships with their environment. Their presence is seen as a vital bridge between forest ecosystems and mainstream society, and their displacement risks eroding biocultural diversity.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaways</mark>

- Conservation delay has costs: prolonged inaction risks states losing administrative control since ecological systems transcend political boundaries
    
- The core policy tension is balancing ecology, livelihoods, and local/indigenous rights
    
- Technology-based assessment (satellite imagery) has credibility gaps that ground-level verification could address
    
- The debate exemplifies federal tensions in environmental governance, relevant to Centre-State relations in polity studies
