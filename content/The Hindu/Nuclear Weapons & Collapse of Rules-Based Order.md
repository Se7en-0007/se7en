## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context and Core Argument</mark>

Eighty-one years after Hiroshima (August 6, 1945), the article argues the world is again approaching a nuclear tipping point due to the erosion of the "rules-based world order." The author contends that the most powerful nations, especially the U.S, are undermining the very order they created, while weaker states remain powerless against militarized responses to conflicts in West Asia and Ukraine.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Global Nuclear Landscape</mark>

- Declared nuclear powers: Russia and the U.S. are officially recognized nuclear weapons states.
    
- Undeclared power: Israel maintains "strategic ambiguity" — widely believed to possess nuclear weapons but never officially declaring so, with no oversight by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA).
    
- Emerging concern: Iran is seen as potentially seeking nuclear capability given current regional tensions.
    
- Non-NPT nuclear states: India, Pakistan, North Korea, and Israel never signed the NPT but possess weapons.
    
- Total nuclear-armed nations: Only nine countries out of nearly 200 in the world possess nuclear weapons.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT)</mark>

- Structure: Divides the world into nuclear "haves" (the P5 — U.S., Russia, China, U.K., France) and "have-nots."
    
- Three pillars: Non-proliferation, peaceful uses of nuclear energy, and disarmament commitments (moving toward abolition).
    
- Review cycle: The NPT undergoes periodic reviews; 2026 marks its 11th review conference, described in the article as largely a "formality."
    
- Compliance gap: Most nations adhered to non-proliferation and peaceful-use commitments, but the P5 never pursued genuine abolition, instead modernizing arsenals while reducing numbers statistically.
    
- Withdrawal precedent: North Korea signed the NPT but withdrew from it in 2003.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Deterrence Is Failing</mark>

The article critiques the classical strategic theory of deterrence, arguing it holds little value when state leadership is despotic or bellicose, since wars have continued unabated despite deterrence doctrines. Israel's undeclared nuclear status is highlighted as especially destabilizing for West Asia, a region already prone to recurring conflict.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Proposed Solution</mark>

- Move beyond NPT reviews: The author argues that periodic NPT reviews, whose core aims are violated with impunity, cannot resolve the crisis.
    
- Total abolition treaty: Calls for a new global treaty aimed at completely abolishing nuclear weapons, not just limiting proliferation.
    
- Redirect nuclear power: Advocates channeling nuclear technology toward peaceful, developmental uses benefiting all nations rather than weapons stockpiling.
    
- Collective leverage: Suggests the "have-not" majority (191 of ~200 countries) should exert enough collective pressure to compel the nine nuclear states to disarm.
