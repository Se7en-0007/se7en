A quick orientation before the details: this editorial critiques a 9-judge Supreme Court Bench ruling (August 20, 2026) that reviewed the classic "Triple Test" for defining "industry" under labour law, and argues the Court erred in discarding it as an interpretive anchor.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of the Case</mark>

- The case traces back to Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board vs A. Rajappa (BWSSB), 1978, where Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer laid down the "Triple Test" for interpreting Section 2(j) of the Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 (ID Act).
    
- A 5-judge Bench in State of U.P. vs Jai Bir Singh (2005) doubted this definition; the matter was later escalated by a 7-judge Bench to the current 9-judge Bench.
    
- The 9-judge Bench, led by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant, delivered its verdict on August 20, 2026.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Triple Test Explained</mark>

Three cumulative conditions must be met for an activity to qualify as an "industry":

- Systematic activity is carried out.
    
- There is employer-employee cooperation.
    
- The activity involves production or distribution of goods/services to satisfy human wants (excluding purely religious or spiritual wants).
    

Additional features of the test:

- Profit motive is irrelevant - what matters is the nature of the activity itself.
    
- Only "sovereign functions" of the state are excluded from this definition.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Ruling of the 9-Judge Bench</mark>

- The Triple Test is left standing for all pending disputes under the old ID Act.
    
- The ID Act itself was repealed on November 21, 2025, when the Industrial Relations Code, 2020 (IRC) came into force.
    
- Crucially, the majority ruled that BWSSB will not serve as a "sheet anchor" for interpreting Section 2(p) of the IRC - i.e., the new code's definition of industry should not be automatically read through the lens of the old Triple Test precedent.
    
- The editorial characterizes this specific move (severing BWSSB from IRC interpretation) as "a mistake."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Dissenting Opinion</mark>

- Justice B.V. Nagarathna dissented, holding that the reference to the larger Bench was itself unnecessary and that the Triple Test required no interference.
    
- Three other judges shared this dissenting view.
    
- Nagarathna's reasoning: since 1978, and especially after the 1991 liberalisation and privatisation reforms, far more workers have shifted to the private sector, away from the security of public employment.
    
- Therefore, an expansive definition of "industry" (as the Triple Test provides) is even more necessary today as a protective bulwark for workers.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Editorial's Core Argument</mark>

The editorial's central critique rests on textual continuity between the old and new laws:

- Section 2(p) of the IRC does not actually depart from Krishna Iyer's Triple Test formula - it reproduces much of its essence.
    
- Given this continuity, the editorial argues it is difficult to justify why BWSSB should be set aside as an interpretive guide when Section 2(p) itself comes up for interpretation.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Triple Test Was Not Only Pro-Worker</mark>

An important nuance highlighted in the piece: the Triple Test balanced interests on both sides.

- An expansive "industry" definition brought ID Act protections for workers, but also imposed restrictions.
    
- For employers: it offered a regulated route to retrenchment and closure.
    
- For workers: it imposed a bar on striking at will.
    
- In essence, the test enabled industrial peace rather than serving worker welfare alone - a mutual-restraint framework.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Consequences of the Ruling</mark>

- By severing the Triple Test principle from the IRC, the majority has cut away the interpretive framework that historically allowed industry-related disputes to be resolved consistently.
    
- Since the essence of the Triple Test is still embedded in Section 2(p), the editorial argues courts and tribunals now bear responsibility to ensure that a mere change of statute (ID Act to IRC) is not mistakenly read as a change of legislative intent.
    
- This must be done even though the majority judgment removed the formal anchor (BWSSB precedent) that would have made this continuity explicit.
