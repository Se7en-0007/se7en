Lie detection tests (narco analysis, polygraph, and BEAP) sit at the intersection of criminal investigation and constitutional rights, particularly the right against self-incrimination (Article 20(3)) and personal liberty (Article 21). The recent NEET UG 2026 paper leak case has brought renewed attention to how Indian courts regulate these techniques.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Types of Lie Detection Tests</mark>

Three main scientific techniques are used by investigative agencies to extract information from suspects:

- Narco analysis: Involves administering drugs to induce a state where the subject makes involuntary revelations
    
- Polygraph examination: Measures physiological responses (heart rate, blood pressure, perspiration) to detect deception
    
- Brain Electrical Activation Profile (BEAP)/Brain Mapping: Analyzes brain activity to assess familiarity with facts related to a crime
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The NEET UG 2026 Case Trigger</mark>

Three accused persons in the NEET UG 2026 paper leak case volunteered to undergo polygraph and brain mapping tests, claiming this would establish their innocence and help the CBI trace the real culprits. Special Judge Ajay Gupta of the Rouse Avenue Court, New Delhi, rejected this plea on August 10, calling it "sheer abuse of the process of law" filed to delay proceedings, especially since the CBI had already filed its chargesheet and required no further assistance from the accused.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Landmark Judgment: Selvi v State of Karnataka (2010)</mark>

This is the foundational Supreme Court ruling on lie detection tests in India.

- Core holding: Narco analysis, polygraph, and BEAP tests cannot be administered without the subject's free consent
    
- Constitutional basis: Compulsory administration violates Article 20(3) (right against self-incrimination) and Article 21 (personal liberty)
    
- Mental privacy rationale: These techniques intrude on mental privacy in ways ordinary questioning does not, because the subject does not consciously choose the answers that emerge
    
- Human rights angle: The court equated compulsory administration with "cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment" under evolving international human rights norms
    
- Key distinction: Unlike voluntary statements or conventional interrogation, these tests bypass the subject's conscious control over their responses
    
- Coercion risk: The court noted that a person could make an incriminating statement merely on being threatened with prospective administration of these techniques
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Conditions for Voluntary Administration</mark>

Lie detection tests are not completely barred; they can be conducted voluntarily if certain safeguards are met.

|Safeguard|Requirement|
|---|---|
|Consent recording|Must be recorded before a judicial magistrate|
|Legal advice|Subject must have access to advice on medical, emotional, and legal consequences|
|Guidelines followed|Must comply with the National Human Rights Commission's 2000 guidelines|
|Evidentiary status|Even with consent, test results alone cannot be admitted as evidence|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Test Administration vs Reliance on Results</mark>

Even when all safeguards are satisfied, the Supreme Court draws a critical distinction between conducting the test and using its results as evidence.

- Since the subject is not in conscious control while responding, results cannot serve as substantive proof of guilt
    
- Section 23 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam (BSA) bars using confessions made to police in custody as evidence against the accused
    
- Exception: Confessions leading to discovery of a physical fact (object, location, or document) fall under the "fact discovered" exception
    
- Even discovered facts function only as corroboration, not as standalone proof
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Amlesh Kumar v State of Bihar (2025)</mark>

This more recent ruling clarifies whether an accused can insist on voluntarily undergoing such tests.

- The Supreme Court set aside a Patna High Court order allowing narco-analysis on all accused and witnesses in a dowry harassment case at the bail stage
    
- Bench: Justices Sanjay Karol and Prasanna B Varale
    
- At the bail stage, courts should only examine the nature of the crime, allegations, custody period, and risk of witness influence — not conduct a "roving enquiry" into involuntary investigative techniques
    
- Even a voluntary request should be examined at the appropriate trial stage, specifically when the accused exercises the right to lead defence evidence
    
- Undergoing narco-analysis is not part of the "indefeasible right to lead evidence," given its inherently unreliable nature
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Judicial Discretion Framework</mark>

Courts ultimately retain broad discretion when deciding whether to permit these tests, weighing three factors together: the presence of free and informed consent, compliance with the laid-down procedural safeguards, and whether the test is genuinely necessary given the specific facts of the case. This layered approach ensures that lie detection remains an exception rather than a routine investigative tool, balancing individual rights against the needs of criminal investigation.
