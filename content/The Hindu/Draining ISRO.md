The article argues that ISRO's excellence is eroding not from external competition but from how the Indian state has treated the organisation over the past decade - extracting value from it rather than nurturing its capacity.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Crisis Indicators</mark>

- Recent high-profile launch anomalies including PSLV-C61 and NVS-02 missions, alongside an unusually opaque public response to failures
    
- ISRO's launch frequency and scientific output have stagnated even as commercial and policy initiatives around the space sector have expanded
    
- The Department of Space confirmed in early July 2026 that Group-A scientific and technical staff working on Gaganyaan and other key missions have submitted a spate of resignation and voluntary retirement requests
    
- Instead of investigating why staff are leaving, the official response was an administrative memo stating such resignations should no longer be accepted routinely
    
- NavIC (India's regional satellite navigation system) is described as "practically defunct" due to atomic clock failures, and several 2026 launches are already delayed
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Start-ups Cannot Substitute for ISRO</mark>

- A strong private space sector historically grows out of a strong public ecosystem, not by replacing it
    
- The state has failed to act as an "anchor customer" for private space companies, a role it has assumed only minimally
    
- India lacks sufficient domestic demand for satellite manufacturers and launch companies to be commercially self-sustaining
    
- Venture capital funding tends to favour software-like growth trajectories, whereas launch vehicles and spacecraft need patient capital, accept uncertain returns, and require heavy regulatory oversight - needs better met by developmental finance
    
- Many private space start-ups recruit talent from ISRO itself; if ISRO stops replenishing its own talent pool, both public and private sectors suffer simultaneously
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Erosion of Tacit Knowledge and Engineering Culture</mark>

- ISRO's historical strength rested on engineers having the authority to declare when a system was genuinely "ready," rather than being pressured by external timelines
    
- As launches became politically salient national events, the engineering culture shifted to become more pressure-driven, discouraging junior engineers from raising concerns ("I think we are making a mistake")
    
- Rocket science depends heavily on tacit knowledge - for example, a propulsion engineer's memory of why a specific weld specification was adopted after an obscure vibration issue years earlier - knowledge that is rarely documented
    
- Accelerating retirements, recruitment delays, and departures of experienced personnel are eroding ISRO's traditional culture of apprenticeship and knowledge transfer
    
- Accountability has become increasingly internal and vertical over the last decade; failure analysis reports are kept out of the public domain, and public communication favours promotional messaging over technical transparency
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Structural and Institutional Gaps</mark>

|Requirement for a healthy space ecosystem|Current status in India|
|---|---|
|Technically excellent public agency|Weakening due to staffing and autonomy issues|
|Universities producing specialised talent|Insufficient pipeline noted|
|Public research laboratories|Limited reinforcement|
|Reliable procurement|Not established as strong support|
|Long-term/patient financing|Lacking; VC favours short-term software-style returns|
|Domestic customer demand|Insufficient for satellite/launch firms to subsist|
|Suppliers managing cyclical demand|Ecosystem not mature|

- The comparison is drawn to NASA, Roscosmos, and Boeing's commercial aircraft division, all of which experienced similar institutional decline patterns that were hard to diagnose early but became obvious later
    
- India may have liberalised its space economy before building the industrial ecosystem such liberalisation requires - described as putting the "cart before the horse"
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Extractive State Argument</mark>

- The decline is framed as gradual and avoidable, driven by cumulative political and administrative choices that demanded more output from ISRO without strengthening the institution
    
- Launch cadence requirements increased without corresponding growth in engineering capacity
    
- Strategic responsibilities multiplied without proportional workforce expansion
    
- When scientists began resigning, the response restricted resignations rather than addressing root causes
    
- When missions failed, transparency decreased rather than increased
    
- Commercial push into the space sector proceeded without first strengthening the anchor public institution
    
- A "productive state" enlarges institutional capacity through funding, autonomy, legal authority, stable careers, and political cover during failures; an "extractive state," by contrast, consumes institutional capacity to meet immediate fiscal, political, and strategic goals
    
- The core conclusion: institutions do not sustain themselves automatically - without deliberate support, they risk cannibalising their own accumulated capabilities
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Likely Consequences If Uncorrected</mark>

- A structurally weakening ISRO
    
- A cluster of capable but relatively small private space firms unable to scale
    
- Continued dependence on the state or foreign suppliers for advanced space technologies
    
- Fewer scientists choosing long-term careers in the public space sector
    
- A progressively fragmented national space ecosystem
