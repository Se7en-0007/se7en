## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context of The Article</mark>

The editorial discusses the Patna High Court's August 14 order on noise pollution, arising from the case Surendra Prasad vs State of Bihar, first heard in February 2025. The case centers on DJ trolleys and loudspeakers being identified as a major source of noise pollution in Patna, with Justice Rajiv Roy leading the judicial intervention.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Timeline of Judicial Escalation</mark>

- February 2025: Patna High Court hearing begins; Justice Rajiv Roy acknowledges DJ trolleys and loudspeakers as major noise sources in Patna.
    
- Court criticises the Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB) for failing to curb the noise menace.
    
- Justice Roy directs BSPCB to obtain police reports on permissions granted to noise-source operators and action taken against violators.
    
- October (2025): Police report seizure of equipment and levying of fines over three months, but only in Patna, Barh, and Fatuha - not in Masaurhi.
    
- Justice Roy calls the Masaurhi picture "unbelievable," implying selective or absent enforcement rather than genuine absence of violations.
    
- During hearings, Justice Roy also summons police officers and criticises "boilerplate affidavits" submitted by authorities.
    
- August 14, 2026: High Court issues Statewide directives on high-decibel noise emission - described as the culmination of this escalation "for now."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Directives Issued by The Court</mark>

- Shift from complaints-based enforcement to routine/proactive enforcement by authorities.
    
- Mandatory registration of DJs, sound-system operators, and event halls with subdivisional authorities.
    
- Loudspeakers must stop playing at 9.55 p.m. - five minutes before the legal cut-off of 10 p.m. - to prevent the common excuse of "just a few more minutes" past the deadline.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Constitutional and Legal Angle</mark>

- Courts have repeatedly held that citizens have a right under Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty) to be protected from unlawful noise.
    
- This reflects the expansive judicial interpretation of Article 21, extending it beyond mere survival to quality of life and a pollution-free environment.
    
- The editorial notes that despite comprehensive laws already existing on noise pollution, enforcement remains "dismal" - indicating an implementation gap rather than a legislative one.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Hindu's Core Argument</mark>

- Enforcement of noise pollution laws has been "episodic," and this episodic nature has itself become "endemic" - a chronic pattern rather than an occasional lapse.
    
- In this vacuum, the Patna High Court stepping in as a quasi-regulator is described as "creditable," but only as a stopgap measure, not a long-term solution.
    
- The editorial calls it "absurd" to expect citizens to file complaints for every instance of loud DJs or honking before police can act - reinforcing the need for proactive rather than reactive regulation.
    
- Registration of operators is highlighted as a mechanism to ensure equal regulation of all violators, eliminating selective and arbitrary enforcement (as seen in the Masaurhi anomaly).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Socio-Political Challenges Identified</mark>

- Loud noise commonly originates from ordinary social events: festivals, weddings, political campaigns, and religious events.
    
- Enforcing noise rules creates friction with citizens who perceive a right to practise their culture and traditions as they see fit.
    
- Governments often have a political incentive to tolerate noise violations rather than antagonise constituents/voters - a governance-versus-populism tension.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Editorial's Concluding Assessment</mark>

- The success of the High Court's intervention should ultimately be judged not by the order itself, but by whether the State (Bihar government and its agencies) develops a consistent habit of enforcement going forward.
    
- This frames judicial activism as necessary but insufficient - sustainable change requires institutional and administrative follow-through, not just court-driven compliance.
