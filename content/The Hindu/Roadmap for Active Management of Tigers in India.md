## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Finding of The Report</mark>

The National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) released a report titled "Roadmap for Active Management of Tigers in India," which reveals serious structural problems despite India's impressive headline tiger numbers. Two-thirds of India's 58 tiger reserves suffer from a declining prey base, poor habitat quality, or both, while nine reserves report zero or critically low resident tiger numbers.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Statistics</mark>

- Total tiger reserves in India: 58
    
- Total tiger population: 3,682 (about 75% of the global tiger population)
    
- Tigers inside tiger reserves: 2,338
    
- Tigers outside protected areas (higher risk exposure): 1,344
    
- Tigers concentrated in just 10 reserves: 1,318 (remainder spread across 48 reserves)
    
- Reserves with adequate prey base and quality habitat (both positive): only 20
    
- Reserves with either poor prey or poor habitat: 38
    
- Reserves lacking both adequate prey and proper habitat: 17
    
- Reserves with overall prey base problems: 36
    
- Reserves with declining tiger numbers: 25
    
- Reserves needing improvement: roughly 50%
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Basis of the Assessment</mark>

The analysis draws on the National Tiger Estimation carried out in 2022. A fresh nationwide tiger estimate was originally scheduled for 2026 but has been delayed by a year.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Contradiction Highlighted</mark>

Union Environment, Forest and Climate Change Minister Bhupender Yadav, at the Global Tiger Day meeting on July 29, 2026, projected India's tiger conservation as a success story, citing the 3,682 figure and calling it reflective of healthy forests. However, the government's own report paints a more troubling ground reality, exposing a gap between the official narrative and on-the-ground habitat/prey conditions.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Reserves With Zero or Critically Low Tigers</mark>

**Zero resident tigers recorded in:**

- Dampa (Mizoram)
    
- Kawal (Telangana)
    
- Satkosia (Odisha)
    
- Sahyadri (Maharashtra)
    
- Kamlang (Arunachal Pradesh)
    

**Critically low presence (1 or fewer tigers):**

- Palamu (Jharkhand) — a single individual captured on camera trap
    
- Buxa (West Bengal) — presence confirmed via a single photograph; experts suspect this tiger actually migrated from Bhutan rather than being a resident
    

**Additional reserves believed to have extremely few or no tigers:**

- Indravati, Udanti Sitanadi, and Guru Ghasidas-Tamor Pingla — all located in Chhattisgarh
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">States and Reserves Performing Well</mark>

Tiger scientist and Wildlife Institute of India (WII) professor Qamar Qureshi noted that recovery has occurred in reserves and states that implemented the NTCA-WII roadmap effectively. States cited as performing well include Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Uttarakhand, Bihar, Assam, and West Bengal's Sundarbans.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Expert Recommendations</mark>

|Issue|Recommended Solution|
|---|---|
|Weak funding for reserves|Enhanced financial allocation|
|Poor scientific basis for management|Prioritise science-based solutions|
|Invasive species spread|Removal of invasive species|
|Low prey numbers|Ungulate recovery and augmentation|
|Weak enforcement|Better on-ground protection|
|Fragmented landscapes|Recognition and improvement of tiger corridors connecting reserves and other protected areas|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Root Cause: Reserves Created "For Optics"</mark>

Pradeep Vyas, retired Chief Wildlife Warden of West Bengal and known as the state's "tiger man" for his Sundarbans experience, stated that many reserves had habitat quality issues right from their inception. He claimed several reserves were created by states merely for optics, without ensuring appropriate habitat conditions. Vyas also noted that restoring habitat and prey base to an optimum threshold takes more than a decade, and only after anthropogenic (human-caused) pressures are brought under control.
