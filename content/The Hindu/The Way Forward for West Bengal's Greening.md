## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

West Bengal's Chief Minister has announced a target of planting 7.2 crore saplings in 2026 under the Aranya Saptaha and Ek Ped Maa Ke Naam initiatives, explicitly framed as a response to Uttar Pradesh's 26-crore plantation pledge. This has turned tree-planting into a high-visibility inter-State political contest, though it comes with welcome commitments like two years of promised aftercare and stricter anti-felling measures.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Argument: One-Size-Fits-All Fails</mark>

The article's central critique is that forestry policy cannot be uniformly copied across States with different ecological realities.

- West Bengal has 18.96% forest cover - nearly three times that of Uttar Pradesh
    
- Uttar Pradesh's landscape is plains-dominated with low forest density
    
- West Bengal has complex, fragile ecosystems: Himalayan foothills, lateritic sal tracts, and tidal Sundarbans mangroves
    
- West Bengal's ecological health depends on biodiversity preservation, not just adding block plantations
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">West Bengal's Existing Legacy</mark>

The State already possesses a proven, successful model that predates the current plantation drive.

- Bankura and Arabari (Paschim Medinipur) are historic sites of community-led Joint Forest Management (JFM)
    
- Arabari is considered the "Mecca of community forestry" in India
    
- The model works by having local communities protect forests while sharing in economic benefits
    
- This approach helped establish community forestry as a national pathway for ecological restoration
    
- Despite this legacy, the movement today "stands at a crossroads"
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Critical Bottlenecks to Address</mark>

For any restoration model (including active restoration with ecologically appropriate site-specific planting) to succeed, several structural gaps must be fixed first.

|Bottleneck|Detail|
|---|---|
|Nursery lead time|Quality seedlings require 18-20 months lead time for robust planting stock at scale|
|Forest guard vacancies|60.5% posts vacant (Annual Report 2022-23)|
|Forester vacancies|55.6% posts vacant|
|Ranger vacancies|32.6% posts vacant|

The human resource gap is described as equally serious as the seedling supply issue, requiring rapid recruitment combined with an institutional reset - realigning priorities, tightening oversight, and investing in training, equipment, and field support as core interventions rather than afterthoughts.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Reviving Community Institutions</mark>

To make restoration participatory and durable, the article calls for revitalising Joint Forest Management Committees (JFMCs).

- Nearly 4,000 JFMCs currently manage about 5-6 lakh hectares
    
- These involve several lakh forest-fringe households
    
- Despite this scale, many committees have gone dormant as their "pro-people ethos" has eroded
    
- Solution: back existing JFMCs with clear, legally guaranteed economic incentives
    
- Solution: mobilise more agile grassroots platforms such as women-led Self-Help Groups (SHGs)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Accountability and Transparency Measures</mark>

The article proposes concrete mechanisms to fix the "pervasive lack of accountability" in plantation programmes.

- Every restoration site must be geo-tagged with precise spatial coordinates
    
- Data must be logged on a public web dashboard
    
- Plant survival rates must be verified through mandatory annual audits
    
- Audits should be led by independent academic institutions, eliminating departmental self-reporting
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Financial Mechanism: The 30/70 Rule</mark>

A structured green budgeting rule is proposed to ensure predictable, effective finance utilisation.

- 30% of allocation: for initial restoration work, including planting
    
- 70% of allocation: for subsequent maintenance, including protection, to secure long-term survival and ecological outcomes
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Concluding Philosophy</mark>

The piece closes on the principle that true forestry success is measured differently than political metrics suggest.

- Success should not be measured in saplings planted, but in forests that survive
    
- Recommends shifting focus from top-down plantation drives to restoration processes
    
- Recommends shifting from bureaucratic metrics to genuine community partnership
    
- Forests should be treated as living systems requiring patience, science, and community participation - not political milestones
