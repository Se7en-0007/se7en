This op-ed by Milinda Moragoda argues that regulation and bans alone cannot address the challenges social media poses to young people, and instead calls for India to draw on its civilisational and cultural resources to build resilience in the digital age.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context and Central Question</mark>

- The global debate on banning social media for minors is becoming a defining digital-age policy issue.
    
- Countries have taken different approaches: Australia has implemented restrictions, the U.K. has proposed measures, and the U.S. has adopted varied state-level responses.
    
- India faces a choice between importing regulatory models from elsewhere or developing an approach rooted in its own traditions.
    
- The core question posed is not just about regulation but whether it alone can prepare the next generation for a world increasingly shaped by AI and digital technologies.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Acknowledged Risks of Social Media</mark>

- Manipulation of young users through algorithms and targeted content.
    
- Addiction arising from compulsive engagement patterns.
    
- Bullying and harassment facilitated by online anonymity and reach.
    
- Exposure to harmful or age-inappropriate content.
    
- These risks are real and deserve serious attention, but the author argues they don't automatically justify outright bans.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Fear as a Flawed Policy Driver</mark>

- Every major communications revolution in history has triggered societal anxiety: the printing press, radio, television, and the early Internet all provoked similar fears.
    
- The persistent challenge has never been whether information should flow, but how society encourages its responsible use.
    
- Today's disruption is magnified by scale and speed - the smartphone now combines the functions of printing press, library, marketplace, classroom, and public square.
    
- Social media, AI, and digital technologies together represent perhaps the biggest societal transformation since the Industrial Revolution.
    
- Young people face this transition amid geopolitical uncertainty, widening inequality, and instability, making fear a potent political force.
    
- Policymakers are cautioned against letting fear-based reactions to child safety concerns morph into a broader resistance to innovation itself.
    
- The real challenge is not stopping technological change but building institutions capable of managing it.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Limits of Regulatory Bans</mark>

- Governments are trying to erect barriers against technologies that are becoming increasingly sophisticated, personalised, and hard to control.
    
- The contest between regulation and innovation is inherently unequal, favouring innovation.
    
- Circumvention tools - VPNs, encrypted apps, alternative platforms, and future AI-driven tools - will make bypassing restrictions progressively easier.
    
- The more relevant question is reframed: not how to keep young people away from technology, but how to prepare them to live with it.
    
- The debate itself may already be outdated, since the next generation will engage not just with social media platforms but with intelligent AI systems capable of educating, entertaining, mentoring - and potentially manipulating.
    
- This signals that the underlying challenge is far larger in scope than social media regulation alone.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legitimate Role of Government</mark>

The author does not dismiss government responsibility entirely, listing specific areas where regulation is appropriate:

- Demanding greater transparency from platforms.
    
- Establishing clear standards for child protection.
    
- Combating criminal activity conducted through digital platforms.
    
- Encouraging responsible innovation rather than blanket restriction.
    
- However, regulation alone is insufficient since the issue is fundamentally social, cultural, and civilisational in nature.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Civilisational Alternative: Drawing from Roots</mark>

- For centuries, Indian society developed mechanisms to transmit values, wisdom, and social responsibility across generations.
    
- Family structures, religious traditions, and educational institutions historically helped young people navigate periods of change and uncertainty.
    
- The guru-shishya tradition is highlighted as a particularly enduring model - it went beyond knowledge transmission to cultivate character, self-discipline, and a sense of responsibility.
    
- The proposed approach is not to recreate the past but to adapt its strengths to 21st-century realities.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Proposed Multi-Stakeholder Response</mark>

|Stakeholder|Recommended Role|
|---|---|
|Parents|Become more digitally literate and actively engaged in children's online lives|
|Schools|Teach digital citizenship, critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and resilience - not just technical skills|
|Religious/cultural organisations|Engage with technology rather than retreat from it|
|Technology companies, educators, civil society|Collaborate to encourage responsible online behaviour|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Concluding Argument</mark>

- Just as earlier generations learned to navigate the physical world responsibly, future generations must learn to navigate the digital world responsibly.
    
- The author frames this as fundamentally an educational and cultural challenge, not merely a technological one.
    
- India's civilisational strength has historically been its ability to absorb powerful external influences without losing its cultural core.
    
- As one of the world's oldest civilisations with a rapidly growing digital society, India is positioned to demonstrate that technological progress and cultural continuity can coexist.
    
- The article's closing thesis: social media challenges will not be resolved by "building a bigger firewall," but by combining safeguards with human institutions that shape judgment, resilience, and responsibility.
