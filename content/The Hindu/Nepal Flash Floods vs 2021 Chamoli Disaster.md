## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Event Overview</mark>

- Trigger event: Avalanche-induced flash floods in Rasuwa district, Nepal (Wednesday, August 26, 2026).
- Nature: Ice and rock avalanche — large ice block of a glacier breaking away and falling downhill, causing a surge in mountainous rivers.
- Toll (as reported): At least 150 confirmed dead, ~1,500 people missing; 60-70 bridges swept away; hundreds of houses/buildings destroyed.
- Expert view: Farooq Azam (Associate Professor, IIT Indore; heads Cryosphere Group at ICIMOD, Kathmandu) — event resembles Chamoli in mechanism but Kedarnath in scale of downstream destruction.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Seismic Confusion — Key Fact</mark>

- USGS initially reported a magnitude 4.4 earthquake as the trigger.
- USGS later revised this: the seismic signal was generated by a glacial collapse and debris flow, not an earthquake.
- Basis of revision: additional analysis of nearby seismic stations, long-period seismic waves, and satellite imagery.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Comparing the Three Himalayan Disasters</mark>

|Feature|Kedarnath (2013)|Chamoli (2021)|Nepal Floods (2026)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Trigger mechanism|GLOF (Glacial Lake Outburst Flood)|Ice and rock avalanche|Ice and rock avalanche|
|Seasonal timing|Monsoon|February (non-monsoon)|Peak monsoon|
|River condition at time|Near capacity|Low water levels|Near capacity (saturated soil)|
|Approx. deaths|High (thousands)|~200 (many hydro-project workers)|150+ confirmed, ~1,500 missing|
|Scale of destruction|Very high|Moderate-high|Very high (compared to Kedarnath-level)|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Nepal's Disaster Could Be Worse Than Chamoli</mark>

- Timing: Chamoli occurred in February when river discharge was low; Nepal's event struck during peak monsoon.
- Saturated ground: Soil already saturated and rivers already carrying near-capacity water before the avalanche hit.
- Cascading chain reaction: A surging river carrying large rocks/ice erodes banks and slopes, pulling in more soil, mud, and rock — amplifying the flood.
- Confined terrain: Himalayan valleys give floodwaters no room to spread out, concentrating destructive energy downstream.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Concept Definitions</mark>

- GLOF (Glacial Lake Outburst Flood): Sudden release of large water volumes when a glacial lake overflows or breaches its boundary (mechanism behind Kedarnath 2013 and Sikkim 2023).
- Ice/Rock Avalanche: Large block of glacier ice (with rock/debris) breaking off and falling downhill, triggering river surges (mechanism behind Chamoli 2021 and Nepal 2026).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Case Study: 2023 Sikkim Floods (Cascading Disaster Model)</mark>

- Trigger: Collapse of a glacier chunk into South Lhonak Lake (over 5,000 metres above sea level, north Sikkim) on October 3, 2023.
- Landslide volume: ~14.7 million cubic metres of rock, soil, and snow collapsed into the lake.
- Mechanism: Generated a 20-metre-high displacement wave that overtopped and breached the frontal frozen moraine.
- Water release: ~50 million cubic metres of water suddenly drained.
- Cascading effect: GLOF eroded ~270 million cubic metres of sediment and triggered 45 secondary landslides.
- Erosion zone: Most erosion occurred within ~67.5 km of the lake.
- Published in: Science journal (study cited in article).
- Policy response: National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) initiated a study of glacial lakes to strengthen boundaries and install early-warning sensors for leakage/water accumulation.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Broader Himalayan Vulnerability (Expert Analysis)</mark>

- Azam's assessment: The Himalayan region is "out of balance entirely" due to combined population pressure and climate change.
- Climate change link: Increasing extreme weather events are exposing newer areas to risk.
- Multiple triggers: Both earthquakes and heavy rainfall events are common triggers, either of which can set off avalanche/GLOF-type disasters.
- Fragility: The Himalayan ecosystem's fragility means a single triggering event can cascade into much larger destruction than the initial cause suggests.
