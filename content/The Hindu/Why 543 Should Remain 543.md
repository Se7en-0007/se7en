## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context and Trigger</mark>

- Monsoon session 2026 adjourned sine die on August 13, 2026 but not yet formally prorogued, fuelling speculation Parliament may reconvene for a constitutional amendment on delimitation and women's reservation.
- Central question: Does delimitation necessarily require Lok Sabha enlargement?

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Constitutional Framework</mark>

- Article 81: representation must "so far as practicable" correspond to population.
- Article 82: provides for readjustment of seats after every Census.
- 42nd Amendment (1976): froze inter-State seat allocation based on 1971 Census so States with successful family planning weren't penalised politically.
- 84th Amendment: extended this freeze until figures from the first Census after 2026 are published.
- Key point: Parliament has twice prioritised federal/demographic-policy fairness over strict population-based arithmetic.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Proportional" Increase Fallacy</mark>

- Government reportedly proposed a ~50% across-the-board seat increase, preserving each State's proportion of total seats.
- Problem: proportions don't matter in practice — Parliament votes in absolute numbers, not ratios.
- Example: If UP rises from 80 to 120 seats and Tamil Nadu from 39 to ~59, the ratio stays same but the absolute gap widens from 41 MPs to ~61 MPs.
- This numerical widening affects government formation, confidence motions, and legislative/constitutional amendment voting power.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Delimitation Without Enlargement</mark>

- Misconception: delimitation and House enlargement are inseparable — they are not.
- Precedent: Delimitation after the 2001 Census redrew constituencies within States without changing each State's total seat allocation.
- Proposed approach: Use 2027 Census data to redraw constituencies within States (reflecting urbanisation/demographic shifts) without touching the 543 cap or inter-State balance.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Bigger House, Weaker Deliberation</mark>

- Parliamentary time is fixed — doesn't expand with member count.
- Even at 543, many MPs get limited opportunities to speak/ask questions.
- An 800+ member House would dilute these opportunities further, hurting quality of deliberation.
- Indian MPs already represent the largest average constituencies among major democracies (multiples of US/UK constituency sizes).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Alternative: Strengthen State Assemblies</mark>

- Accessibility problem (large constituencies) is real, but additional representatives need not sit in Parliament.
- Most citizen-representative interactions concern roads, schools, hospitals, local/State administration — matters within State jurisdiction.
- Solution: Expand Vidhan Sabhas (more MLAs, smaller Assembly constituencies) instead of Lok Sabha.
- Crucial advantage: Assembly expansion faces no federal-arithmetic problem since each State answers only to its own population — doesn't disturb inter-State Parliamentary balance.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Women's Reservation — No Need to Link with Enlargement</mark>

- One-third reservation for women ≈ 181 seats within the existing 543-seat House — achievable without expansion.
- Reservation's purpose: change who occupies seats, not create new ones.
- Risk of 50% expansion: while women MPs increase, it simultaneously deepens dominance of already-larger States — fixing one representational imbalance while worsening another.
- Argument: Women's representation and federal fairness are not mutually exclusive; both achievable within 543.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Proposed Solution (Five-Point Package)</mark>

1. Retain Lok Sabha at 543 seats.
2. Preserve present inter-State seat distribution.
3. Undertake delimitation within States using updated (2027 Census) population data.
4. Implement women's reservation within the existing 543-seat structure.
5. Expand Vidhan Sabhas to absorb accessibility pressure from population growth.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Federal Equity Argument</mark>

- Southern States invested heavily in education, healthcare, and family welfare, achieving lower fertility as nationally encouraged.
- Rewarding faster-population-growth States with proportionally larger political weight would invert the incentive structure the 1976 freeze deliberately created.
- Central question reframed: not whether population matters, but whether it should mechanically override a federal balance consciously preserved for five decades.
