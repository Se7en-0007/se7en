## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

A volatile political climate in Washington has caused strategic anxiety in New Delhi. The Trump administration's transactional approach has included punitive tariffs on steel and aluminium, withdrawal of India's preferential trade status, renewed U.S.-Pakistan ties, and tightened H-1B visa rules. This has fuelled a domestic push, led by a business lobby, for a fundamental reset of India's China policy.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Business Lobby's Argument (Critiqued)</mark>

- Claims India's confrontational China stance is overly synchronised with Washington's "containment" agenda rather than India's own interests.
    
- Argues India's deep reliance on Chinese technology, supply chains, industrial inputs, and capital means anti-China posture hurts India's growth more than China's economy.
    
- Warns that Washington's history of tactical flip-flops (escalation followed by bilateral deals) leaves an overextended India vulnerable to Chinese retaliation.
    
- Critique: This treats trade as apolitical, ignoring national power realities; a hasty economic embrace of China is deemed a "dangerous prescription."

Other coercive tactics include renaming Arunachal Pradesh's geographical features, issuing stapled visas to J&K and Arunachal residents, and weaponising monopolies over rare earths and tunnel-boring machines.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Myth of Reciprocity"</mark>

- Core argument: China does not view India as a peer, and easing India's economic guard will not translate into geopolitical goodwill.
    
- China has repeatedly used its UNSC veto to shield Pakistan-based terror groups and has blocked India's bids for UNSC permanent membership and NSG entry.
    
- India's trade deficit with China already exceeds 100 billion dollars annually; further opening would deepen this asymmetry.
    
- Concern: Chinese dominance over critical components could act as an "economic kill-switch," paralysing Indian industry during a future crisis.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Strategic Logic on Leverage</mark>

- China is argued to "respect power and exploit vulnerability" — surrendering economic leverage signals weakness, not conciliation.
    
- Post-Galwan restrictions (on Chinese apps, investments, telecom infrastructure) are framed as diplomatic leverage, not just emotional reactions.
    
- Dismantling these restrictions for minor economic relief is termed "unilateral disarmament."
    
- If India shows it cannot sustain a prolonged stand-off, Beijing may eventually dictate border settlement terms from a position of dominance.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Position Vis-à-Vis the U.S.</mark>

- The Trump administration's unpredictability (tariffs, visa curbs, warming to Pakistan) makes it unwise for India to depend entirely on Washington as a security guarantor.
    
- Despite outreach from U.S. emissary Sergio Gor, the U.S. will always prioritise its own domestic and global calculations first.
    
- India must recognize that on strategic matters like Ladakh, it effectively stands alone.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Recommended Middle Path</mark>

- Reject both extremes: subordinating national interest to the U.S., and unconditional economic/political surrender to China.
    
- Accelerate supply chain resilience and reduce dependency on China.
    
- Diversify trade partnerships across Europe, East Asia, and the Global South.
    
- Aggressively build domestic manufacturing capacity, even accepting short-term inflationary costs.
    
- Adopt "strategic patience" rather than panic-driven policy reversal.
