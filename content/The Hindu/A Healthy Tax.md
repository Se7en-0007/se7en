## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: The Nutrition Paradox</mark>

India, historically focused on tackling undernutrition, now faces the parallel and rising challenge of overnutrition driven by changing lifestyles.

- National Family Health Survey (NFHS) data shows stunting and undernutrition persist despite gains, but adult overweight and obesity are rising sharply.
    
- Diet-related metabolic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease) are increasingly significant public health concerns.
    
- This creates a "double burden of malnutrition" — undernutrition and overnutrition coexisting in the same population.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Two Key Recent Developments</mark>

Two unrelated but complementary developments have brought unhealthy diets into policy focus.

- **Parliamentary Standing Committee recommendation**: The Standing Committee on Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution recommended mandatory front-of-pack nutrition labelling (FOPL) to flag foods high in sugar, and also proposed including sugar content disclosure on baby foods specifically.
    
- **National consortium recommendation**: A consortium named "Let's Fix Our Food," led by ICMR-National Institute of Nutrition (ICMR-NIN) along with other prominent Indian and international institutions, has advised the government to levy a health tax on foods high in fat, salt and sugar (HFSS foods) and impose stricter advertisement regulations.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Policy Recommendations</mark>

The consortium's broader strategy for adolescent and child nutrition includes multiple pillars beyond taxation.

|Recommendation|Target Area|
|---|---|
|Health tax on HFSS foods|Pricing/consumption deterrent|
|Front-of-pack nutrition labelling|Consumer awareness|
|Stricter regulation of unhealthy food marketing/advertising|Especially targeting children|
|Healthier school food environment|Institutional/school policy|
|Sugar content disclosure in baby foods|Early-life nutrition|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Global Precedents: Health Taxes Worldwide</mark>

The World Health Organization (WHO) notes that since 2017, at least 133 countries have introduced or increased a health tax on unhealthy foods, showing this is a globally recognized fiscal-health tool.

- **Colombia**: Introduced a "junk food law" in 2023 — an escalating tax on packaged foods starting at 10%, rising to 15%, then to 20% in subsequent years.
    
- **Norway, Hungary, Denmark**: European countries with specific taxes on sugar or sugar-added foods.
    
- **Bermuda, Dominica, St. Vincent and the Grenadines**: Small island nations that have also implemented such taxes.
    
- **Navajo Nation (U.S.)**: A notable sub-national/tribal-level example of targeted taxation on unhealthy food.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scale of the Obesity Problem</mark>

The urgency of policy action is underscored by alarming projections from ICMR-NIN data.

- Over 17 million children and adolescents in India are currently affected by obesity.
    
- This number is projected to cross 27 million by 2030 if unaddressed.
    
- This represents a rapid escalation in childhood/adolescent obesity within a short timeframe.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Editorial's Core Argument</mark>

The editorial's central message is that the Indian government can no longer delay acting on these evidence-based recommendations, given both domestic data and international precedent supporting health taxes as an effective nudge toward healthier consumer choices.
