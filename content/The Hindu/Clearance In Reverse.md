## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

The piece analyses the Supreme Court's July 29, 2026 order on post facto (retrospective) environmental clearances, delivered by a Bench led by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant. It examines the implications of this ruling on India's Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) framework and questions whether "regularisation" of environmental violations undermines the precautionary principle.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Issue: Sequencing Rule in EIA</mark>

- India's EIA regime is built on one non-negotiable sequencing principle: a project must be appraised and cleared before construction begins, not after it becomes operational.
    
- The Supreme Court's order technically reaffirms this rule but simultaneously weakens it by permitting an alternative route for legalising violations.
    
- The Court quashed the 2021 Office Memorandum (OM) that had earlier empowered the Centre and other authorities to grant retrospective environmental clearances.
    
- However, the Court left open a door for the Centre to legalise the same violations through a statutory notification instead of an administrative order.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Administrative Order vs Statutory Notification</mark>

- The Court draws a distinction between an "administrative order" (like the quashed 2021 OM) and a "statutory notification," treating the latter as more legitimate.
    
- Critics call this distinction a "fig-leaf" — essentially a procedural technicality dressed up as environmental rigour, since the practical effect (legalising post-facto violations) remains unchanged.
    
- The deeper structural problem is not addressed: the mismatch between State-level appraisal machinery and Central law.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Design Flaw: Violations as the Norm, Not Exception</mark>

- State EIA authorities, expert appraisal committees, and pollution control boards frequently detect violations only after infrastructure (highways, ports, steel plants) is already built.
    
- This has caused post facto clearance to shift from being an exception to becoming the de facto operating model for large projects.
    
- Projects are treated as a "fait accompli" — their ecological cost is weighed only when demolition would be costlier than granting approval after the fact.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Flawed Analogy: Environmental Amnesty vs Tax Amnesty</mark>

- The Supreme Court reasoned that governments may design environmental amnesty schemes similar to tax defaulter amnesty schemes.
    
- The editorial rejects this analogy: the logic used to regularise an unauthorised colony for electoral benefit cannot be applied to projects damaging forests and floodplains.
    
- Environmental science is founded on the principle of interconnectedness — plant, animal, human, and microbial systems are interlinked, so damage to one part propagates through the whole system.
    
- A wetland is fundamentally different from an "unregistered plot" that can be regularised merely by paying a fee.
    
- Even public-good justifications (hospitals, airports) cannot automatically offset ecological costs.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Weakening of Penalties: The Jan Vishwas Amendments</mark>

- Existing retrospective clearances impose fines on violators, but the Jan Vishwas (Amendment of Provisions) Act removed the threat of imprisonment for environmental lapses, leaving only monetary penalties.
    
- This effectively converts environmental compliance into a "cost of doing business" — an expense that well-capitalised project proponents can simply budget for in advance.
    
- The Supreme Court's judgment does not scrutinise this shift; instead, it endorses the underlying premise by treating environmental harm as a "debt that can be settled after the fact."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Precautionary Principle Under Strain</mark>

- The precautionary principle is meant to prevent irreversible ecological harm before it occurs.
    
- The Court holds that reducing violations to civil (monetary) penalties does not dilute this principle.
    
- The editorial disagrees, arguing that a principle designed to prevent irreversible harm cannot be honoured merely by "pricing the harm and collecting the cheque."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Real-World Consequences</mark>

- The piece links this transactional, fine-based approach to real infrastructure failures, citing incidents of projects being damaged or flattened during intense monsoon activity — a direct consequence of construction proceeding without proper prior ecological assessment.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Suggested Way Forward</mark>

- The editorial's key recommendation: the Centre should not read the Supreme Court's order as a licence to multiply environmental amnesty schemes.
    
- Instead, it should be treated as a mandate to repair and strengthen the clearance apparatus itself — fixing the systemic gap between State appraisal machinery and Central law before violations occur, rather than legalising them afterward.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Terms </mark>

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|EIA (Environmental Impact Assessment)|Prior appraisal process assessing ecological impact of a project before clearance|
|Post facto/retrospective clearance|Environmental approval granted after construction/operation has already begun|
|Precautionary principle|Doctrine requiring preventive action against irreversible environmental harm, even before scientific certainty|
|Jan Vishwas Act|Law that decriminalised several regulatory offences, including environmental violations, replacing jail terms with fines|
|2021 Office Memorandum|Administrative order (quashed by SC) that had permitted retrospective clearances|
|Fait accompli|A project treated as an already-completed fact, making ecological review a formality|
