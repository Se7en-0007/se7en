The article discusses Kerala's plan to relaunch its historic People's Plan Campaign (PPC), a landmark experiment in democratic decentralisation, as a modernised "2.0" version to address contemporary governance challenges.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background Event</mark>

- On August 16, prominent figures associated with Kerala's decentralisation experiment gathered in Thiruvananthapuram to mark the 30th anniversary of the People's Plan Campaign (PPC), launched officially on August 17, 1996.
    
- The Thiruvananthapuram conclave was a non-governmental initiative attended by elected representatives, former Ministers, bureaucrats, and hundreds of grassroots activists.
    
- Beyond commemorating the anniversary, the meeting's core purpose was to conceptualise and plan "People's Plan Campaign 2.0."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Personalities Involved</mark>

- T.M. Thomas Isaac — CPI(M) veteran, former Kerala Finance Minister, previously in charge of PPC as a member of the Kerala State Planning Board; outlined the new plan involving student internships with local bodies and a tech-driven, online-portal-coordinated campaign.
    
- K.M. Shaji — Minister for Local Self-Government in the ruling Congress-led United Democratic Front (UDF) government; opened the event and noted how successive Kerala governments have supported the PPC's process of deepening democracy.
    
- Richard W. Franke — Professor Emeritus, Montclair State University; co-authored (with Isaac) the book "People's Planning — Kerala, Local Democracy and Development."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Historical and Ideological Roots</mark>

- The PPC traces its conceptual origin to Mahatma Gandhi's idea of "Gram Swaraj" (village self-rule).
    
- It draws constitutional strength from the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments, which mandated decentralised local self-governance (Panchayats and Municipalities) across India.
    
- It was institutionalised in Kerala through the Kerala Panchayati Raj Act and the Kerala Municipality Act.
    
- Kerala's pre-existing socio-economic conditions made it fertile ground for this experiment: a legacy of social reform movements (late 19th–early 20th century), successful land reforms that transformed the agrarian structure, and high social indicators in literacy and health.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Launch and Implementation (1996)</mark>

- The PPC was officially launched on August 17, 1996 by the Left Democratic Front (LDF) government.
    
- It was run in "campaign mode" since participatory planning was a novel concept for the population at the time.
    
- Grama sabhas (village assemblies) and urban ward assemblies were organised statewide to enable grassroots participation in planning.
    
- A defining structural feature: the LDF government devolved 35%-40% of the state's Plan outlay directly to local governments — a major fiscal decentralisation step.
    
- The State Planning Board's Economic Review recorded this as a major shift, placing local self-governments at the centre of the development planning process.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Achievements and Outcomes</mark>

- The programme is assessed as neither a complete success nor a failure — it deepened participatory democracy and strengthened local governments substantially.
    
- The Kudumbashree scheme (women's self-help group-based poverty eradication and empowerment programme) emerged from this ecosystem and gained national and international recognition.
    
- Isaac and Franke, in their book, characterised the PPC's evolution as transforming decentralisation "from a mere government-led administrative reform" into "an object of mass action and a part of the popular politics."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Contemporary Challenges Facing 2.0</mark>

|Challenge|Detail|
|---|---|
|Declining participation|Waning public enthusiasm and low turnout in grama sabhas, especially in urban areas|
|Youth engagement|Attracting younger generations is harder now compared to the 1990s, given a faster-paced, more urbanised Kerala|
|Rapid urbanisation|Government estimates project 96.43% of Kerala's population could be urban by 2036|
|Governance trends|Emerging centralisation trends in government run counter to decentralisation goals|
|Fiscal stress|Kerala faces increasing fiscal constraints, limiting resources for local bodies|
|Persistent old issues|Unemployment, waste disposal, gender justice, and marginalised sections' welfare remain unresolved|
|New/emerging issues|Limited income sources, climate change, and an ageing society are additional pressures|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Way Forward</mark>

- Local bodies need to evolve a new political and fiscal culture combined with stronger public involvement.
    
- There is a need for elected members with impeccable credentials to restore trust and effectiveness in local governance.
    
- The 2.0 version must factor in centralisation trends, changing public aspirations, and fiscal stress simultaneously while designing interventions.
