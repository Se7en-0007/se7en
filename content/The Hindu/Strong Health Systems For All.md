## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Thesis</mark>

The article argues that with global health financing sources shrinking, the practical way forward is not waiting for more money but spending existing public health budgets more efficiently across LMICs (Low- and Middle-Income Countries), including India.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Global Health Financing Gap</mark>

- World Bank data shows per capita public spending on universal health coverage in LMICs is about half the minimum required benchmark.
    
- As a share of GDP, the health expenditure gap between LMICs and high-income countries narrowed from 2.05 percentage points (2000) to 1.68 percentage points (2023).
    
- However, WHO data reveals the per capita gap has actually expanded more than three-fold in the same period, showing GDP-share figures can be misleading.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Decline in Development Assistance for Health (DAH)</mark>

- DAH (Development Assistance for Health) peaked in 2021 during COVID-19, then reversed sharply post-pandemic.
    
- In early 2025, the US (historically over one-third of global DAH) cut foreign assistance by 67%.
    
- UK, France, and Germany followed with cuts of 39%, 35%, and 12% respectively.
    
- OECD projects health funding could fall by up to 60% from its 2022 peak.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Rising Public Debt Constraining Budgets</mark>

- Global public debt hit a record $102 trillion in 2024; developing countries account for $31 trillion of this.
    
- Since 2010, developing-country debt has grown twice as fast as advanced-country debt.
    
- In 2024, developing countries paid a record $921 billion in net interest payments (UNCTAD), squeezing funds available for health.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Three Strategies to Improve Public Health Spending</mark>

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">1. Spend what is allocated (budget execution)</mark>

- LMIC health budgets are executed at only 85%-90%, lower than general budget or education execution rates.
    
- In India, a parliamentary panel found only about two-thirds of the flagship health infrastructure mission's allocation was spent in 2024-25.
    
- Within the National Health Mission (NHM), only 26% of funds earmarked for communicable and non-communicable disease programmes were actually used that year.
    

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">2. Spend on the right things (allocative efficiency)</mark>

- Wage/salary budgets are usually fully implemented, but spending on goods and services is underutilized, leaving health workers ill-equipped.
    
- A large share of money goes to curative care (secondary/tertiary) instead of preventive, primary healthcare.
    
- India spends less than one-fourth of public health money on preventive care (London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine estimate).
    
- Public money is best directed at classic public goods with "market failure" (infectious disease control, sanitation) rather than curative services the private sector already provides.
    
- Evidence shows public health spending strongly improves infectious disease outcomes (access, vaccination, sanitation) but does less for maternal/child health or non-communicable diseases (NCDs).
    
- As populations age, more focus will be needed on chronic disease risk factors, early detection, and management.
    

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">3. Improve governance and operational efficiency</mark>

- Reduced corruption and better bureaucratic quality amplify the positive effect of health spending on outcomes like child mortality.
    
- Conversely, increasing spending where governance is weak does not necessarily improve outcomes.
    
- Decentralization makes subnational governance quality increasingly critical.
    
- Key levers: improving budget credibility and cash disbursement, involving public health providers in budget processes for accountability, improving procurement for value-for-money, and building budget flexibility for unforeseen shocks (a COVID-19 lesson).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Conclusion of The Article</mark>

The authors (Nalini Gulati and Vikas Dimble) maintain that enhanced health spending still has the greatest returns where outcomes are poorest, so well-directed money can narrow health gaps across and within countries.
