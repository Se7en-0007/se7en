Author Rajeev Kumar argues that India must shift from ad-hoc, examination-specific security fixes to a common, standardized, and independently auditable Public Examination Integrity Framework covering the entire exam lifecycle.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Thesis</mark>

- Securing question papers alone is insufficient; integrity must be protected across the entire examination lifecycle, not just at the leak-prevention stage.
    
- Recurring controversies (NEET, UGC-NET, JEE) show vulnerabilities exist within the examination ecosystem itself, not merely from external threats.
    
- India built strong safeguards through Public Examination Reform 1.0 (PER 1.0); it now needs PER 2.0 — common, standardized SOPs with clear accountability at every stage.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: PER 1.0 (Pre-2005 to 2015)</mark>

- Before the RTI Act, 2005, competitive exams operated as "black boxes" — no disclosure of question papers, answer keys, marksheets, or selection criteria.
    
- Sections 4 and 25 of the RTI Act, 2005 mandated proactive disclosure of processes, rules, records, and norms by public authorities, reducing dependence on individual RTI applications.
    
- A decade of RTI activism, litigation, and public debate (2006-2015) pushed IIT-JEE toward transparency and standardized procedures.
    

**Four key PER 1.0 safeguards:**

1. Selection criteria defined and disclosed before the exam.
    
2. Candidates allowed to challenge questions and provisional answer keys.
    
3. Candidates given access to OMR sheets to verify scores.
    
4. Common counselling for optimal seat allocation, reducing wastage.
    

- These safeguards became de facto standards across CBSE, NTA, and UPSC exams, benefiting millions annually.
    
- Recent proof of value: NTA cancelled and reconducted three UGC-NET 2026 subject papers after errors; in UPSC CSE Prelims 2026, eight GS questions reportedly had no correct option — both caught via the challenge/answer-key mechanism.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The 2014 Trigger Event</mark>

- Delhi High Court, hearing an IIT admissions irregularities case in 2014, remarked the situation resembled "darkest beneath the lamp."
    
- This led IITs to introduce transparency-oriented SOPs, many of which became de facto national standards.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Problems with NEET</mark>

- NEET has faced recurring allegations: paper leaks, question errors, impersonation, score inflation, and unusual clustering of high scores since inception.
    
- Vulnerabilities span question setting, exam centres, OMR handling, and digital systems — yet no conventional printed-paper leak has been proven in major recent cases.
    

**NEET-2024:** Multiple process failures — incorrect question papers, delayed OMR collection, grace marks, unusually high scores; investigation found no prosecutable evidence of a conventional leak.

**NEET-2026:** More direct challenge — targeted question material reportedly circulated weeks before the exam; CBI chargesheet reportedly named NTA panel subject-matter experts, coaching-linked persons, and intermediaries, suggesting compromise at the question-setting stage itself.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Lesson: Security vs Transparency</mark>

- IIT-JEE converted failures into defined, auditable SOPs over time; NEET has responded to controversies with reactive, panic-mode security additions rather than systematic process reform.
    
- Security protects confidential information from unauthorized access; transparency mechanisms detect errors and irregularities.
    
- A credible examination system needs both — not one at the expense of the other.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Failure of Committee-Based Reform</mark>

- Multiple technical committees (2009-2014): Acharyya, Kakodkar, and Ramasami Committees — recommendations never translated into an implemented reform framework.
    
- Post NEET-2024 fiasco: Dr. Radhakrishnan Committee gave comprehensive recommendations, but no Action Taken Report was prepared; Radhakrishnan was instead tasked with steering ongoing NEET reforms.
    
- NEET-2026 controversy and re-examination raised fresh doubts about effectiveness of this approach.
    
- July 2026: Nandan Nilekani Task Force constituted, largely overlapping with earlier Radhakrishnan recommendations — risk of "committee-hopping" instead of institutional reform, a pattern the Supreme Court has cautioned against.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Proposed SOP Framework for PER 2.0</mark>

SOPs must secure the entire lifecycle from within, ensuring no individual, agency, or process can compromise any link undetected. Minimum coverage areas beyond PER 1.0:

- Question setting and moderation: vetted experts, conflict-of-interest checks, independent moderation, role separation, avoiding repeated use of same experts, post-exam intelligence watch.
    
- Translation: independent verification, secure machine translation via protected LLMs with minimal human handling.
    
- Digitisation, compilation, logistics: restricted access, strong authentication, activity logs, trackable chain of custody.
    
- Examination centres: risk-based selection, randomisation, surveillance, accountable management, collusion checks.
    
- OMR, evaluation, results: sealed collection/tracking, scanning reconciliation, anomaly detection, pre-declaration review.
    
- Post-examination accountability: forensic audit, independent complaint handling, whistleblower protection, public integrity report.
    
- Every process needs defined ownership, verifiable audit trail, independent oversight, and explicit conflict-of-interest management.
    
- Standardisation converts safeguards into enforceable national standards for NTA and all exam bodies, rather than leaving integrity to individual institutions or personalities.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Way Forward</mark>

- India should move from exam-specific security measures to a common Public Examination Integrity Framework.
    
- First step: a comprehensive white paper honestly documenting ground realities — what has worked, failed, or remained unimplemented — forming the basis for standardized, continuously updated SOPs for NTA and all national/State exam bodies.
    
- Supreme Court's 2011 judgment held that RTI, public debate, and expert input made merit-ranking "more transparent and accurate," and stressed selection processes must be "upgraded and fine-tuned year after year" to stay relevant.
    
- Goal: a constantly upgraded, robust, resilient examination framework that detects irregularities while keeping integrity and merit sacrosanct — exams should be "secure by design," not secured only after a breach.
