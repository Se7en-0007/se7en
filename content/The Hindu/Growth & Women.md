## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Thesis</mark>

The article argues that India's aspiration to become a developed nation (Viksit Bharat) by 2047 cannot be realized without a substantial increase in women's Work Participation Rate (WPR). A 10-percentage-point rise in female WPR could add nearly two percentage points to GDP growth, making it one of the strongest levers for economic expansion.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Economic Context</mark>

- Youth unemployment today is double the 2012 rate.
    
- Structural weaknesses predate the recent West Asia war shock and trace back to 2016 policy shocks: demonetisation, a poorly designed GST, NBFC crisis, and COVID-19 mismanagement.
    
- These shocks reversed positive structural change (rising farm employment share, falling manufacturing share in GVA and employment) and slowed non-farm job growth.
    
- Rising inequality collapsed aggregate demand and the investment-to-GDP ratio.
    
- Official GDP growth is quoted at 6.2% per annum, but a former Chief Economic Adviser estimates actual growth at closer to 4.5%.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Female Employment Drives Growth</mark>

- Increases overall labour supply and expands productive capacity.
    
- Raises household incomes, boosting consumption and savings.
    
- Improves nutrition, education, and healthcare outcomes for children, strengthening long-term human capital.
    
- Enhances workplace productivity and innovation through gender-diverse teams (citing Nobel laureate Claudia Goldin's cross-country research).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">State of Female Work Participation</mark>

- India's female WPR remains below 30%, among the lowest globally - comparable to Saudi Arabia and Yemen.
    
- Female Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) is low even compared to countries at similar per capita income levels.
    
- It declined steadily from 1983 to 2018 despite improving female education levels.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The U-Shaped Curve Explained</mark>

|Phase|Period|Key Driver|
|---|---|---|
|High WPR|1980s–early 1990s|High agricultural participation|
|Declining WPR|2004-05 to 2018-19|Structural shift away from agriculture, mechanisation, rising education/school attendance among young women|
|Apparent Rise|Post-2020|Distress-driven feminisation of agriculture|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Post-2020 "Distress-Driven" Rise</mark>

- The post-2020 increase in female WPR is not a positive signal; it followed a GDP slowdown since 2017.
    
- COVID-19 caused large-scale reverse migration; men lost urban jobs and returned to villages.
    
- Women were pushed into subsistence agriculture as Unpaid Family Labour (UFL), often informal and unremunerated.
    
- This reversed a long-term declining trend in UFL share (which had fallen from 1983 to 2012).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Structural Weaknesses in Growth Pattern</mark>

- Recent GDP growth is concentrated in capital-intensive sectors (finance, IT, organised manufacturing) that generate few jobs.
    
- Labour-intensive sectors (textiles, food processing, garments, small-scale manufacturing) saw absolute employment decline between 2013 and 2019.
    
- Fewer women were employed in manufacturing in 2019 than in 2004, despite "Make in India" and Performance-Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes.
    
- Manufacturing employment for women only returned to 2004 levels by 2022.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The North-South Divide</mark>

- Tamil Nadu employs over 40% of India's women factory workers despite having only about 5–6% of India's population.
    
- Key drivers in Tamil Nadu: textiles and garments (Tiruppur, Coimbatore), footwear, electronics assembly (Sriperumbudur), automobiles and auto components.
    
- Enabling factors: higher female literacy, greater mobility, developed hostel and transport infrastructure for women workers.
    
- Hindi-belt states lag due to weaker investment in health and public education, contributing to malnutrition and stunting rates higher than African averages.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Educated Youth Paradox</mark>

- Secondary-level gross enrolment achieved gender parity between 2010 and 2015, even in rural Uttar Pradesh and Bihar.
    
- Despite this, educated young women face high and rising unemployment.
    
- Young women (aged 15–29) who are neither in education nor seeking work (NEET category) rose sharply:
    

|Year|NEET Young Women|
|---|---|
|Until 2004|Under 70 million|
|2012|84 million|
|2018|Over 100 million|

- Bihar's female WPR stands at just 15%.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Terms for Revision</mark>

- WPR (Work Participation Rate): Percentage of population engaged in economic activity.
    
- LFPR (Labour Force Participation Rate): Includes employed and those actively seeking work.
    
- UFL (Unpaid Family Labour): Work performed without direct remuneration, typically within family enterprises or farms.
    
- Distress-driven feminisation of agriculture: Women's forced entry/retention in insecure farm work due to lack of alternatives.
