This editorial (The Hindu, August 26, 2026) argues that India's space sector must shift its focus from symbolic prestige toward reliable, regular, and cost-competitive launch capacity.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context & Historical Framing</mark>

- Space exploration served as India's "football" in the 20th century — an arena where nations without economic power could demonstrate skill and nerve.
    
- Despite being a poor, newly independent republic, India invested heavily in rockets and atomic energy as a bet on future capability rather than immediate utility.
    
- In the era of a controlled economy and state broadcasting monopoly, a successful launch was symbolically significant regardless of the payload's practical use.
    
- Today, rockets and satellites function as essential infrastructure — the "unglamorous scaffolding" behind navigation, banking, weather forecasting, and even warfare — rather than symbols of prestige.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Trigger Event</mark>

- The editorial coincides with India's third National Space Day, marking the anniversary of Chandrayaan-3's landing in the lunar south polar region.
    
- On this occasion, Prime Minister Narendra Modi urged 20 space-startup founders to build a global "aura" of innovation to attract international talent to India.
    
- The editorial critiques this framing as outdated — arguing a private space sector cannot succeed merely by association with the romance of rocketry; business success requires different foundations.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Data: India's Launch Cost Problem</mark>

A peer-reviewed study (Alessio Terzi and Francesco Nicoli, Economics Letters) quantified India's launch cost disadvantage in 2025.

|Country/Region|Cost per kg to LEO (2025)|
|---|---|
|India|$13,302 (highest)|
|China|$5,809|
|Global average|$3,868|
|United States|$3,225|

Japan, Russia, and Europe are also cited as cheaper than India, though exact figures are not specified in the article.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Root Cause: Launch Frequency Deficit</mark>

- India conducted only 5 launches in 2025, far short of the 30 that were projected.
    
- Infrequent and uneconomical launches prevent India from building the reliability reputation needed to compete globally.
    
- This scarcity directly drives up per-unit costs, since fixed costs and reputational risk aren't spread across a high launch cadence.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Real-World Consequences</mark>

- GSAT-N2, at 4,700 kg, exceeded the capacity of ISRO's LVM-3 launcher and had to be launched via SpaceX's Falcon 9 instead.
    
- Pixxel and Digantara — two of the very 20 startups Modi addressed at the National Space Day event — relied on SpaceX to reach orbit.
    
- SpaceX captured roughly three-quarters of the world's total payload mass launched into orbit in 2025, reflecting a near-monopolistic global market position.
    
- This dependence on a foreign competitor undercuts India's own stated ambitions for self-reliant, competitive space capability.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Editorial's Core Argument</mark>

- The sector's real economic value lies not in symbolic "aura" but in tangible outcomes: jobs, long-term capital investment, and tax revenues.
    
- India should prioritize heavy-lift launch capability that can undercut SpaceX and China on cost and reliability.
    
- High-profile initiatives like Moon bases with NASA or interplanetary missions are termed the "ornament, not the engine" — worthwhile but secondary to building dependable core launch infrastructure.
    
- The central prescription: build a regular, reliable, economically viable launch cadence before chasing prestige projects.
