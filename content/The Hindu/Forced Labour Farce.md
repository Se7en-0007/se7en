## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Tariff Timeline</mark>

- U.S. Supreme Court's February 2026 decision struck down the U.S.'s primary legal basis for imposing high reciprocal tariffs on trading partners.
    
- Trump's fallback was a temporary 10% flat tariff on all countries, but this had a built-in expiry of 150 days.
    
- That flat tariff applied uniformly regardless of whether a country had a trade deal with the U.S., diluting its usefulness as a negotiating lever.
    
- These temporary 10% tariffs have now expired, creating the need for a new legal tool.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">New Tariff Mechanism: Section 301 Forced Labour Tariffs</mark>

- Announced last week (as of late July 2026), these are Section 301 tariffs framed around "forced labour" in supply chains.
    
- Unlike the earlier flat tariff, this mechanism is designed to be more permanent.
    
- It builds in explicit benefits for countries that already have trade deals with the U.S., making it function as a deal-inducing tool rather than a pure enforcement measure.
    
- The final version includes several product-wise exemptions and country-wise quotas, unlike a uniform enforcement action.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Country-Wise Tariff Impact</mark>

|Country/Bloc|Tariff Outcome|Trade Deal Status with U.S.|
|---|---|---|
|India|10% additional tariff over base U.S. tariff|No finalized trade deal|
|European Union|Total tariff capped at 10%|Deal in place/advanced stage|
|Taiwan|Total tariff capped at 10%|Deal in place/advanced stage|
|Japan|Similar benefit (capped)|Deal at various stage of formalisation|
|South Korea|Similar benefit (capped)|Deal at various stage of formalisation|
|Switzerland|Similar benefit (capped)|Deal at various stage of formalisation|

- The key distinction: countries with U.S. trade deals face a total tariff of 10%, while India faces 10% on top of existing base tariffs — a meaningfully higher effective burden.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Critique</mark>

- If the actual policy goal were stopping forced labour, tariffs would apply equally to all offending countries irrespective of trade-deal status — this isn't the case.
    
- The presence of numerous product-wise exemptions and country quotas suggests the mechanism is a bargaining tool, not a genuine labour-rights enforcement measure — hence the editorial's title "forced labour farce."
    
- India itself has not been accused of using forced labour directly, yet still faces the tariff because of its trade linkages with third countries (like China and Malaysia) that are accused.
    
- This is seen as inconsistent, especially since the U.S. government has separately told a court it does not wish to act as the "world police."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Response and Outcome</mark>

- India reduced its proposed tariff from 12.5% to a final 10% — comparable to or better than most competitor nations.
    
- This reduction was achieved simply by India issuing a domestic notification banning the import of goods made using forced labour.
    
- Actual enforcement of this notification is expected to be difficult, since it would require countries such as China and Malaysia to permit Indian officials to inspect their labour conditions — a level of access unlikely to be granted.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Remaining Uncertainty</mark>

- A separate Section 301 investigation into "excess capacity" (likely referring to industrial overcapacity, e.g., in sectors like steel) is still pending and could trigger further tariffs on India.
    
- Given this unresolved risk, the editorial argues India is unlikely to accept a broader trade deal with the U.S. until the excess capacity tariff picture becomes clear.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recommendation</mark>

- The core policy advice: India should not let these new tariffs pressure it into rushing a trade deal with the U.S.
    
- Rationale: Past experience shows that the U.S. tariff landscape can shift radically even after a deal has been signed, undermining the value of quick concessions.
