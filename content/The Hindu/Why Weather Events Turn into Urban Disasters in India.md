## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Trigger Event</mark>

- Gurugram recorded temperatures above 44°C in May 2026, prompting IMD heat alerts for Haryana.
    
- Just 11 weeks later (August 6, 2026), heavy rain flooded arterial roads and low-lying sectors in the same city.
    
- Authorities responded by advising work-from-home to reduce traffic congestion.
    
- Both episodes, despite being opposite weather extremes, stemmed from the same underlying planning failures: dense construction, weak drainage, loss of open land, unsafe housing, and inadequate protection for at-risk populations.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Hazard vs Disaster: The Core Distinction</mark>

- A heatwave, cloudburst, or cyclone is merely a "hazard"; it becomes a "disaster" only when it strikes people, buildings, and services that are exposed and lack capacity to cope.
    
- This framework comes from the UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction, which defines disaster risk as the interaction between a hazard and the vulnerability of the conditions it hits.
    
- For flooding: heavy rain need not cause disaster if water can drain, spread, or seep into ground — but narrowed drains, built-over lakes/floodplains, and excess concrete turn rain into crisis.
    
- For heat: dense construction, asphalt, poor ventilation, and loss of tree cover cause neighbourhoods to absorb and retain heat; risk is highest for those in poorly ventilated homes or working outdoors without shade, water, or healthcare access.
    
- Key takeaway: the hazard originates in weather, but the scale of damage is determined by how a city is built and governed.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Indian Cities Are Becoming More Vulnerable</mark>

- Climate change (more severe heatwaves and intense rainfall) and unplanned urban growth are compounding risks together.
    
- Cities are replacing soil, vegetation, and water bodies with buildings/roads, raising local temperatures and reducing rainwater absorption capacity.
    
- World Bank estimate: city centre temperatures in India can be 3–4°C higher than surrounding areas (urban heat island effect).
    
- Bengaluru case study (IISc research): built-up land rose from ~8% (1973) to ~74% (2013); vegetation cover fell from 68% to under 15% in the same period.
    
- Future growth pressure: India's urban population projected to reach 951 million by 2050; over 144 million new homes may be needed by 2070; more than half of the infrastructure cities will need by 2050 is yet to be built.
    
- Whether this growth increases risk depends on where construction is permitted, materials used, and whether natural drainage/open spaces are preserved.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Economic and Human Costs</mark>

- Official disaster estimates typically only capture deaths and damage to roads, buildings, and public assets.
    
- Overlooked costs: lost wages for informal workers, illness-related expenses, disrupted small businesses, and time lost to repeated flood recovery.
    
- World Bank projections: timely adaptation could prevent annual urban flood losses of about $5 billion by 2030, rising to $30 billion by 2070.
    
- Heat-reduction measures could save more than 130,000 lives by 2050.
    
- These figures represent avoidable losses, not inevitable costs — framing disaster risk as a matter of policy choice.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legal and Institutional Changes</mark>

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Disaster Management Act, 2005 and 2025 Amendment</mark>

- The 2005 Act established disaster management authorities at national, state, and district levels.
    
- The 2025 amendment introduced a mechanism for risks specific to large cities via Section 41A.
    
- Section 41A allows state governments to constitute an Urban Disaster Management Authority in every state capital and municipal corporation city, except Delhi and Chandigarh.
    
- These authorities are chaired by the municipal commissioner and are tasked with preparing/implementing an urban disaster management plan.
    
- The amendment also mandates a national database of risk assessments, mitigation plans, and real-time disaster information.
    

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Disaster Financing Change (August 2026)</mark>

- Heatwaves and lightning were added to the notified list of natural calamities for the 2026-31 Finance Commission period.
    
- States can now use disaster response funds for eligible relief from these events, and heatwave mitigation projects can draw on disaster mitigation funds.
    
- This closes a financing gap but does not fix underlying urban planning failures that create heat vulnerability.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Where the System Falls Short</mark>

|Gap|Detail|
|---|---|
|No deadline for urban authorities|2025 amendment leaves constitution of urban disaster authorities to state discretion, with no timeline set|
|Poor compliance|As of February 2026, Home Ministry told Parliament only Karnataka had set up such an authority (for Bengaluru)|
|Fragmented responsibility|Municipal corporations, district administrations, and state departments often work under different plans/authority lines|
|Missing master plans|2021 NITI Aayog report: 65% of India's 7,933 urban settlements had no master plan|
|Planner shortage|Fewer than 4,000 sanctioned town-planner posts exist against an estimated need of 12,000; many sanctioned posts remain vacant|
|Weak plan enforcement|Even where master plans exist, floodplains/lakes/drains often aren't protected via zoning; heat/climate plans have little effect if building approvals ignore them|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recommendations for Reducing Risk</mark>

- **Flood maps** should determine construction-prohibited zones, drainage capacity upgrades, and mandatory building plinth levels.
    
- **Heat maps** should inform requirements for shade, ventilation, tree cover, and building materials.
    
- These provisions must be embedded in statutory development plans, not left as separate advisory documents.
    
- City governments need qualified urban planners and technical experts embedded in development decision-making.
    
- Urban disaster authorities require adequate staff expertise, dedicated budgets, and power to coordinate across municipal engineering, water, health, transport, revenue, and emergency services.
    
- Investment/project approvals should follow prepared plans, with clear responsibility assignment and reviewable deadlines before the next expected disaster season.
    
- Financing should extend beyond post-event relief: municipal grants and infrastructure spending can be linked to compliance with flood, heat, and land-use plans.
    
- Early warning systems should trigger pre-agreed measures: adjusted outdoor working hours, hospital preparedness, school closures, and traffic diversions.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway</mark>

Weather-related risk cannot be eliminated from cities, but recurring flooding and overheating in the same roads and neighbourhoods each year reflect prior decisions on land use, construction, and public infrastructure rather than the weather event itself.
