A direct read of the article: rising climate extremes in both India and China are creating a potential low-risk avenue for bilateral cooperation on disaster resilience, even as broader ties remain strained.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background</mark>

This year, El Niño delayed India's monsoon, but when rains finally came, cities like Mumbai and Surat received a month's rainfall within days, while Assam and Odisha suffered severe flooding with loss of life and infrastructure damage. China faced parallel extreme weather in Guangxi, Shaanxi, and Gansu provinces. Experts link these high-intensity, short-duration weather bursts to climate change, driven by rising atmospheric temperatures and rapidly warming oceans.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Shared Structural Vulnerabilities</mark>

Both countries face similar climate challenges rooted in urbanisation and governance gaps.

- Rapid urbanisation has replaced wetlands, forests, and permeable surfaces with concrete
    
- Outdated drainage systems, poor waste management, and lack of green spaces worsen waterlogging and flooding
    
- Coastal megacities face extreme precipitation, storm surges, and sea-level rise combined
    
- Inland cities face heatwaves, droughts, and flash floods in rapid succession
    
- Costs of inaction include disrupted supply chains, economic losses, falling productivity, and public health risks
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Now: The Diplomatic Window</mark>

After years of strained relations, India-China ties are showing tentative signs of improvement. In April 2026, a Chinese delegation led by China's Special Envoy for Climate Change visited New Delhi, signalling that climate cooperation remains a live priority. While collaboration on green technology and critical minerals is hampered by economic competition and geopolitical tension, disaster mitigation and urban resilience offer a comparatively low-risk pathway to rebuild trust.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">History of Climate Engagement</mark>

A CSEP (Centre for Social and Economic Progress) paper mapped India-China climate engagement from the early 1990s to 2020, showing real precedent for cooperation.

- Multiple summit-level joint statements, agreements, and MoUs called for practical climate cooperation
    
- Topics covered floods, earthquakes, droughts, and extreme weather events
    
- MoUs promised joint R&D, hydrological/oceanic/seismic data sharing, and exchange of governance practices
    
- Six Strategic Economic Dialogues discussed urban planning, waste management, sewage treatment, water efficiency, and capacity building
    
- Sister-city agreements were formalised: Delhi-Beijing, Mumbai-Shanghai, Chennai-Chongqing
    
- However, diplomatic disruptions meant little on-ground implementation followed
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Complementary Strengths</mark>

|Aspect|China|India|
|---|---|---|
|Core strength|Embedding resilience into urban/rural planning investment decisions|Adaptive, community-led governance|
|Approach|Data-driven policies in transport, housing, drainage|Targeted interventions for vulnerable populations|
|Notable successes|Sponge cities, drainage modernisation|Early warning systems, heat action plans, cool roofs, nature-based solutions|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Priority Areas for Cooperation</mark>

- Municipal-level knowledge and policy exchange on urban design, sponge cities, drainage, and waste management
    
- Rural livelihoods and resilient agricultural practices for food security
    
- Joint work between planning agencies and academic institutions on hydrological modelling
    
- Integrating regional and indigenous knowledge into climate adaptation strategies
    
- Addressing melting glacial systems in the shared Himalayan ecosystem (existing water-sharing agreements lapsed in 2022)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Financing Challenge</mark>

Both countries depend heavily on public funds for climate adaptation, and bridging the finance gap requires greater private sector participation through tools like blended finance, municipal bonds, and credit enhancement. These financial ecosystems remain nascent, constrained by limited local capacity and regulatory gaps. Evidence-based studies and knowledge exchange between the two countries on such models could open an innovative cooperation avenue, potentially helping shape climate resilience norms across the Global South through better standards, metrics, and fiscal frameworks.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway</mark>

Since climate change impacts cross national borders, greater India-China coordination on disaster resilience could offer a genuine win-win, low-risk pathway even amid broader geopolitical friction.
