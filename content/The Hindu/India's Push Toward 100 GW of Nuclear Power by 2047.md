India's push toward 100 GW of nuclear power by 2047 combines large reactor expansion, a new small modular reactor (SMR) programme, and progress on the long-standing three-stage nuclear fuel cycle plan.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

- PM Modi, in his 2026 Independence Day address, linked rising electricity demand to emerging technologies like AI, chips, and data centres.
    
- The target: 100 GW nuclear capacity by 2047, with five new reactors to be commissioned within this decade.
    
- Growth is being enabled by the SHANTI Act (passed last year), which opens India's nuclear sector to wider private participation.
    
- The Department of Atomic Energy (DAE) has released draft rules under the Act covering private participation, captive generation, licensing, safety oversight, and nuclear liability.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Two-Pronged Expansion Strategy</mark>

- Large-capacity reactors: Indigenous Pressurised Heavy Water Reactors (PHWR) remain the backbone of expansion.
    
- Small Modular Reactors (SMRs): Increasingly promoted for faster deployment, industrial decarbonisation, and providing flexible baseload power to balance renewable energy intermittency.
    
- SMRs are also the focal point of India's international nuclear outreach, since large Light Water Reactors (LWRs) face high project costs that raise feasibility concerns.
    
- Foreign collaboration (interest from Russia, US, France) is currently centred on SMRs rather than large conventional reactors.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Indigenous SMR Programme</mark>

Three SMR types are being designed by the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC) for demonstration:

- Bharat Small Modular Reactor (BSMR-200) - 200 MWe capacity.
    
- A smaller SMR - 55 MWe capacity.
    
- A high-temperature gas-cooled reactor - 5 MWt, intended for hydrogen production via a thermochemical process.
    

Key programme details:

- Target: At least five indigenously designed and operational SMRs by 2033.
    
- Nuclear Energy Mission launched for SMR research, design, development, and deployment, with a budgetary outlay of Rs 20,000 crore.
    
- Government is studying repurposing old thermal power plant sites for nuclear use, particularly suited to SMRs and smaller-capacity projects rather than large reactors.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Three-Stage Nuclear Programme</mark>

India follows a three-stage plan aimed at long-term nuclear fuel self-reliance, built around its indigenous PHWR technology.

- Stage 1 - PHWRs: Use natural uranium as fuel; generate plutonium in spent fuel.
    
- Stage 2 - Fast Breeder Reactors (FBRs): Use plutonium plus depleted uranium to convert Thorium-232 into Uranium-233, using thorium as a "blanket" material.
    
- Stage 3 - Advanced reactors: Use the Uranium-233 (derived from thorium) as fuel, enabling India to exploit its large thorium reserves.
    

Recent milestone:

- In April 2026, India's first indigenous fast breeder reactor at Kalpakkam achieved criticality - a major step in mastering fast breeder technology and advancing fuel self-reliance.
    
- PM Modi highlighted this in his Independence Day speech as a significant step toward nuclear fuel self-sufficiency.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why This Matters For Power Demand</mark>

- Emerging technologies - AI computing, semiconductor fabrication, and data centres - are projected to sharply raise electricity consumption.
    
- Nuclear power is positioned as a carbon-neutral baseload source, complementing renewables (solar/wind) whose output varies, thereby helping grid stability.
    
- Thermal power remains important for baseload too, but nuclear offers a low-carbon alternative for this role as India scales up renewable integration.
