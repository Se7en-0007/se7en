## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context of the Judgment</mark>

On August 20, 2026, a nine-judge Bench of the Supreme Court ruled on a Reference examining the correctness of Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board vs A. Rajappa (BWSSB), 1978. The core issue was the "Triple Test" used to define "industry" under Section 2(j) of the Industrial Disputes (ID) Act, 1947.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background and Timeline of the Reference</mark>

- State of U.P. vs Jai Bir Singh (2005), 5 SCC 1: five-judge Bench referred BWSSB (a seven-judge Bench ruling) for reconsideration.
    
- January 2, 2017: a seven-judge Bench further referred the matter to a nine-judge Bench.
    
- February 16, 2026: the nine-judge Bench framed four questions covering the correctness of the BWSSB test, effect of the 1982 Amendment and the Industrial Relations Code (IRC), 2020, whether government welfare activities qualify as "industrial activities," and the scope of "sovereign functions" under Section 2(j).
    
- November 21, 2025: IRC, 2020 came into force, repealing the ID Act, 1947 — rendering the Reference largely academic even before it was decided.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Judicial Opinions</mark>

- Justices D. Datta and U. Bhuyan: held the Reference "requires no answer."
    
- Justice B.V. Nagarathna: called the exercise "unnecessary and only academic in nature," but on the merits affirmed BWSSB, stating its applicability to the IRC's Section 2(p) must be tested by comparing actual statutory text, not decided in the abstract.
    
- Justice P.S. Narasimha: agreed an authoritative interpretation was once needed but became "unnecessary" after repeal; also said IRC interpretation must be "freed of the bondage of ratio" in BWSSB and "not burdened by existing interpretations."
    
- Chief Justice of India (for four judges): reformulated the Triple Test but expressly termed the reformulation "hypothetical" and prospective only — not applicable to pending cases, and pending disputes remain governed by BWSSB. Also stated BWSSB should not be treated as the "sheet anchor" for future IRC interpretation, while claiming not to construe the IRC itself.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legal/Procedural Contradictions Highlighted</mark>

- The four questions framed (Feb 2026) concerned only ID Act Section 2(j), not IRC Section 2(p) — so the IRC's interpretation was never formally before the Court.
    
- The CJI's opinion simultaneously refuses to interpret the IRC provision, yet rules on how BWSSB (as precedent) cannot be used to interpret it — the authors call this "a form of construing it," an attempt to "police the tools" of future interpretation.
    
- Since the ID Act stands repealed, no future cases can arise under Section 2(j); the reformulated test is thus purely academic.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Deeper Anxiety Around BWSSB</mark>

- The Reference originated from Jai Bir Singh (2005), which criticised BWSSB's "worker-oriented approach" as "unmindful of the interests of the employer or owner."
    
- This anxiety recurs in the CJI's opinion (describing BWSSB as a "burden" whose removal lets law "move forward unencumbered") and in Justice Narasimha's language of "bondage."
    
- The authors argue this framing normalises employer-favourable baselines — calling a requirement to explain dismissal a "burden," while unrestricted power to dismiss is treated as ordinary business freedom.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Constitutional and Philosophical Arguments</mark>

- Asymmetry between capital and labour: Justice Bagchi noted workmen rarely enter negotiation "as an equal."
    
- Article 42: directs the state to secure "just and humane conditions of work."
    
- Article 23: prohibits "traffic in human beings," "begar," and forced labour.
    
- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's warning: liberty from state control can mean "the dictatorship of the private employer."
    
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973), 13-judge Bench: held that social, economic and political justice is part of the Constitution's basic structure.
    
- Argument: inequality between employer and worker has not narrowed since 1947; the "ease of doing business" policy has arguably widened it, strengthening (not weakening) the case for labour protections under Part IV (Directive Principles).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Significance of BWSSB's "Industry" Definition</mark>

- The ID Act, 1947 aimed to maintain industrial peace via basic protections and adjudication forums (labour courts/tribunals with wide powers).
    
- Exclusion from the statutory definition of "industry" meant exclusion from this baseline protection and remedy.
    
- BWSSB's contribution was ensuring protection reached all workers the Act intended to cover, rather than narrowing coverage.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Concluding Critique</mark>

- The authors (V. Gopala Gowda, former Supreme Court Judge, and Maitreyi Krishnan, advocate) argue there was never real justification to treat BWSSB as a "burden" — it simply made the Act function as designed.
    
- They contend the anxiety around BWSSB reflects a persistent desire to narrow which workers receive legal protection.
    
- With the ID Act repealed and IRC in force, they argue the new labour codes are built on a premise of exclusion and must be tested against the Constitution's promise of social justice.
