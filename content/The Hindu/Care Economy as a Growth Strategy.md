## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Thesis</mark>

The care economy — childcare, eldercare, disability care, home-based care — is not merely a social welfare or women's issue but a core economic growth constraint for Asia. Economies cannot reach full potential when the "infrastructure of care" is missing, as this directly limits workforce participation and productivity.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Care Is an Economic Issue</mark>

- Millions of Asian women cannot take jobs, work longer hours, or pursue careers due to unpaid caregiving duties for children or elderly family members
- When care demands rise, women are usually the ones who exit paid work, reduce hours, or decline promotions
- This creates cascading losses: businesses lose talent, families lose income, governments lose tax revenue, economies lose productive capacity
- An ageing Asia cannot sustain productivity if families alone absorb growing care burdens

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Structural Drivers Making This Urgent</mark>

- Shrinking family sizes reducing informal/family-based care capacity
- Urbanisation and migration disrupting traditional joint-family care structures
- Rising female workforce participation increasing demand for external care support
- Rapid population ageing across most of Asia

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Barriers to a Commercial Care Ecosystem</mark>

The care sector across developing Asia remains underdeveloped, fragmented, and informal. Three key constraints:

- **Finance**: Care enterprises seen as too small/fragmented to scale; uncertain revenue models since household paying capacity is limited
- **Regulation**: Care spans health, education, labour, and social protection sectors, creating complicated and inconsistent licensing; weak regulation also lets poor-quality providers persist
- **Workforce**: Quality care needs skilled workers, but care workers are typically poorly paid with no recognised career paths; expansion without training investment risks creating more low-paid, insecure jobs

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Role of Impact Investment and Philanthropy</mark>

- Impact investors and philanthropies can help build this emerging market while generating both social and financial returns
- Capital helps enterprises develop viable, scalable models
- Blended finance can de-risk investments and attract mainstream commercial capital
- Philanthropy can fund innovation and workforce development where returns are slow to materialize
- Governments must set standards, finance essential services, and create enabling regulation

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Multiple Returns from Care Investment</mark>

Investment in the care economy is framed as generating simultaneous benefits:

- Financial returns for enterprises and investors
- Employment generation for care workers
- Greater economic participation for women
- Better developmental outcomes for children and dignified ageing for the elderly
- Stronger, more resilient communities

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Caution Against Pure Commercialisation</mark>

- Simply commercialising care risks deepening inequality if quality services are accessible only to those who can pay
- If care workers remain poorly paid and insecure, care work is merely shifted from unpaid households to low-paid formal jobs, not genuinely improved
- The solution requires a multi-stakeholder ecosystem: governments, families, communities, businesses, philanthropy, and investors each playing a distinct role

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Specific Opportunity</mark>

- India combines a large working-age population, rising female aspirations, rapid urbanisation, and a growing elderly population
- Affordable, quality childcare and eldercare services have not kept pace with these demographic shifts
- India needs both public provision and private enterprise, not a choice between them
- Government role: set standards, finance essential services, ensure affordability
- Private role: entrepreneurs build new models, investors scale them, philanthropy takes early-stage risks

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context: AVPN Global Conference</mark>

The article is framed around the AVPN (Asian Venture Philanthropy Network) Global Conference in New Delhi, urging investors and policymakers to reframe impact investing questions — not just "how can capital solve social problems" but "how can capital build missing economic systems."

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway Framing</mark>

- Care is described as "part of the economy's foundation," not its "safety net"
- The article reframes the guiding question from "Can we afford to invest in the care economy?" to "Can Asia afford the economic cost of not investing in it?"
- Central call to action: stop treating care as free labour provided by women; start treating it as infrastructure worth investing in
