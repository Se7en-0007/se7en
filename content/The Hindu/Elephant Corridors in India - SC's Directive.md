## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context and SC Order</mark>

On August 17, a Supreme Court bench led by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant directed the Union government to conduct a fresh nationwide survey of elephant corridors. The bench held that corridors cannot be blocked merely out of fear of crop damage, since elephant herds naturally travel long distances.

- Matter arose from an ongoing writ petition on managing human-elephant conflict.
    
- The petition had earlier also sought a ban on using fireballs, spikes, and similar devices to drive elephants away.
    
- In the August 17 order, the Centre was asked to report steps taken to prohibit such "coercive measures" used to divert elephants' natural movement.
    
- Elephant corridors are officially defined and identified by the Environment Ministry.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Are Elephant Corridors</mark>

Elephant corridors are movement pathways that connect two natural habitats, enabling elephants to travel between fragmented forest patches. Disrupting these corridors can trigger human-elephant conflict and directly threaten elephant populations.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Elephants Travel Long Distances</mark>

Asian elephants are highly mobile, social mammals with extensive home ranges.

- A male elephant's average home range spans 50 to 300 square kilometres for food, water and shelter, though this can expand based on resource availability.
    
- Elephants move through their range either in herds or individually.
    
- Home range size varies with habitat type, food availability, water sources, population density, and human disturbance.
    
- Movement is strongly shaped by seasonal and spatial distribution of food and water (per studies by the National Centre for Biological Sciences and Wildlife Institute of India).
    
- Example: Seasonal migration into Kerala occurs during dry months, as elephants seek water and food in the moister forests of the Western Ghats.
    
- Historically, climate change and drought have also driven migration.
    
- Corridors facilitate feeding, natural dispersal, and everyday movement; increasingly, routes are being reshaped by infrastructure, mining, and farmland expansion.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Causes of Corridor Fragmentation</mark>

Multiple human activities disrupt elephant corridors and habitats across India.

- Infrastructure construction and industrial/mining activity are the major disruptors.
    
- Linear infrastructure - highways, existing and new railway lines, canals, and power lines - pushes elephants toward farmlands and settlements, escalating conflict.
    
- Habitat fragmentation drivers vary regionally: changing land use, coffee/tea plantations, invasive plants, and farmland fencing (Western Ghats); railway lines, roads, and power infrastructure (Shivalik and Brahmaputra plains).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Elephant Population Landscapes</mark>

India has four principal elephant population landscapes, each facing distinct pressures.

|Landscape|Key Details|
|---|---|
|Western Ghats|Houses over 50% of India's 22,446 elephants; Karnataka (6,013), Tamil Nadu (3,136), Kerala (2,785); habitat fragmenting due to land-use change, plantations, invasive species, fencing|
|North-Eastern Hills & Brahmaputra floodplains|Corridor disruption from railway lines, roads, power infrastructure, land-use change|
|Shivalik Hills & Gangetic plains|Similar fragmentation patterns as Brahmaputra plains|
|Central India & Eastern Ghats|Biggest conservation challenge; elephant numbers rising due to historical drought and mining pressures|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Central India: A Growing Conservation Challenge</mark>

The Central India and Eastern Ghats landscape presents the most significant conservation difficulty, with a distinct migration history.

- From the mid-to-late 1980s, herd movement into this region increased, initially driven by an El Nino-linked drought in erstwhile southern Bihar.
    
- Later, iron ore mining in Jharkhand and Odisha further pushed elephant migration.
    
- Chhattisgarh has seen elephants arrive from Jharkhand and Odisha over two decades due to habitat loss and mining pressure.
    
- Elephants have subsequently expanded range into Umaria and Shahdol (Madhya Pradesh) and Gadchiroli (Maharashtra).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Importance of Corridor Mapping</mark>

Corridors serve multiple ecological functions beyond simple connectivity.

- They enable genetic exchange and dispersal, maintaining diversity in elephant population pools.
    
- Well-conserved corridors provide access to food and water in natural habitats during seasonal shifts, reducing crop-raiding incidents.
    
- The government's last major corridor mapping exercise (2023) documented 150 elephant corridors across 15 states in the four major landscapes.
    
- A recent Environment Ministry report on human-elephant conflict in Southern India mapped bottlenecks within corridors to highlight challenges elephants face.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Corridor Bottlenecks: Southern India Case Study</mark>

The Southern India report identified specific obstructions across 32 corridors in the region.

- Bottlenecks include: highways, railway projects (e.g., Bengaluru-Chennai railway line), hydel projects, high-tension power lines, electric fences, quarries, corridor narrowing, tea/coffee estates, resorts, and ashrams.
    
- West Bengal recorded the highest number of corridors nationally - 26 identified across its northern and southern parts.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Corridor Example: Charmar-Jingol</mark>

One corridor stands out for its scale and ecological significance in Central India.

- Located largely in Chhattisgarh, it is 20 km long and 2.5 km wide.
    
- It facilitates movement of an estimated 80-100 elephants from Odisha into the interiors of Chhattisgarh, making it ecologically critical for the Central India landscape.
