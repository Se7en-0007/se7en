## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Concept: Two Investor Models</mark>

- **Homo Oeconomicus**: The traditional finance assumption of a rational agent who maximises wealth purely by balancing risk against return; morality plays no role. Underlies modern portfolio theory and the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM).
    
- **Homo Ethicus**: An alternative model from behavioural/ethical finance where the investor derives utility not just from financial gain but also from investing in line with personal values.
    
- **Historical root**: Adam Smith, before _The Wealth of Nations_, argued in _The Theory of Moral Sentiments_ that human behaviour is shaped by sympathy and justice, not self-interest alone.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What is the Nifty 500 Ahimsa Index</mark>

- Launched by the National Stock Exchange (NSE), explicitly inspired by the principle of "Ahimsa" (non-violence).
    
- Screening is based on categorisation by the **Ahimsagain Foundation**; companies in the "orange" and "red" bands (e.g., linked to animal cruelty, sin goods) are excluded.
    
- Derived from the Nifty 500, leaving **326 companies**, weighted by free-float market capitalisation.
    
- Designed as a diversified, broad-market benchmark suitable for ETFs and passive investment products (unlike niche/sectoral indices).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">How It Differs from Other Ethical Indices</mark>

|Index type|Basis of selection|Examples|
|---|---|---|
|Conventional ESG indices|Rate companies on sustainability performance within each sector|Nifty100 ESG, Nifty100 Enhanced ESG, Nifty100 ESG Sector Leaders, MSCI World SRI, FTSE4Good, Dow Jones Sustainability Index, S&P 500 ESG|
|Faith-based indices|Exclude based on religious compliance|Nifty50 Shariah|
|Ahimsa Index|Values-based exclusion — entire industries excluded regardless of individual firm performance|Nifty 500 Ahimsa Index|

The Ahimsa Index is described as closer to **socially responsible and faith-based investing** than to mainstream ESG investing, since it excludes whole industries outright rather than ranking firms within them.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Central Question Raised</mark>

Should morality become an investment criterion? The author argues ethical investing is a legitimate personal choice, but the risk lies in **ethical branding creating a false impression** that investors can satisfy their conscience without any financial trade-off — "there is no free lunch in investing."

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Economic Arguments Against a Free Lunch</mark>

**1. Reduced opportunity set (Markowitz's Modern Portfolio Theory)**

- Analogy: fishing in half a pond instead of the whole pond reduces the chance of the best catch.
    
- Any exclusion narrows the investable universe, shifting the efficient frontier inward.
    
- Consequences: reduced diversification, higher tracking error, potentially lower risk-adjusted returns.
    

**2. The "sin stock" premium (Hong and Kacperczyk)**

- Institutional investors avoiding tobacco, alcohol, and gambling stocks reduce demand for them.
    
- Reduced demand depresses these stocks' prices, which **raises their expected future returns**.
    
- Implication: ethical investors may unintentionally forgo a return premium available in "sin" industries.
    

**3. Ethical stocks becoming overpriced (Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor)**

- As more investors prefer ethical companies, demand bids up their prices.
    
- Higher prices today imply **lower expected returns tomorrow**.
    
- Ethical firms aren't necessarily better or worse businesses — they simply become more expensive assets.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Reframing via Welfare Economics</mark>

- Ethical investing generates **non-pecuniary utility**, not financial alpha — this shifts the debate from pure finance to welfare economics.
    
- Traditional finance judges success only by returns; welfare economics also accounts for values, identity, and personal satisfaction.
    
- Accepting a slightly lower expected return to avoid socially harmful industries is not irrational — the financial sacrifice is offset by **moral utility**.
    
- Analogy given: similar to paying a premium for organic food or renewable energy — partly a consumption choice, not purely an investment one.
    
- Key caveat: "doing good" does not automatically mean "earning higher returns" — there is "no such instant Karma."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Deeper Governance Question</mark>

- Should investors outsource morality to corporations rather than exercising it themselves as consumers, citizens, and philanthropists?
    
- Asking CEOs to resolve moral trade-offs may **weaken accountability**, since corporate leaders are not appointed or directly answerable to a moral standard.
    
- A clear objective like long-term value creation is more transparent than an open-ended commitment to satisfy every stakeholder's values.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Concluding Perspective</mark>

- The success of the Ahimsa Index should not be judged by financial alpha alone.
    
- Its real significance lies in whether it signals a genuine rise of **Homo Ethicus** in Indian capital markets, or whether it is merely morality repackaged as a marketing pitch.
