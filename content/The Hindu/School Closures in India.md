School consolidation in India is a growing policy debate examining whether closing small/low-enrolment government schools represents a loss of access or a step towards improved educational quality. This topic connects directly to Indian polity (Concurrent List, Centre-State relations), education policy (NEP 2020), and socio-economic indicators (TFR, urbanisation).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background and Core Dilemma</mark>

For decades, India's education policy aimed to place a school within reach of every child, making the neighborhood school an instrument of social justice for poor and rural families. However, many such schools have limited infrastructure and operate with very few students or a single teacher, creating a genuine trade-off between access and quality.

- Education falls under the Concurrent List, so States have adopted varying approaches to low-enrolment schools.
    
- Closing a village school can mean longer travel, weaker community participation, and reduced neighborhood access.
    
- A very small or single-teacher school may struggle to provide subject-specific teaching, peer learning, sports, laboratories, and co-curricular activities.
    
- The central question is not how many schools disappeared, but whether affected children now access better schooling.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Makes a School More Than a Classroom</mark>

A school fosters intellectual, social, emotional, and physical development through peer interaction, sports, co-curricular activities, and group discussions-not just individual instruction. Single-teacher and very low-enrolment schools often cannot provide this environment, since one teacher must handle multiple classes, subjects, and administrative duties simultaneously.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Data: Changes Between 2014–15 and 2024–25 (NITI Aayog 2026 Report, based on UDISE+)</mark>

|Indicator|2014-15|2024-25|Change|
|---|---|---|---|
|Total schools|15.16 lakh|14.71 lakh|Down ~45,000|
|Government schools|11.07 lakh|10.13 lakh|Down ~94,000|
|Student enrolment|26.95 crore|24.69 crore|Declined|
|Teachers|89.84 lakh|Over 1 crore|Increased|

**Shift towards private schooling** (Rajya Sabha disclosure, March 2026, 2022-23 to 2024-25):

- Government-school enrolment fell from 13.62 crore to 12.16 crore.
    
- Private-school enrolment rose from 8.42 crore to 9.59 crore.
    

**2024-25 school composition:**

- Government schools: 68.1% of all schools, but only 49.2% of students, 50.9% of teachers.
    
- Private schools: 23.1% of schools, but 38.8% of students, 39.1% of teachers.
    
- Average government school: ~120 students, 5 teachers.
    
- Average private school: ~282 students, ~12 teachers (similar teacher-student ratio, but larger scale).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Is Student Enrolment Falling?</mark>

The primary driver is demographic transition, not necessarily rising dropout rates.

- India's Total Fertility Rate (TFR) has fallen from over 3 in the early 1990s to about 2, below the replacement level of 2.1.
    
- Contributing factors: rising female literacy, improved healthcare, wider family planning access, delayed marriage, changing family aspirations.
    
- Decline is uneven - several southern and western States are already below replacement fertility; many northern States retain younger populations.
    
- Effect is first visible in primary education (fewer children entering Class I), then moves up through the system.
    
- Urbanisation and migration are also reshaping where children study, leaving rural schools under-enrolled while urban/peri-urban schools face pressure.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Lessons from the Gurukul Model</mark>

The Gurukul system offers a philosophical reference point for holistic education, though it cannot be directly replicated in modern mass schooling.

- Led by a Guru/Rishi as academic and administrative head, bringing together students of different ages and abilities.
    
- Education extended beyond formal subjects to character, discipline, physical development, values, and leadership.
    
- Close teacher-student interaction enabled personalised guidance; mixed-age learning encouraged cooperation and peer mentoring.
    
- Modern mass education requires universal access, professional teachers, structured curricula, and defined hours - a different model.
    
- Single-teacher schools resemble one Gurukul feature (close teacher-student bond) but lack the diversity of teachers and peers that enriched the traditional system.
    
- The challenge: combine Gurukul's personalised, holistic spirit with the scale, subject specialisation, and resources of a modern school.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Scale of Very Small Schools</mark>

Data presented by the Union Education Minister in Lok Sabha (December 2025) reveals the extent of ultra-small schools.

- Government schools with fewer than 10 or zero students rose from about 52,300 (2022-23) to over 65,000 (2024-25).
    
- States with large numbers include West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana.
    
- Teachers deployed in these schools increased from 1.26 lakh to 1.44 lakh.
    
- Such schools remain vital for neighborhood access and welfare schemes like the Mid-Day Meal Scheme, especially in remote areas.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">School Consolidation: Arguments and Safeguards</mark>

Several States have pursued consolidation (mergers/rationalisation) to move students from under-enrolled schools into better-equipped composite schools.

**Case for consolidation:**

- Pools resources - teachers, infrastructure, laboratories, sports facilities.
    
- A school with only 15-20 students still requires full staffing and maintenance while struggling to offer specialist teaching.
    
- NEP 2020 supports this via school complexes/clusters, enabling neighboring schools to share teachers, labs, libraries, and sports facilities.
    

**Equity safeguards needed:**

- Efficiency cannot come at the cost of equity.
    
- Neighborhood schools reduce travel and enhance safety/community participation, particularly for young children, girls, children with disabilities, and remote/tribal populations.
    
- Where a school is beyond reasonable walking distance, safe transport or travel assistance should be guaranteed.
    

**Evaluation criteria for consolidation:**

- Do children reach a safe, accessible, better-resourced school?
    
- Does learning improve?
    
- Are teachers deployed more effectively?
    
- Does dropout decline?
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Way Forward</mark>

India must balance access, equity, and quality rather than choosing between neighborhood schools and consolidation outright. Decisions should be data-driven and locally sensitive (factoring in enrolment, geography, distance, and available alternatives) rather than driven by administrative convenience or cost-cutting alone. Success should ultimately be measured not by the number of schools closed or retained, but by whether children learn better, stay enrolled, and experience safe, inclusive, enriching education.
