## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of The Issue</mark>

The Lok Sabha recently amended a law to legalise surcharges or fees on UPI and RuPay debit card transactions. The government has clarified that surcharges would initially apply only to purchases of ₹2,000 and above from businesses crossing a certain turnover threshold, but removing the legal bar gives the government flexibility to expand this anytime.

- Trigger: Legislative amendment passed in Lok Sabha allowing UPI/RuPay surcharges.
    
- Government's stated threshold: ₹2,000+ transactions, applicable to businesses above a turnover limit.
    
- Underlying concern: Legal restriction removed means future scope for surcharges can widen without further debate.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Government's Stated Justification</mark>

RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra's reasoning is that "somebody has to pay" for UPI, since the government reportedly subsidised UPI by about ₹2,000 crore in the past year.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Comparing Physical Currency vs Digital Payments</mark>

The article draws a direct parallel between how physical cash has historically been free and why digital payment systems (like UPI) should also remain free.

|Aspect|Physical Currency System|Digital Payment System (UPI)|
|---|---|---|
|Who bears cost|RBI prints notes/mints coins, given to banks free of charge|Government subsidises UPI infrastructure|
|Cost efficiency|Higher cost, especially for small denomination notes|Lower cost; instant secure messaging between banks|
|Process|Cash withdrawal, physical handover, deposit - tedious and costly|Instant debit-credit messages - trivial and inexpensive|
|Government savings|N/A|₹1,500 crore saved last year in reduced currency printing costs|
|Trend|Static/costly|Costs likely to fall further as tech improves and digital adoption rises|

Key argument: If cash movement was never separately charged for, there is no logical reason digital payments - which are cheaper and more efficient - should be.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Public Goods Logic</mark>

The author argues UPI functions like a public good, similar to lighthouses or public parks, where free access maximizes overall economic benefit.

- Reduced friction in payments increases economic flows and transaction volume.
    
- Charging users for public-good-like services tends to reduce or distort usage and benefits.
    
- Example: European cities offering free transit passes to tourists - this boosts tourist mobility and spending, and governments often recover more via sales tax than they spend on the free pass.
    
- Similarly, subsidising motor fuel increases mobility and economic activity - reduced friction in payments can do this "even more."
    
- Conclusion drawn: If banknotes require no surcharges, digital payments (which need even less effort/cost) should not either.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Real" Reason Behind The Amendment</mark>

The author suggests the actual driver behind allowing UPI surcharges is external pressure rather than domestic economic logic.

- Countries such as Brazil, Indonesia, Vietnam, and Turkey have UPI-like state-run payment systems.
    
- The U.S. government has labeled such systems (India's UPI, Brazil's Pix) as "unfair trade practices" that disadvantage American financial companies like Visa and Mastercard.
    
- In July 2026, the U.S. imposed a 25% tariff on Brazilian imports, explicitly citing Pix as an unfair trade barrier.
    
- India is reportedly finalizing a trade deal with the U.S. and appears to be "checking the boxes" on U.S. demands - unlike Brazil, which held firm.
    
- Practical effect already visible: merchants at retail stores/petrol pumps sometimes claim card machines are "not working" to push customers toward UPI (avoiding surcharges), showing traders' preference for free payment rails.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why This Matters - Author's Concerns</mark>

- **Sovereignty concern**: Changing UPI's public-good architecture under U.S. pressure, to protect Visa/Mastercard's business model, reflects poorly on Indian policy independence.
    
- **Social equity angle**: UPI is described as a matter of financial inclusion and social justice, not merely a market commodity.
    
- **Data/asset control**: Allowing foreign card companies greater space could let them influence one of India's most valuable assets - payments data.
    
- **Policy recommendation**: India should keep UPI free and public, promote competitive domestic payment players, and preserve it as a "public and free switch" rather than adopting the U.S. market-good model for payment systems.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Comparative Case: India vs Brazil</mark>

Brazil's Pix system faced similar U.S. pressure but the Brazilian government did not concede, resulting in a 25% U.S. tariff instead. India, by contrast, appears to be amending its own law preemptively as part of ongoing trade negotiations - a contrast the author uses to question India's stance on economic sovereignty.
