## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Is a Solar Eclipse</mark>

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, blocking some or all of the Sun's light as seen from Earth. This requires a precise alignment of the Sun, Moon, and Earth, making total solar eclipses rare events. A total solar eclipse happens when the Moon completely covers the Sun's disc, while a partial eclipse occurs when only part of the Sun is obscured.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The August 12, 2026 Eclipse Event</mark>

Europe witnessed its first total solar eclipse in 27 years on August 12, 2026. This eclipse was not visible from any location in India.

Key visibility details:

- Path of totality: passed through northern Russia, Greenland, western Iceland, northern Spain, and a small area of northeast Portugal
    
- Duration of totality: maximum of 2 minutes 18 seconds, occurring over Látrabjarg, Iceland
    
- Partial eclipse zones: most of Europe (UK residents saw over 90 percent Sun coverage), North America, and northwest Africa
    
- Totality duration pattern: longest near the centre of the path width-wise, decreasing toward the edges
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Scientists Study the Sun</mark>

Scientists focus heavily on the Sun's corona and inner corona because of their practical and scientific importance.

- Corona definition: the Sun's outermost atmosphere, extending millions of kilometres into space
    
- Inner corona: the part of the outer atmosphere closest to the Sun's surface
    
- Significance: the corona's magnetic field triggers solar flares and coronal mass ejections (CMEs)
    
- Real-world impact: these events can disrupt satellites, GPS systems, and other Earth-based communication infrastructure
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Challenge in Studying the Inner Corona</mark>
<mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;"></mark>
The inner corona is extremely faint compared to the bright solar disc, making it difficult to observe directly.

- Instrument used: a coronagraph, which artificially blocks the solar disc to reveal the corona
    
- Placement: typically mounted on solar probes or space missions
    
- Limitation: coronagraphs are often affected by scattered light, which can make it impossible to properly observe the inner corona
    
- This technical gap is precisely where natural eclipses become scientifically valuable
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why an Eclipse Is Ideal for Corona Study</mark>

During a total solar eclipse, the Moon naturally and perfectly blocks the bright solar disc without the scattered-light problem that affects artificial coronagraphs.

- The inner corona becomes visible as a halo around the darkened lunar body
    
- This gives helio-physicists a rare window for ground-based observation of the solar atmosphere
    
- According to astrophysicist Dibyendu Nandi, blocking the bright inner disk reveals the fainter outer atmosphere, offering clues to the underlying magnetic structure of the corona
    
- Ground-based eclipse observation complements space-based coronagraph data
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Solar Cycle Connection</mark>

The Sun follows an approximately 11-year solar cycle, during which its activity rises and falls.

- Indicators of solar activity: number of sunspots, solar flares, and related phenomena
    
- Current cycle: began in 2020
    
- Status as of the 2026 eclipse: the Sun has already passed its maxima phase (period of peak activity)
    
- Significance: more complex structures in the inner corona are typically visible closer to solar maximum, making eclipse timing relative to the solar cycle scientifically important
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Scientists Expected to Observe</mark>

Indian researchers at the Centre of Excellence for Space Science India (CESSI), IISER-Kolkata, made specific predictions ahead of the eclipse.

- Predicted observation: multiple large-scale "streamers" — petal-like structures in the corona
    
- Nature of streamers: bright magnetic field structures rooted to the solar surface
    
- Scientific value: eclipses are the only way to see these magnetic field structures directly, since the Sun's disc is normally too bright
    
- Expectation: if spotted, these structures would likely be highly complex and spectacular in appearance
