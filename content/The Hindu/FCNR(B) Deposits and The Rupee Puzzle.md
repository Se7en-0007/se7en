## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Core Puzzle</mark>

India received $49 billion in foreign inflows during June–July 2026 through FCNR(B) deposits, foreign loans, and government bond investments, yet the rupee barely moved. On the relevant Friday, the rupee closed at 95.39 per dollar — just 0.4% stronger than its level on June 4, the day before the government and RBI announced measures to boost these inflows.

This is starkly different from the 2013 episode, when a similar FCNR(B) swap window caused the rupee to rise 10.3% (from 67.6 to 61.3 per dollar) within the first 40 days.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Are FCNR(B) Deposits and How the Swap Works</mark>

- FCNR(B) stands for Foreign Currency Non-Resident (Bank) deposits, a scheme allowing NRIs to park foreign currency in Indian banks.
    
- Banks collect these dollar deposits and swap them directly with the RBI in exchange for rupees.
    
- Because the dollars go straight to the RBI, they never enter the open market for foreign currency and do not directly affect open-market dollar demand/supply.
    
- The rupee's exchange rate is determined by supply and demand for dollars specifically in the open market — similar to how a bumper harvest crashing tomato supply lowers tomato prices.
    
- NRIs are being offered 19x leverage on these deposits, driving intense interest, with banks reporting queues of depositors (minimum deposits are often around 500,000 dollars, though some banks accept as low as 50,000 dollars).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why RBI's Reserves Didn't Rise as Expected</mark>

Between June 8 and July 17, Indian banks raised 17 billion dollars via FCNR(B) deposits, but RBI's Foreign Currency Assets (FCA) rose by only about 7 billion dollars — leaving a 10 billion dollar gap. Several explanations account for this mismatch:

- **Operational/reporting delay**: Each bank has a designated weekly window to swap deposits with the RBI, so FCNR(B) inflows may show up in RBI's FCA data with a lag, as noted by SBI's Group Chief Economic Advisor Soumya Kanti Ghosh.
    
- **Spot market intervention**: Renewed US-Iran tensions in late July weakened the rupee, prompting the RBI to sell dollars in the spot market, offsetting some inflows.
    
- **Forward market operations**: The RBI sold dollars heavily in the forward market — 103 billion dollars net at end-June (near the all-time high of 107 billion dollars in May) — a method that defends the rupee without immediate dollar sales, but commits the RBI to deliver dollars later (around 9 billion dollars due in July alone).
    
- **Bank hedging behavior**: Since RBI only bears exchange rate risk on the principal, banks must independently hedge their interest payment obligations, which raises future dollar demand and adds pressure on the rupee.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Comparing 2013–14 vs 2026 FCNR(B) Episodes</mark>

|Aspect|2013–14 Scheme|2026 Scheme|
|---|---|---|
|Rupee movement|Rose 10.3% in 40 days (67.6 to 61.3/dollar)|Only 0.4% stronger in comparable period|
|Trigger context|Local imbalances, taper tantrum-driven crisis|Global risks (West Asia conflict, oil prices, strong dollar)|
|Key architect|Announced under incoming RBI Governor Raghuram Rajan|Announced by government and RBI jointly|
|Effectiveness driver|Addressed India-specific perception problems|Offset by global headwinds, limiting effectiveness|

Economists Sanjay Mathur and Dhiraj Nim of ANZ noted that such schemes work best when addressing local imbalances and country-specific negative perceptions, not global risk factors — explaining why the current scheme has had a muted effect compared to 2013.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Outlook for FCNR(B) and the Rupee</mark>

- If the current pace continues, bankers expect NRI inflows to reach as much as 75 billion dollars by September 30, 2026, when the scheme window closes.
    
- UBS Chief India Economist Tanvee Gupta Jain projects the rupee will end fiscal year 2026–27 largely unchanged, around 96 per dollar.
    
- This flat trajectory reflects a balance between a modestly improved near-term external outlook and persistent medium-term structural headwinds.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Structural Headwinds for the Rupee</mark>

- Need to attract higher net Foreign Direct Investment (FDI).
    
- Risks to IT services exports from AI-driven disruption.
    
- Potential for a stronger dollar if the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates.
    
- Ongoing global risk factors such as the West Asia conflict and elevated oil prices.
