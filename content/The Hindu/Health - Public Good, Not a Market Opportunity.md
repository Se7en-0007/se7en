## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

The article uses World Humanitarian Day (August 19) as an occasion to highlight a "slow-burning" health crisis in South Asia - one built over years through inefficient policies and profit-driven systems, rather than sudden disasters like outbreaks or conflict. The central thesis: health systems should be judged not by profit generation but by whether the least powerful person can access timely, affordable, respectful care.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Nature of the Crisis in South Asia</mark>

- The region possesses strong assets: medical talent, pharmaceutical manufacturing capacity, public health experience, and community resilience.
    
- Simultaneously, it carries a heavy burden of preventable illness, malnutrition, infectious diseases, climate-related health risks, and high out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE).
    
- For many families, a health crisis begins not with an ambulance but when a treatable condition becomes unaffordable or diagnosis is delayed, forcing trade-offs between food, rent, education, medicine, and wages.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">MSF's On-Ground Observations</mark>

- Médecins Sans Frontières (Doctors Without Borders) reports that patients often reach free care only after exhausting savings, selling assets, or borrowing money.
    
- By the time they access such care, illness has often escalated into a broader monetary and social crisis.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Data Points on India's Health Financing</mark>

- Per-capita out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) in India rose 28.4% over five years, from 2018-19 to 2022-23.
    
- India's public share of total health expenditure grew to around 48% in this period, but remains well below China, Brazil, and most OECD countries.
    
- Medical inflation in India was recorded at 14% by a corporate health report.
    
- The same report found 71% of workers paid for healthcare out of pocket, while only 15% had employer-supported insurance.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Critique of Insurance-Led Models</mark>

- Insurance has a role but cannot substitute for public investment in healthcare infrastructure.
    
- A "health card" is not equivalent to a functioning primary healthcare network.
    
- A reimbursement package cannot replace a staffed clinic with non-burnt-out doctors and nurses, reliable labs, affordable medicines, counselling, nutrition support, and community follow-up.
    
- If public healthcare foundations are weak, channeling public money to private providers ("purchasing care from private providers") does not automatically create equity - it merely shifts funds without guaranteeing sustained care for the neediest.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Public Health as Investment, Not Expense</mark>

- Investing in public healthcare should be seen as an investment in productivity, resilience, and human dignity - not a "welfare expense to be minimised."
    
- Healthy children learn better; healthy adults work, care, innovate, and contribute economically.
    
- Families shielded from catastrophic health costs are more likely to invest in education and long-term livelihoods.
    
- Public health systems protect not just individuals but the future productive capacity of societies.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Sri Lanka as a Regional Case Study</mark>

- Despite resource constraints and economic pressures, Sri Lanka has been recognised for strong public-sector health delivery.
    
- It shows notable progress on maternal and child health, immunisation, and life expectancy.
    
- Key takeaway: health outcomes are shaped by political choices (sustained public investment, accessible primary care, trained health workforce, payment-independent access) - not solely by national wealth.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Regional and Cross-Border Dimensions</mark>

South Asia's health challenges transcend national borders, making public health investment a matter of regional preparedness rather than purely domestic policy.

- Outbreaks, antimicrobial resistance (AMR), climate shocks, displacement, and conflict are inherently regional issues.
    
- A weak surveillance system in one country can affect neighbouring communities.
    
- Climate disasters can displace populations across districts and international borders.
    
- Drug-resistant infections do not respect national boundaries.
    
- Preparedness must be built into everyday public health systems, not activated only during crises; humanitarian response saves lives in emergencies but cannot substitute for sustained public investment.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Medical Education and Workforce Planning</mark>

- The region needs to strengthen the public character of medical education, research, and health workforce planning.
    
- Merely expanding medical seats is insufficient - public medical colleges require adequate funding and protected training quality, alongside stronger research capacity.
    
- South Asia needs doctors, nurses, lab staff, public health professionals, counsellors, and community health workers willing to serve in diverse and difficult settings, not just profitable urban markets.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Special Role</mark>

- As the region's largest country and a major producer of medicines and health technologies, India's policy choices affect not just its own population but also regional access to medicines, diagnostics, research, manufacturing, and public health cooperation.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Concluding Message</mark>

The author argues that the true tribute to humanitarian responders is building systems that "fail fewer people in the first place." South Asia's future health security depends on governments treating health as a shared public good - building healthcare systems from "the last person first," rather than treating illness as a market opportunity.
