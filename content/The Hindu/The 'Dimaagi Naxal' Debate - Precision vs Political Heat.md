## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

The controversy stems from PM Narendra Modi's use of the term "dimaagi Naxal" (intellectual Naxal), which triggered a political exchange between the ruling and opposition parties. Kiren Rijiju invoked Manmohan Singh's 2006 remark calling Naxalism India's "single biggest internal security challenge," while P. Chidambaram countered by calling himself "proud to be a dimaagi Naxal," reversing the label as a badge of dissent.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Argument of the Article</mark>

The author argues both political sides are scoring points without answering the central question: what conduct actually defines a "dimaagi Naxal"? The piece calls for definitional precision rather than partisan heat, distinguishing genuine security threats from mere political disagreement.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Naxalism: Historical Background</mark>

- Term originated from the 1967 Naxalbari uprising, evolving into various revolutionary movements.
    
- It later split into multiple organisations and strategies.
    
- Contemporary left-wing extremism is more specifically tied to CPI(Maoist).
    
- "Naxal" has never simply meant communist, left-wing, anti-government, or critical of the ruling party - it has a distinct organisational/violent connotation.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Two Possible Meanings of "Dimaagi Naxal"</mark>

|Interpretation|Nature|Appropriate Response|
|---|---|---|
|Member, recruiter, financier, or material supporter of a violent Naxalite organisation|Criminal conduct|Identify conduct, gather evidence, apply law|
|Person who sympathises with grievances, criticises government's tribal-area or security policy, or holds radical views|Political/ideological expression|Constitutionally protected; cannot be criminalised merely by the label|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Constitutional and Legal Angle</mark>

- The Supreme Court's ruling in Shreya Singhal v. Union of India distinguishes between discussion, advocacy, and incitement.
    
- Free speech protection does not vanish because an idea is unpopular or radical, but it is not a licence for incitement to violence or unlawful participation.
    
- This distinction protects "categories," not any specific ideology - one can study Naxalism, report on human rights abuses, or examine social causes of insurgency without endorsing violence.
    
- Conversely, actual recruiters/financiers of armed groups cannot escape liability by claiming to be "activists" or "intellectuals."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Phrase "Identify and Isolate"</mark>

- The article flags this phrase as needing scrutiny: identify based on what evidence, by which institution, under what legal standard?
    
- "Isolate" is ambiguous - it could mean legitimately dismantling recruitment/financing networks (acceptable) or socially/professionally isolating citizens for their presumed political opinions (serious constitutional concern).
    
- The government is urged to clarify this rather than leave it to political interpretation.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Critique of the Manmohan Singh Comparison</mark>

- Rijiju's invocation of Singh's 2006 statement establishes Naxalism as a genuine security threat but does not validate "dimaagi Naxal" as a well-defined legal/policy category.
    
- Singh was addressing an armed movement and state response, not proposing a category of citizens identified by presumed ideological mindset.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Pattern of Political Labelling in India</mark>

- The article situates "dimaagi Naxal" within a broader trend of labels: "Urban Naxal," "tukde-tukde gang," "Andolanjeevi," "anti-national."
    
- These labels differ in origin but share a common function: shifting debate from a person's actual words/actions to an imputed identity.
    
- Effective as politics, but problematic as a basis for public policy or law enforcement.
    
- Chidambaram's "proud to be a dimaagi Naxal" response inadvertently shows the term's ambiguity - usable as an accusation by one side and as a badge of pride by the other.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Balanced Political Points</mark>

- BJP's position: Naxal violence is real; families of victims (security personnel, civilians) do not see it as an academic debate; the state must protect them.
    
- Opposition's position: Equally entitled to ask where the line lies between government criticism and actual ideological support for armed insurgency.
    
- Author's view: Neither point invalidates the other - both are legitimate concerns.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why the State Needs Greater Precision</mark>

- A political party's harmful use of a label is limited to rhetoric.
    
- A state's powers are far greater - it can investigate, prosecute, surveil, and restrict liberty.
    
- This asymmetry of power is why governmental language on such categories requires more precision than political sloganeering.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Concluding Standard</mark>

- Understanding social/political roots of insurgency ≠ justifying violence.
    
- Criticising counter-insurgency policy ≠ supporting insurgency.
    
- Defending free expression ≠ defending every person invoking it.
    
- Proposed consistent standard: judge conduct by evidence, judge arguments by merit, and avoid converting political disagreement into criminal/extremist allegations without establishing an actual conduct-based link.
    
- Final message: India can be uncompromising against armed extremism while still preserving space for dissent - a democratic state's strength lies in doing both simultaneously.
