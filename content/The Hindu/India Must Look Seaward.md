## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Argument</mark>

India's foreign policy needs a stronger maritime orientation because thousands of Indian seafarers work in complex, multi-jurisdictional environments with no clear line of accountability. The current consular framework, built around fixed territories, is poorly suited to a workforce whose "workplace moves" constantly across borders.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Jurisdictional Problem</mark>

A single Indian seafarer can be recruited in Mumbai, employed by a Singapore-based company, serve on a Panama-flagged ship, carry Kuwaiti oil, face an attack off Oman, and get detained in a fifth country entirely.

- No single authority is fully responsible for such a seafarer
    
- Consular systems are organised by territory, but seafarers cross multiple jurisdictions in one voyage
    
- Indian missions may not even know a national is nearby until a crisis (seizure, abandonment, attack) occurs
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scale of the Workforce</mark>

- India's seafarer workforce: about 3.2 lakh as of June 2025, nearly three times its size in 2014
    
- The 2026 Seafarer Workforce Report ranks India second globally, behind the Philippines, narrowly ahead of China
    
- India supplies just over 12% of the global seafarer supply
    
- 1,125 Indian seafarers were abandoned in 2025 — the highest of any nationality, per International Transport Workers' Federation data
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recent Casualties and Risk Zones</mark>

- 14 Indians killed in attacks linked to the West Asia conflict since February 28, 2026; at least 8 were seafarers on commercial ships
    
- High-risk zones: Strait of Hormuz, Black Sea, Red Sea (missiles, drones, mines), Gulf of Aden and West Africa (piracy, hostage-taking)
    
- On July 15, 2026, the government directed shipowners and recruitment agencies to avoid deploying Indian seafarers on vessels through the Strait of Hormuz until further notice
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Government's "Seafarer First" Response</mark>

|Measure|Details|
|---|---|
|Accountability mandate|Officials must track every Indian seafarer in West Asia, regardless of vessel flag|
|Dashboard|Tracks ships, threats, and crew welfare in real time|
|Liaison officers|Assigned to each affected family|
|Vessel ban|May 14, 2026 — Directorate General of Shipping barred agencies from placing seafarers on 366 vessels linked to abandonment cases, unless compliance conditions are met|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Gaps in the Current System</mark>

- Technology limitation: tracking apps show ship location but not whether the seafarer is injured, detained, or in danger — a "moving dot is a poor substitute for a phone call"
    
- Dashboard cannot compel flag states or owners to act or grant access to a detained seafarer
    
- India lacks a standing maritime consular protocol defining who takes charge from the moment a distress signal is received
    
- Bilateral maritime agreements mostly cover certificate recognition and job access, but say little about legal assistance, consular access, or repatriation
    
- Flag states are often quick to register ships but slow to enforce Maritime Labour Convention obligations (wages, abandonment, repatriation)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Proposed Reforms</mark>

- Establish designated officers in major shipping centres familiar with port authorities, hospitals, insurers, and local lawyers
    
- Build coalitions with the Philippines and Indonesia (facing similar issues) to push for common global standards via the International Maritime Organization (IMO) and International Labour Organization (ILO)
    
- Ensure recruitment agencies supply current crew and route data during high-risk activations
    
- Guarantee a seafarer's "right to know": true vessel ownership, sailing routes, sanction status, insurance validity, and past abandonment record before signing a contract
    
- Allow seafarers to refuse deployment into designated high-risk regions and return home without penalty
    
- Require agencies to proactively disclose banned-vessel lists rather than making seafarers search for them
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Institutions and Frameworks Mentioned</mark>

- Directorate General of Shipping (India) — regulatory body issuing vessel bans and advisories
    
- International Transport Workers' Federation (ITF) — tracks global seafarer abandonment data
    
- Maritime Labour Convention — international treaty on seafarer welfare enforced (unevenly) by flag states
    
- International Maritime Organization (IMO) and International Labour Organization (ILO) — proposed platforms for coalition-building on common standards
