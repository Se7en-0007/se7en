Strait of Hormuz disruption is turning oil markets from a "war premium" phase to a "delivery premium" phase — the world has enough oil, but not the logistics to move it reliably.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Price Snapshot</mark>

- Brent crude: $90.46/barrel (Tuesday reference point)
    
- WTI (West Texas Intermediate): $82.43/barrel
    
- Murban crude (key Asian benchmark): ~$101.40/barrel
    
- Different benchmarks diverge, but all signal the market entering a riskier phase
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Strait of Hormuz — Why It Matters</mark>

- Pre-conflict flow through Hormuz: ~21 million barrels/day (crude + petroleum liquids)
    
- EIA data: flows fell from 21.6 mb/d (Q4 2025) to just 4.9 mb/d (Q2 2026)
    
- Hormuz is the principal maritime outlet for Gulf crude oil and LNG
    
- Saudi Arabia and UAE have pipeline bypass routes, but these only partially offset disruption
    
- Key distinction: occasional tanker movement ≠ normalisation; shipping must be regular, safe, "financeable," and insurable
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">From War Premium to Delivery Premium</mark>

- Markets tolerate geopolitical tension if shipping continues normally
    
- Markets become unforgiving when: tanker movement is uncertain, marine insurance costs spike, cargoes are delayed, refiners compete for replacement grades
    
- Sanctions add complexity: a barrel can physically exist but be commercially unavailable if banks, insurers, or shipowners refuse to support the transaction
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Role of Inventories</mark>

- Inventories act as a buffer, not a permanent solution
    
- U.S. commercial crude stocks: ~429 million barrels (mid-August)
    
- U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR): ~293 million barrels — lowest in over four decades
    
- China: an "opaque variable" — does not publish comprehensive strategic/commercial inventory data
    
- Key limitation: a barrel stored in the U.S. cannot instantly replace a delayed Gulf cargo at an Asian refinery (needs release, matching to refinery needs, and transport)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Three Price Scenarios (Next 4–6 Weeks)</mark>

|Scenario|Conditions|Brent Price Range|
|---|---|---|
|Rapid normalisation|Sustained tanker traffic resumes, insurance/freight ease, predictable loadings|$80–$95|
|Prolonged impasse (most likely)|Hormuz remains constrained, bypass routes prevent outright shortage, inventories absorb shock|$95–$110|
|Wider escalation|Tanker traffic worsens, export infrastructure hit, sanctions constrain alternatives|$110–$130 (could push toward $120+)|

- Author's assessment: prolonged impasse is most probable — Brent could move above $100 repeatedly without staying there continuously
    
- A durable return below $90 requires visible restoration of shipping, insurance, payment channels, and physical deliveries — not just reassuring rhetoric
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Significance of the $100 Threshold</mark>

- Not "physically magical," but politically significant
    
- Above $100: importing governments face greater inflationary and subsidy pressures
    
- Rising costs for transport, farming, and manufacturing sectors
    
- Strategic-stock releases and retail-price interventions become more politically likely
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Where the Real Pain Shows First</mark>

- Not necessarily in headline Brent price — may appear first in: tanker freight rates, war-risk insurance costs, diesel cracks, aviation-fuel prices
    
- IEA data (August report): global refinery runs in July were ~5 million barrels/day below year-earlier levels
    
- Refining margins in Atlantic Basin hit record highs due to tighter light/middle-distillate markets
    
- Diesel exports from Russia, West Asia, and Asia: 1.3 million barrels/day lower year-on-year
    
- Key takeaway for importers: the relevant number is the delivered barrel and delivered product cost, not just crude futures price
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Position — Flexibility, Not Immunity</mark>

- Indian refiners can process a diverse crude basket and switch sourcing rapidly — a genuine strategic strength
    
- Limitation: refinery flexibility cannot eliminate geography
    
- Replacing nearby Gulf crude with Atlantic Basin crude means: longer voyages, greater tanker requirements, higher freight, more working capital
    
- Not all crude grades are interchangeable without affecting refinery yields/economics
    
- Critical distinction: diversification of crude supply (India can strengthen this) vs. security of crude delivery (India cannot fully insulate against this)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Four Indicators to Watch (More Important Than Daily Brent Moves)</mark>

1. Sustained tanker movement through Hormuz — a handful of voyages doesn't establish normality
    
2. Marine war-risk insurance and tanker freight — elevated levels keep effective supply cost high even if crude price softens
    
3. Pricing of Gulf/Asian physical crude relative to global benchmarks — persistent premiums signal regional tightness
    
4. Inventory drawdown and product-market stress — elevated diesel/aviation-fuel margins despite "calm" crude prices signal deeper disruption
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Gulf Production Recovery Data (IEA)</mark>

- Gulf oil production recovered to 23.9 million barrels/day in July
    
- Still 8.3 million barrels/day below pre-war levels
    
- Regional exports (including bypass routes): fell to ~15 million barrels/day
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway</mark>

The world has sufficient oil supply globally, but lacks the ability to move it — with the speed, certainty, and cost previously taken for granted — from producers to consumers. Continued volatility with an upward bias is expected over the next 4–6 weeks, with $100 Brent likely to be repeatedly tested rather than being an extreme outlier.
