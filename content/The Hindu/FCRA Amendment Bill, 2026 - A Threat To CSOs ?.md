## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of FCRA</mark>

- Foreign contribution regulation in India began with the FCRA, 1976, enacted during Indira Gandhi's Emergency, driven by fears of foreign forces destabilising India's democracy through NGOs.
    
- Before 1976, there was no statutory framework governing foreign contributions in India.
    
- The 1976 Act was replaced by the FCRA, 2010, which continued the regulatory approach but was not "unduly harsh" on recipients.
    
- Foreign contributions have never been prohibited outright in India; they were always permitted, but subject to progressively stricter regulation with each amendment.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Provisions of FCRA, 2010</mark>

- Primary focus was on legislature and political parties, addressing fears of large-scale foreign money influencing Indian politics.
    
- Prohibited foreign contributions to: candidates for election, media personnel, public servants, judges, government employees, legislators, political parties/office-bearers, and organisations of a political nature.
    
- Mandated a certificate of registration for any person/organisation engaged in cultural, economic, educational, religious, or social programmes before receiving foreign contributions.
    
- Provided for vesting of foreign-contribution-created assets in a prescribed authority only under certain specific circumstances.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What's New in the FCRA Amendment Bill, 2026</mark>

- Listed for consideration in the monsoon session of Parliament 2026; was earlier postponed after protests, particularly from Christian organisations and NGOs.
    
- Government has reaffirmed commitment to passing it, dispelling expectations of an indefinite deferral.
    
- Described as having a "sledgehammer effect" on NGOs and religious/cultural organisations — a marked shift from earlier regulatory (not punitive) approach.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Certificate Cancellation Grounds</mark>

- A registration certificate can be denied/cancelled if the applicant has been prosecuted, convicted, or found indulging in religious conversion through inducement or force.
    
- Also cancellable if the applicant is prosecuted/convicted for creating communal tension or disharmony.
    
- Section 14(1)(c) allows cancellation if the central government believes it is "necessary in the public interest" — a vague standard granting the government virtually unfettered discretion.
    
- Concern: it is easy for anyone to file a complaint alleging forcible conversion to trigger cancellation, creating high potential for misuse, especially against minority religious groups.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Asset Vesting Mechanism (Section 16A)</mark>

|Aspect|Provision|
|---|---|
|Partial foreign funding|Even if an asset is only partly created from foreign contribution, the _entire_ asset vests in the designated authority|
|Initial vesting|Provisional|
|If certificate not renewed/restored in time|Vesting becomes permanent|
|Disposal of assets|Authority may transfer assets to central/state government or local authority, or sell/auction them|
|Sale proceeds|Credited to the Consolidated Fund of India|
|Voluntary surrender of certificate|Under Clause 16A(1)(b), all assets and unutilised foreign contribution still vest in the authority|

- This means even organisations that voluntarily stop needing foreign funds and surrender their certificate lose all related assets — a provision the article calls difficult to justify rationally.
    
- Assets that could vest include places of worship, depending on the organisation's nature.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Exemption Clause Concerns</mark>

- Clause 16L allows the government to exempt any organisation, class of organisations, or person from the Bill's operation if "in the public interest."
    
- This clause is flagged as potentially unconstitutional under Article 14 (equality before law) since it lacks an intelligible differentia distinguishing exempted entities and no rational nexus to the law's objective.
    
- Exemption is left entirely to government discretion/opinion, raising concerns of arbitrary and selective application.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Overall Assessment</mark>

- The article argues the amendments reflect a shift from _regulating_ foreign contributions to enabling the _elimination_ of civil society organisations working in educational, cultural, environmental, and charitable sectors.
    
- The author suggests this stems from "deep-seated prejudices" rather than genuine regulatory necessity.
    
- Author: P.D.T. Achary, former Secretary General, Lok Sabha.
