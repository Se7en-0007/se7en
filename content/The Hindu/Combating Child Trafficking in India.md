Trafficking is defined not as a single act but as a chain - recruitment, transportation, harbouring or receiving a person through force, deception, or abuse of vulnerability, for exploitation (sexual exploitation, forced labour, servitude, forced marriage, or begging). This definition was shaped by a 2011 Supreme Court case aligned with the UN Protocol on trafficking.

- Committee concluded trafficking of young girls is among the largest, most hidden forms of rape in society.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scale of The Crisis</mark>

- 2025 U.S. Trafficking in Persons Report: 1,02,027 victims identified globally in 2024, but only 7,975 convictions (roughly 1 conviction per 13 victims).
    
- India's NCRB data (2024): 2,135 trafficking cases registered, 6,018 victims, including 2,297 children.
    
- Many more cases remain hidden within missing persons reports, child labour rescues, and forced marriage cases filed under other laws.
    
- NGOs and law enforcement have rescued over 1,50,000 children from trafficking in India in the last three years.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legal Framework in India</mark>

Trafficking provisions are scattered across multiple laws rather than unified under one authority, causing accountability gaps.

- Constitution of India - foundational protections.
    
- Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS) - Sections 140-144 criminalize trafficking offences.
    
- POCSO Act - protection of children from sexual offences.
    
- Juvenile Justice Act - child welfare and rehabilitation provisions.
    
- Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), Section 33 - mandates any person aware of trafficking to report it to the nearest Magistrate or police station.
    
- BNSS, Section 34 - extends reporting duty specifically to panchayat members and village residents for non-bailable offences including trafficking.
    

Key gap: no single authority owns the entire chain from prevention to prosecution to rehabilitation.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Prevention Strategy (Whole-of-System Response)</mark>

Requires coordination across policing, labour administration, railways, local government, judiciary, and civil society.

- Schools: report absenteeism early before it becomes disappearance; link vulnerable children to scholarships/schemes.
    
- Welfare delivery: entitlements must reach poor families before poverty pushes them toward recruiters.
    
- Inter-state coordination: enforcement across source, transit, and destination states must match traffickers' own coordination.
    
- Community duty: villages/panchayats legally obligated (BNSS Sec 34) but largely unaware of this duty; almost none held accountable.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Post-Rescue Response - Three Pillars of Recovery</mark>

1. School - return to education.
    
2. Skill - livelihood and skill-development support.
    
3. Scheme - access to welfare entitlements reducing vulnerability to re-trafficking.
    

Additional requirements:

- Child Welfare Committees must provide immediate medical care, trauma-informed counselling, legal aid, and legally guaranteed compensation.
    
- Fast-track courts must complete trials within a defined timeframe - delays prolong trauma and expose survivors/witnesses to intimidation.
    
- Judicial accountability is described as inseparable from criminal justice response, not a separate function.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Trafficking as an Economic Crime - Disruption Approach</mark>

The article frames trafficking fundamentally as a profit-driven economic crime rather than isolated criminal acts.

- Demand finances supply: commercial sexual exploitation exists because of buyers; forced labour exists because of employers benefiting from exploitation.
    
- "No innocent link" - the customer and the employer who looks away are both culpable.
    
- Recommended strategy: dismantle networks and confiscate proceeds, rather than merely arresting individual links in the chain.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">International</mark>

Trafficking is called a "borderless crime that demands a borderless response," requiring:

- Intelligence sharing between countries.
    
- Joint investigations and extradition mechanisms.
    
- Mutual legal assistance and financial investigations tracing money across borders.
    
- Proposed: an international registry of convicted traffickers to prevent cross-border relocation of offenders.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Concluding Argument</mark>

Bhuwan Ribhu (child rights activist, founder of Just Rights for Children) argues that a "Viksit Bharat" (Developed India) vision must be measured not just by economic growth or infrastructure, but by whether institutions protect every child before organised crime reaches them.
