## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

India's proposed Corporate Average Fuel Efficiency (CAFE) III norms arrive at a pivotal moment as the global auto industry shifts from internal combustion engines (ICE) to electrified powertrains. The framework India chooses will shape investment flows, technology adoption and long-term energy security, especially given India's heavy dependence on imported crude oil and current geopolitical tensions.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Are CAFE Norms</mark>

- CAFE norms set annual sales-weighted average fuel-efficiency targets for a manufacturer's entire passenger vehicle fleet, not individual models.
    
- Origin: Introduced in the United States in 1975, following the 1973 Arab oil embargo, to cut oil dependence and shield consumers from high fuel costs.
    
- Effect in the US: Pushed automakers toward smaller, more efficient cars and helped Japanese manufacturers rise globally.
    
- From the 1990s, fuel-efficiency rules gained a second goal — reducing greenhouse gas emissions — with the US EPA's Endangerment Finding bringing GHGs under the Clean Air Act.
    
- These technology-neutral rules spurred innovation in hybrids and EVs (e.g., the 2010 US Department of Energy's $465 million loan to Tesla).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">China's Dual Credit System (Comparative Model)</mark>

- China introduced mandatory fuel-consumption standards in 2004, initially by vehicle weight rather than fleet average.
    
- In 2018, China shifted to a Dual Credit System combining two requirements:
    
    - Corporate Average Fuel Consumption (CAFC) standards
        
    - New Energy Vehicle (NEV) credit requirements
        
- Manufacturers deficient in NEV credits (e.g., due to lack of EVs) must buy credits from EV-heavy manufacturers, creating a market that rewards electrification and penalises delay.
    
- CAFC credits can be banked/transferred within corporate groups; NEV credits cannot be offset the same way — making NEV compliance stricter.
    
- Result: China sold over 13 million electric cars in 2025 — about 55% of new passenger vehicle sales, versus roughly 27% in the EU, 10% in the US, and only about 4% in India.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's CAFE III Proposal — Key Features</mark>

|Aspect|Detail|
|---|---|
|Notification|Power Ministry's draft dated July 16, 2026 (third iteration)|
|Target|Reduce average emissions from ~113 gCO2/km to 77 gCO2/km by FY2031-32|
|Earlier exemption|Small/lighter cars were initially carved out from full stringency; this exemption has since been removed|
|Industry stance|Some manufacturers pushed for stricter norms, others sought flexibility|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Flexibility Mechanisms That Dilute Stringency</mark>

- **Carbon Neutrality Factor**: Gives compliance benefit for ethanol-blend-compatible vehicles, even though government has not committed to moving beyond E20 (20% ethanol-80% petrol) blending; ethanol is costlier and has lower energy density/mileage than petrol.
    
- **Super credits**: Extra weight given to BEVs, plug-in hybrids, strong hybrids and flex-fuel vehicles in fleet calculations, meaning fewer actual low-emission sales are needed to offset high-emission models; strong hybrids get this benefit despite only partial electrification.
    
- **Banking and trading of credits**: Manufacturers exceeding targets can sell surplus credits; those in deficit can buy credits directly from the Bureau of Energy Efficiency (BEE) at fixed administrative prices, making BEE a "seller of last resort" rather than a true market exchange.
    
- **Buyout pricing**: BEE credit buyout starts at ₹2,500 per gram CO2/km in FY2028, rising to ₹4,500 by FY2032 — less than half the effective penalty rate implied by the Energy Conservation Act (roughly ₹5,000+ per gram CO2/km equivalent).
    
- **Compliance blocks**: Assessment shifts from three-year blocks to two-year blocks, allowing manufacturers to average performance across years rather than meet annual targets — so a shortfall in one year can be offset later.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The CNG Precedent and Policy Lesson</mark>

India's own CNG experience shows regulation can shape markets rather than merely follow them — once policy support aligned with regulatory certainty, manufacturers rapidly rolled out CNG variants. This illustrates that ambitious, well-designed regulation can drive technology shifts rather than just accommodate existing industry preferences.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Argument and Stakes</mark>

- Manufacturers' own voluntary commitments already average ~20% EV share by 2030, with some OEMs targeting 30%+ — meaning the revised CAFE III regulation asks for only about half of what industry has already committed to voluntarily.
    
- Strengthening fuel-efficiency standards is framed as more than an environmental issue — it is presented as an energy-security strategy, industrial policy tool and macroeconomic imperative, given India's crude oil import dependence and exposure to geopolitical shocks, imported inflation and currency pressure.
    
- The article's core conclusion: revising CAFE norms is an opportunity for India to address a macroeconomic vulnerability while helping meet its Glasgow commitment on energy efficiency, and to position itself among the world's leading automotive markets in the low-carbon mobility transition.
