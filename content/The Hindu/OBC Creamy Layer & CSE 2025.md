## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context of The Issue</mark>

- On August 25, 2026, the Supreme Court agreed to set up a Special Bench to hear the Centre's plea on whether its March 2026 verdict on OBC creamy layer criteria applies to CSE 2025.
    
- Core dispute: what happens when courts change legal criteria after an examination process has largely concluded.
    
- The March 2026 judgment held that salary income alone cannot be the basis for excluding children of PSU/private-sector employees from OBC reservation benefits.
    
- This ruling came just five days after CSE 2025 results were declared, creating a timing conflict.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Origin of the Creamy Layer Dispute</mark>

- Case traces back to a DoPT letter dated October 14, 2004, clarifying a September 1993 Official Memorandum (OM) on the creamy layer criterion for OBCs.
    
- Creamy layer definition: economically, socially, and educationally advanced OBC members excluded from reservation benefits.
    
- 1993 OM: excluded salary income and agricultural income from the income/wealth test for creamy layer determination.
    
- 2004 clarification: added salary income of PSU and private-sector employees as a criterion for creamy layer status.
    
- Implementation timeline: not applied effectively until 2014; rigorously enforced from CSE-2015 (2016 batch) onward.
    
- Impact: around 100 candidates with valid caste certificates had OBC claims rejected on creamy layer grounds since then.
    
- Petitioners' argument: this created "hostile discrimination" between children of government servants versus children of PSU/private-sector employees.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The March 2026 Supreme Court Judgment</mark>

|Aspect|Details|
|---|---|
|Bench|Division Bench: Justices P S Narasimha and R Mahadevan|
|Date|March 11, 2026|
|Core holding|Income cannot be sole criterion for creamy layer determination among OBCs|
|Reasoning|Excluding creamy layer should not create artificial distinctions between equally placed members of same social class|
|Key finding|Excluding PSU/private employees' children solely on salary income (without considering post nature: Group A, B, C, D) causes hostile discrimination|
|Constitutional basis|Violates equality doctrine under Articles 14, 15 and 16|
|Implementation timeline|Six months, deadline September 11, 2026|
|Retrospective effect|Could extend benefits to earlier examination cycles, potentially causing revised ranks|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The 'Rules of the Game' Doctrine</mark>

- A settled service law principle: selection criteria cannot be changed midway through a selection process.
    
- Established by Constitution Bench in Tej Prakash Pathak v. Rajasthan High Court (2024): criteria for selection cannot be altered mid-process or after selection has commenced.
    
- Rationale: protects candidates who entered a process under a specific rule framework; courts are reluctant to allow new criteria introduced after process begins.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Precedent: Divjot Sekhon v. State of Punjab (January 2026)</mark>

- Dispute: MBBS/BDS admissions under Punjab's sports quota.
    
- Facts: state widened sports quota eligibility criteria after prospectus issued and candidates had already applied.
    
- Court's ruling: "rules of the game cannot be altered once the game has begun" — applicable to both admissions and recruitment processes.
    
- Court's reasoning: admission process must be fully defined before commencement to prevent authorities stipulating norms later to suit interests or enable nepotism.
    
- Relief granted: court found revised criteria unlawful but declined to redraw the entire merit list.
    
- Reason for limited relief: redrawing would affect candidates not party to the case and would "unsettle settled matters."
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Centre's Current Plea</mark>

- Argument 1: March 11 judgment was delivered five days after CSE-2025 final results were declared; the exam "stood substantially concluded" prior to the judgment.
    
- Argument 2: CSE-2025 is at the "terminal stage of service allocation."
    
- Argument 3: Mechanical/unqualified retrospective application of the ruling to CSE-2025 would create "a peculiar and acute anomaly" — discrimination among candidates of the very cycle meant to benefit.
    
- Practical concerns cited: reopening category-wise merit lists would cascade into training schedules, batch strength, cadre allocation, seniority, and pay fixation.
    
- Relief sought: clarification that the March 11 judgment should not apply retrospectively to the "already-concluded" CSE-2025 process, so as not to unsettle completed admissions, academic sessions, degrees conferred, or settled student rights.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Linking the Precedent to the Present Case</mark>

- Centre's concerns mirror considerations that led the court in Divjot Sekhon to avoid redrawing the entire admission list.
    
- In Divjot Sekhon, the court moulded relief specifically to avoid disrupting students already admitted who were not parties to the case.
    
- Key open question before the Special Bench: whether a similar "moulded relief" approach will be applied to CSE-2025, preserving already-declared results while implementing the creamy layer ruling prospectively.
