The article discusses the Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court's July 23, 2026 judgment recognising the Tamirabharani river as a "deity" and thereby a legal person, examined against the backdrop of the Supreme Court's refusal to extend legal personhood to non-humans, and critiques the court's flawed handling of ash immersion under the guise of religious freedom.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Rights of Nature Movement</mark>

- Christopher Stone's 1972 essay "Should Trees Have Standing?" argued that granting rights to new entities is initially met with ridicule or fear.
    
- The Rights of Nature (RoN) movement gained global traction, but India lagged despite a cultural tradition of environmental reverence embedded in customs and stories.
    
- India's cultural ethos traditionally treats nature (rivers, trees) as sacred, creating an ironic gap between tradition and legal recognition.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Legal Precedents</mark>

- Mohammed Salim v. State of Uttarakhand (2014): Uttarakhand High Court granted "legal personality" to the Ganga and Yamuna rivers.
    
- Supreme Court (SLP (C) No.16879 of 2017, decided 07/07/2017): Stayed the Uttarakhand HC judgment, setting back the RoN movement in India.
    
- People's Charioteer Organization case (2023): Supreme Court definitively ruled that "legal personhood status" cannot be extended to non-humans.
    
- T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India & Ors (2012) 3 SCC 277: Supreme Court had earlier observed that environmental justice requires shifting from an anthropocentric to an eco-centric approach — a stance seemingly contradicted by its later 2023 ruling.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Tamirabharani Judgment (July 23, 2026)</mark>

- Delivered by the Madurai Bench of the Madras High Court while hearing a writ petition on the eviction of an individual.
    
- The court examined severe pollution of the Tamirabharani river caused by regular funeral/obsequial rites.
    
- Pollution statistics cited: approximately 90 tonnes of clothes, 2 tonnes of ash, and 3 tonnes of other waste collected from the river within three weeks.
    
- This was held to violate Section 24 of the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.
    
- The court balanced two competing rights: Article 25 (Fundamental Right to practise and propagate religion) versus the right to a healthy environment (read into Article 21).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">How the Court Circumvented the Supreme Court Ruling</mark>

- Since the Supreme Court barred legal personhood for non-humans directly, the Madras HC used the legal concept of "deity" as a workaround.
    
- By categorising the Tamirabharani as a deity, the court held it could be treated as a "legal person" — deities already have recognised legal personality in Indian law.
    
- This conferment of personhood was explicitly limited in purpose: solely to prevent pollution of the river, recognising its "intrinsic value."
    
- The court laid down strict measures to curb pollution.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Where the Court Erred: Ash Immersion</mark>

- The court treated ash immersion differently from other pollutants, permitting it under specific conditions — only in unfired earthen pots that dissolve immediately.
    
- This carve-out ignored that around 2 tonnes of ash were collected during the cleaning drive, indicating a real ecological burden.
    
- Cremated remains (cremains) pose a documented environmental threat: studies show moderate to high ecological risk in lotic systems (rivers/streams).
    
- In high-volume flowing rivers this may be less concerning, but in areas of mass cremation, continuous ash immersion overwhelms the river's self-purification capacity.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Comparative Practice: Sea Burials (US)</mark>

- The US permits "sea burials" or scattering of cremains under the Marine Protection, Research and Sanctuaries Act, 1972, provided disposal occurs at least three nautical miles from land.
    
- This permission is restricted strictly to human remains, excluding pet remains and non-biodegradable materials like plastic or metal flowers/wreaths.
    
- Marine systems are considered a better option than rivers for such disposal due to dilution capacity.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legal Definitions of Pollution: India vs US</mark>

|Aspect|United States|India|
|---|---|---|
|Pollutant definition|Section 502(6), Clean Water Act explicitly includes biological materials|Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 does not define "pollutant"|
|Air pollutant|—|Defined under Section 2(a), Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981|
|Pollution definition|—|Section 2(e) of Water Act includes "alteration of biological properties of water," which covers mass cremation impacts|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Author's Critique</mark>

- Placing reliance on religious ritual as inviolable under Article 25, while ignoring the "alteration of biological properties" language in Section 2(e) of the Water Act, was counterproductive.
    
- The court failed to invoke "health" as a reasonable restriction on Article 25(1); the author argues "health" should be read broadly to include both human and environmental health.
    
- Reading health restrictively (via Article 21) could have provided a valid basis to limit Article 25's application in an eco-centric manner.
    
- The court could alternatively have drawn on Indian traditions that treat nature as "Mother Earth," which impose a positive duty on humans not to pollute her, even in pursuit of religious practice.
