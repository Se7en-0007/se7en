## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Why Tribunals Exist</mark>

Tribunals serve a specific purpose in India's justice system - they allow domain specialists to resolve technical disputes faster than regular courts. However, a structural flaw has persisted historically: tribunals were administered by the same Ministries whose decisions they were meant to review, raising serious questions about independence and impartiality.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Judicial Precedents</mark>

|Case|Year|Key Ruling|
|---|---|---|
|S.P. Sampath Kumar|1987|Tribunal decisions remain subject to High Court review since judicial review is part of the basic structure|
|L. Chandra Kumar|1997|Reaffirmed that judicial review by High Courts over tribunal decisions cannot be excluded|
|Rojer Mathew|2019|Recommended an independent, statutory National Tribunals Commission (NTC) to oversee tribunal selection and administration; Justice Deepak Gupta held that defining qualifications for exercising judicial power is an essential legislative function that cannot be delegated to rulemaking|
|Madras Bar Association|2025|Struck down objectionable re-enacted provisions, restored the earlier framework, and gave the government four months to establish the NTC|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legislative Timeline of Executive Overreach</mark>

- Finance Act 2017 - Gave the executive greater control over tribunal appointments.
    
- Tribunals Reforms Ordinance 2021 - Reinstated conditions that the Supreme Court had earlier rejected.
    
- Madras Bar Association judgment (2025) - Forced the government's hand, mandating creation of the NTC within four months.
    
- Tribunal Reforms Bill 2026 - Passed by the Lok Sabha on August 10, 2026 (with no discussion) and by the Rajya Sabha on August 11, 2026.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Positive Features of the Bill</mark>

- Restores five-year terms for tribunal members.
    
- Introduces uniform service conditions across tribunals.
    
- Creates a National Tribunals Data Grid.
    
- Does not disturb already pending appointments.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Structural Weaknesses in the Bill</mark>

- Section 14 leaves qualifications, selection process, salaries, allowances, and other service conditions to future executive rules - contrary to Justice Deepak Gupta's reasoning in Rojer Mathew that such matters are essential legislative functions, not delegable to rulemaking.
    
- Section 3 has similarly vague language flagged by experts.
    
- Under Section 16, a Ministry first screens a complaint before it is passed to the NTC for inquiry - meaning executive gatekeeping persists even in the complaints process.
    
- The Centre continues to appoint NTC members, consulting the Chief Justice of India only for the chairperson and judicial members.
    
- The Centre retains substantial control over the NTC's finances and administration.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Criticism</mark>

The central problem: a commission designed specifically to insulate tribunals from executive control cannot itself remain subject to executive terms of appointment, removal, and funding. The government would need to relinquish its power to appoint or remove NTC members at will for the NTC to achieve genuine institutional autonomy.
