The article argues that the Indus Water Treaty (IWT), 1960, currently in abeyance, should be revised using the principle of "equitable and reasonable utilisation" (ERU) rather than its existing partition-based approach, drawing lessons from international treaties and environmental law obligations.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background Context</mark>

- IWT 1960 is currently in abeyance (suspended following the Pahalgam terror attack, with India closing gates like Salal Dam on the Chenab).
    
- Existing debates focus on two themes: need to revisit IWT due to climate change, and justification of India's suspension citing Pakistan's inimical behaviour despite Pakistan getting a major share of western river waters.
    
- Missing element in current discourse: advocacy for Equitable and Reasonable Utilisation (ERU) as the binding governing principle for shared waters.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What is ERU</mark>

- ERU accounts for all relevant circumstances beyond "who reached the river first" (prior appropriation doctrine).
    
- Climate change affects both quality and quantity of water, challenging beneficial use for upper riparian states too.
    
- Beneficial use = appreciable gain/benefit to the user, dependent on water quantity and quality available.
    
- ERU is enshrined in: Helsinki Rules (1966), UN Watercourses Convention (1997), Berlin Rules (2004).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">International Examples for Climate Integration</mark>

- Cooperative Framework Agreement on the Nile — Article 4(5): Nile Basin States must keep water utilisation under review considering substantial sectoral/circumstantial changes.
    
- Mekong River Commission's Procedures for Data and Information Exchange — creates formal framework for sharing water, climate, ecological data for basin-wide decisions.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Problem with Current IWT</mark>

- IWT structure: partition of rivers, not shared management based on ERU — leads to inequitable utilisation.
    
- Approach rooted in 1950s data/knowledge, fails core concerns of both sides:
    

|Country|Core Concern|
|---|---|
|Pakistan|Reliable water supply from western rivers (heavy agriculture dependence), also flood management|
|India|Hydropower development, water needs in Kashmir and beyond (river origin region)|

- Solution suggested: move beyond zero-sum game, codify ERU principles for mutual benefit and flexibility to adapt to climate-driven changes in water availability.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Models of Shared Management for Mutual Benefit</mark>

- **U.S.-Canada Water Treaty, 1961**: Equal sharing of downstream benefits; Canada received lumpsum USD 64.4 million for flood damages prevented in the U.S. over first 60 years, plus ongoing share of extra downstream power generation.
    
- **U.S.-Mexico Treaty, 1944 (Colorado River)**: Provides flexibility for annual hydrological variability (Article III); prohibits upper basin U.S. states from depleting Colorado River flow at Lee Ferry below 75 million acre-feet over any consecutive 10-year period.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Other International Legal Obligations</mark>

- **Ramsar Convention on Wetlands (1971)**: Both India and Pakistan are signatories; aims to conserve transboundary wetlands/migratory bird habitats (e.g., Rann of Kutch).
    
- **Convention on Biological Diversity (1992)**: Both are signatories; requires biodiversity conservation and sustainable use of shared wetland ecosystems, especially in Indus basin.
    
- Updating IWT could ensure "environmental water" maintenance for river/wetland health, supporting aquatic species including endangered ones.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Domestic Policy Signals</mark>

|Country|Policy Signal|
|---|---|
|India|Parliament Standing Committee on Water Resources highlighted institutional/legal gaps in transboundary water governance; noted absence of cohesive national framework for shared rivers|
|Pakistan|2018 National Water Policy recognised climate change as key stressor; flagged Indus basin vulnerability to glacial melt, erratic monsoons, droughts; called for stronger transboundary cooperation|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway</mark>

- Cooperation on transboundary basins is highly politicized and linked to national security concerns.
    
- However, historical experience shows willingness to cooperate emerges when net benefits of cooperation exceed net benefits of non-cooperation.
