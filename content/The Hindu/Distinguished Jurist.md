## What The Constitution States

- **Article 124** sets out how the **Supreme Court** is constituted.
- Under **Article 124(3)**, a person may be appointed a Supreme Court judge if they are an **Indian citizen** and have:
    
    - Served as a **High Court judge for five years**, or
    - Practised as an **advocate for ten years**, or
    - Been, in the **President’s opinion**, a **“distinguished jurist.”**
- The first two routes have always been used:
    
    - **Most judges** are elevated from the **High Courts**.
    - A **smaller number of lawyers** are appointed directly from the **Bar**.
    - The most recent direct appointment from the Bar was **Justice V Mohana**, recommended by the **collegium in May 2026** and sworn in in the **following month**.
- The third route - appointment of a **“distinguished jurist”** - has **never been used**, although the Constitution has completed **more than 76 years**.
- **Article 217** prescribes conditions for appointment of a **High Court judge**.
- The **42nd Amendment**, enacted during the **Emergency**, added a provision for appointing a **“distinguished jurist”** as a High Court judge. This provision was later repealed by the **44th Constitutional Amendment**.

## Diversify The Bench

- At the **13th convocation** for the **LL.M programmes** at **National Law University Delhi** on **August 30**, Justice **Ujjal Bhuyan** described Article 124(3) as an **“unused mandate”** of the Constitution that **“needs our serious attention.”**
- He stated that **no jurist has so far been appointed** to the Supreme Court despite the constitutional provision.
- A distinguished jurist on the Bench would:
    
    - **“Diversify the Bench.”**
    - Not remain **“constricted by narrow technicalities.”**
    - Be in a **stronger position to deal with public law issues**.
    - Make a **visible contribution** to the top-level decision-making process.
    - Enable participation of **legal academia and scholars**, with a **defining impact** on the judicial functioning of the Supreme Court.

## Where The Clause Came From

- The clause was introduced during the **Constituent Assembly debates on May 24, 1949**, after **H V Kamath** moved an amendment.
- Kamath argued that the candidate pool should not be **“necessarily confined to Judges or Advocates.”**
- He favoured bringing in **men and women possessing outstanding legal and juristic learning**, even if they had **never practised law**.
- He pointed to the **International Court of Justice at The Hague**, where judges are chosen on similar terms.
- **M Ananthasayanam Ayyangar** supported the proposal, arguing that a practising lawyer **“barely comes across constitutional problems”** of the kind routinely dealt with by the Supreme Court.
- He referred to the appointment of a **Harvard law professor** to the **US Supreme Court** by President **Franklin D Roosevelt**, calling it **“a novel experiment”** that had worked out well.
- **B R Ambedkar** did not object to the idea, but expressed hesitation about the term **“distinguished”** and considered whether **“eminent”** would be more appropriate.
- Ambedkar left the final decision to the **Drafting Committee**, and the amendment was eventually adopted.

## Who Qualifies And Why No One Has

- A **“distinguished jurist”** is generally understood as a person engaged in serious work in the **practice, teaching, or research of law**.
- **Courtroom experience** is not necessary to fit this description.
- This understanding faces a separate issue in India because **Bar Council rules generally prevent full-time law teachers from practising law**.
- Justice Ujjal Bhuyan offered two possible explanations for why the provision remains unused:
    
    - The **Central Government**, and later the **collegium**, may have considered that Indian academia lacked sufficient depth for elevation to the Supreme Court.
    - Both the **Central Government** and the **collegium** may not have seriously explored the provision.

## Collegium System

- Supreme Court appointments are now initiated through the **collegium system**.
- Therefore, a jurist must first be **recommended by the Supreme Court collegium** before the appointment is sent to the **government**.
- In a **2015** interview, **Upendra Baxi** recalled being called **“Judge Baxi”** by a professor who had been told by Justice **P N Bhagwati** that Baxi would be made a judge within a few months.
- The appointment, however, **never materialised**.
- Baxi remarked that the **Presidents of India** had looked **“with a telescope”** but had not found a jurist worth appointing as a Supreme Court judge, and described the matter as a **“dead issue.”**
