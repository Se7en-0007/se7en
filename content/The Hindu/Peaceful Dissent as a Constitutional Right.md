Peaceful dissent in a constitutional democracy is not a favour granted by the state but a fundamental right rooted directly in the Constitution, protecting citizens' ability to question, criticise, and seek accountability from those in power. When this right is suppressed through criminal law, prolonged detention, or arbitrary restrictions, the health of democracy itself is compromised.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Constitutional Principle</mark>

Democracy depends on the ability of citizens to disagree openly with the government without fear of arrest or punishment. Disagreement with the government is not the same as disloyalty to the nation - this distinction is central to constitutional governance. Reasonable restrictions on speech and assembly can exist, but they must be exceptional, proportionate, and legally justified rather than routine tools to silence criticism.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Constitutional Provisions</mark>

- Article 19(1)(a): Guarantees freedom of speech and expression.
    
- Article 19(1)(b): Guarantees the right to assemble peacefully without arms.
    
- Part III of the Constitution: Houses these fundamental freedoms, making the right to dissent inseparable from guaranteed liberties.
    
- Reasonable restrictions: Permitted under the Constitution but must remain narrow, proportionate, and not become instruments of routine suppression.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Justice Ujjal Bhuyan's Observations</mark>

Speaking at the National Law Institute University, Bhopal, Justice Bhuyan warned that India's democratic space is shrinking due to the growing criminalisation of peaceful dissent. He argued that liberty should be the rule and detention the exception, questioning why bail is increasingly denied to protesting students, activists, and journalists for extended periods, effectively making bail a pre-trial punishment.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Illustrative Incidents Cited</mark>

- Varanasi case: Eight Muslim men were detained for months for eating chicken while breaking their Ramzan fast on a boat in the Ganga, despite no law prohibiting the act.
    
- Mumbai case: A peaceful pro-Palestine demonstration was denied permission, showing that unpopular or politically inconvenient views should not justify curbing assembly.
    
- Bail conditions: Increasingly used to restrict free speech or public participation even before conviction.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Role of Courts</mark>

Courts function as constitutional guardians, not merely dispute-resolution forums, and must actively protect citizens against arbitrary state action. When peaceful protesters, students, journalists, or activists face prolonged incarceration without timely trial, it signals a deeper concern about the health of constitutional democracy rather than being a matter of isolated cases.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Case Study: Jantar Mantar Protests</mark>

Thousands of young citizens gathered peacefully at Delhi's Jantar Mantar to demand accountability over examination irregularities, an act rooted in constitutional values of dialogue and transparency. Reports of baton charges, pellet gun use, mass detentions, prolonged custody, and surveillance raised concerns about whether the right to peaceful assembly was adequately protected.

- Supreme Court intervention: Directed no coercive action against protesters, ordered release of detained minors, and called for an independent inquiry into alleged police excesses.
    
- Broader significance: The protest exemplified citizens asserting their right to question authority and demand accountability, irrespective of whether one agrees with every specific demand raised.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Central Takeaway</mark>

Criminal law is meant to address genuine crimes, not to function as a deterrent against lawful expression or peaceful civic participation. Strong governments respond to criticism with dialogue and evidence rather than arrests or restrictions, and suppressing dissent tends to erode public trust and weaken constitutional legitimacy rather than strengthen governance. The true test of a democracy lies not in how it treats those who agree with it, but in how it safeguards those who peacefully disagree.
