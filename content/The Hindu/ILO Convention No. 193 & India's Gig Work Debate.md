## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Core Event</mark>

On June 12, 2026, the 114th International Labour Conference in Geneva adopted Convention No. 193, "Decent Work in the Platform Economy" — the first binding international treaty specifically for platform/gig workers (riders, drivers, pickers, data-labellers). The vote tally was 406 in favour, 8 against, and 36 abstentions; nations voting in favour included China, Brazil, Germany, France, South Africa, and Japan.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Position at the Vote</mark>

India's government delegate abstained, while under the ILO's tripartite voting system (government, employers, workers), India's employer and worker delegates both voted in favour. This split shows the abstention was a deliberate government choice, not a technical or consensus gap — abstention is treated as a decision to keep distance from the treaty rather than genuine neutrality.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Convention No. 193 Actually Provides</mark>

- Universal floor of rights: applies regardless of how a company classifies workers, even if labelled "employees" or "independent partners"
    
- Core protections: statutory/negotiated minimum pay, on-time payment, occupational safety and health, and social security on par with comparable workers
    
- Algorithmic management rules: platforms must disclose significant automated decisions, explain them in writing, and keep a human in the loop (covers work allocation, pay setting, performance monitoring, account deactivation)
    
- Article 9 — correct classification: governments must classify workers based mainly on the facts of how work is actually performed, not on contractual labels

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Domestic Framework vs. the Global Standard</mark>

- Code on Social Security, 2020 (part of the four Labour Codes in force since November 2025) was among the first central laws globally to define "gig worker" and "platform worker"
    
- Requires aggregators to contribute 1%-2% of annual turnover (capped at 5% of worker payouts) to a social security fund
    
- Problem: neither the central law nor most State laws specify the nature, quantum, or eligibility of benefits — the contribution mechanism remains largely un-operationalised and schemes stay notional
    
- Real movement has come from States, not the Centre: Rajasthan's Platform-Based Gig Workers Act, 2023, and welfare boards drafted in Karnataka and Telangana

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India's Broader Pattern of ILO Abstention</mark>

India is a founding member of the ILO and has ratified six of the eight core conventions, but has notable gaps:

- Convention No. 87 (Freedom of Association) — not ratified, as it would grant government servants the right to strike, barred by domestic rules
    
- Convention No. 98 (Right to Organise and Collective Bargaining) — not ratified for the same reason
    
- Convention No. 190 (Violence and Harassment at Work) — not ratified

This reflects a consistent institutional posture of endorsing principles while withholding binding obligations, with India's official position being that it ratifies conventions only once domestic law is in full conformity. Labour being a Concurrent List subject is cited as a federalism-related justification, though States independently legislating on the issue undercuts this argument.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Consequences of Abstention</mark>

- Ratification + domestic incorporation would let a worker legally sue a platform for redress; abstention forecloses this route
    
- Signals to aggregators operating in India that the "employee vs. independent partner" classification fiction remains legally safe
    
- Creates a rights gap between platform workers in ratifying countries (e.g., China) and those in India (e.g., Chennai)
    
- Framed as an implicit choice favouring platforms over workers, since the government "held" neither outcome but effectively preserved the status quo benefiting aggregators
