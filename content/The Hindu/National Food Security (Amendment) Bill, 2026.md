## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background</mark>

India's food security debate has shifted from pure hunger-relief to nutrition-adequacy. Households unable to afford the ICMR-NIN recommended diet fell from about 52% in 2011-12 to about 25% in 2023-24 (25% rural, 21% urban). Despite this progress, poverty, hunger, and inability to afford a healthy diet are distinct issues that a modern policy must address separately. The draft Bill links Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) entitlements to household size, aiming to correct inequity without cutting existing foodgrain access.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Entitlement Structure</mark>

Two categories exist under the NFSA framework:

- Priority Households: 5 kg foodgrains per person per month
    
- AAY Households: flat 35 kg per household, regardless of size
    

This flat-rate design creates a per-capita disadvantage as household size grows — a 7-member AAY household gets only 5 kg/person, and an 8-member household gets about 4.4 kg/person, both below the Priority Household norm.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Proposed Reform</mark>

The draft proposes 7 kg per person, capped at 35 kg. Effects of this formula:

- Households with 1-4 members: entitlement reduced by 20% to 80%
    
- Households with 5+ members: entitlement unchanged
    
- No AAY household gains additional foodgrain under this formula
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Tamil Nadu Case Study</mark>

Tamil Nadu illustrates the scale of impact: 15.75 lakh of its 18.64 lakh AAY households (84.5%) have fewer than five members. The state projects the proposal would cut monthly AAY allocation from 65,261 tonnes to 42,040 tonnes — a fall of about 35.6%. Smaller households often include vulnerable individuals such as elderly persons living alone, widows, or persons with disabilities, making blanket cuts risky.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recommended Safeguards</mark>

Three safeguards are proposed to anchor the amendment:

1. No-loss guarantee: preserve the existing 35 kg monthly entitlement for every AAY household
    
2. Periodic review: reassess adequacy of the 35 kg ceiling for larger or high-dependency households using consumption, nutrition, and fiscal evidence
    
3. Separate financing: fund dietary diversification independently, without reducing existing cereal entitlements
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Coverage Gap Issue</mark>

NFSA allows coverage of up to 75% rural and 50% urban population, but its 81.35-crore beneficiary ceiling is still based on Census 2011. About 80 crore people currently receive free foodgrains, covering only 55.6% of the estimated 2025 population of 146.4 crore. This ceiling should be recalculated once Census 2027 data is available.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Nutrition and Health Indicators</mark>

|Indicator|2015-16 (NFHS-4)|2023-24 (NFHS-6)|
|---|---|---|
|Stunting (under-5)|35.5%|29.3%|
|Wasting (under-5)|19.3%|19.0%|
|Underweight (under-5)|32.1%|31.8%|

Only about 15% of children aged 6-23 months receive a minimally adequate diet. Meanwhile, the ICMR-INDIAB study estimated 101 million Indians had diabetes and 136 million had prediabetes in 2021, reflecting India's "double burden" of malnutrition — undernutrition alongside rising non-communicable diseases (NCDs).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Dietary Evidence</mark>

A 2025 ICMR-INDIAB study of 18,090 adults found:

- Carbohydrates supplied 62.3% of daily energy nationally; protein only 12%
    
- Highest carbohydrate consumers had 30% higher odds of new type 2 diabetes than lowest consumers
    
- Switching refined cereals to whole-wheat or millet flour did not lower risk if carbohydrate share stayed high
    
- Modelling suggests greater benefit from replacing some carbohydrate calories with protein-rich foods
    

ICMR-NIN's 2024 guidelines recommend cereals and millets provide at most 45% of energy, with greater shares from pulses, beans, milk, nuts, vegetables, and fruits.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Policy Direction for Diversification</mark>

The Public Distribution System (PDS) cannot supply an entire healthy plate but can offer affordable, shelf-stable diversified foods. Suggested measures include:

- States supported to offer pulses, local rice, wheat, millet choices, and healthy edible oils where feasible
    
- Local production-consumption linkages via effective MSP procurement for pulses, millets, oilseeds
    
- Convergence between PDS, Anganwadi services, and PM POSHAN (school nutrition) programmes
    
- Eggs, milk, or locally acceptable alternatives added where feasible
    
- Additions tested via phased state pilots assessing consumption, anaemia, glycaemic risk, spending, and wastage before scale-up
    

The 2026-27 Union Budget allocates ₹2,27,629 crore to food subsidy, underscoring the need for transparent costing of any expansion.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Delivery Infrastructure and Digital Access</mark>

India has built a large-scale delivery network:

- By end-2025, 5.50 lakh of 5.51 lakh fair price shops used electronic point-of-sale devices
    
- One Nation One Ration Card covers nearly all NFSA beneficiaries
    
- By June 2026, over 1.86 lakh Ayushman Arogya Mandirs existed, with 41.3 crore diabetes screenings recorded
    

Safeguards needed include reliable offline alternatives, assisted/doorstep access for low-mobility persons, and a guarantee that authentication failure will not cause denial of entitled foodgrains.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Fair Price Shops as Nutrition Touchpoints</mark>

Fair price shops could double as last-mile nutrition information hubs through multilingual receipts, displays, voice, and mobile messages promoting dietary diversity and healthier choices. Trained health workers could conduct periodic outreach linking willing adults to NCD services, but dealers should only facilitate referral  (not diagnose or counsel) and participation must remain voluntary, confidential, and unrelated to ration eligibility.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Takeaway for The Reform</mark>

India's food security reform must simultaneously protect against hunger and address dietary drivers of diabetes and NCDs. Success should be measured not just by tonnage of grain distributed, but by whether vulnerable families can eat enough, eat healthier, and access entitlements with dignity.
