## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context</mark>

- Nepal was already recovering from a devastating flood that killed hundreds when a fresh flood threat emerged.
- A "significant barrier lake" formed on the Tibetan side of the Nepal-Tibet border, and it has now breached its banks.
- The lake formed after a likely glacial collapse (the trigger of Wednesday's Nepal disaster) caused ice-and-rock debris to block the confluence of the Chhochen and Purepu Tsangpo rivers.
- Nepal Police (in a Nepali-language post) warned that the "water barrier/dam" on the Tibetan side had breached; both Nepal and China had earlier warned of this possibility.
- Pre-breach data: volume ~2 million cubic metres (as of Thursday morning per China Central Television); an additional 3 million cubic metres (~1,200 Olympic swimming pools) was expected to flow in over the next three days, raising breach risk.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Is a Barrier Lake?</mark>

- Also called a landslide dam or landslide lake; Chinese authorities term it a "barrier lake."
- Definition (US Geological Survey): forms in a river valley when landslide debris completely blocks a stream or river.
- Common triggers: earthquakes and excessive rainfall.
- These are temporary lakes with no outlet; continued water accumulation can cause overflow or breach.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Terminology</mark>

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|LLOF|Landslide Lake Outburst Flood — disaster caused when a landslide dam breaches|
|GLOF|Glacial Lake Outburst Flood — sudden release of water dammed by a glacier or moraine|
|Barrier lake/landslide dam|Lake formed when landslide debris blocks a river valley|

- Note: A GLOF was initially suspected as the cause of the Nepal flooding, but the more likely cause now is a glacier break-off, which subsequently created the landslide (barrier) lake.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Timescale of Dam Failure</mark>

- Breach timing is highly unpredictable — ranges from days to decades after dam formation.
- Fastest recorded worst-case LLOF breach: 10 days (1786 Sichuan event, detailed below).
- Slowest recorded case: a dam on the Tagermarch River, Kyrgyzstan, took 131 years to fail (1835–1966).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Geographic/Geological Context</mark>

- A 2017 study in Current Science notes landslide dams commonly form where there are high, steep mountains and narrow river valleys prone to rock, soil, and mud avalanches/slides/flows — a description matching the Himalayas.
- A National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) document states LLOFs are common across Himalayan river basins.
- Mitigation approach: creating controlled outlets/drainage channels in the dam (an approach China is currently exploring).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why a Breach Is Devastating</mark>

- A dam failure can release millions of cubic metres of water in a short period, causing catastrophic downstream flooding.
- The flood carries debris and can trigger secondary landslides downstream, compounding damage.
- Nepal's situation is especially precarious since it has already suffered flooding that destroyed settlements, infrastructure, and lives.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Historical Case Studies of LLOFs</mark>

### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">1786 Dadu River, Sichuan, China (Worst Recorded LLOF)</mark>

- Triggered by an earthquake that caused a massive landslide, damming the Dadu River.
- Breached after 10 days.
- Flood travelled 1,400 km downstream.
- Killed approximately 100,000 people — the worst recorded LLOF in history.

### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">2000 Yigong Tsangpo River, Tibet</mark>

- A landslide created a 60-metre-high dam (Yigong landslide).
- The dammed lake collapsed two months later.
- Flood surged 500 km through Arunachal Pradesh into the Brahmaputra floodplain in Assam (cross-border impact) — per a 2020 study in Scientific Reports.
- Destroyed 20 bridges; left 50,000 people homeless.

### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">2000 and 2005 LLOFs, Himachal Pradesh</mark>

- Damaged infrastructure extensively along the Satluj and Spiti rivers (per a 2008 study in the journal Natural Hazards).
- At least 156 people killed in the 2000 event (toll possibly higher).
- Both were cross-border disasters — the landslide lakes originally formed in Tibet.

### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">2008 Wenchuan Earthquake, China</mark>

- Triggered landslides/rock avalanches producing 257 landslide dams, mostly along the eastern boundary of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau.
- The largest dam blocked the Tongkou River (a tributary of the Fujiang River) at Tangjiashan.
- Threatened up to 1.3 million people downstream.
- Mitigation: authorities built a spillway to drain the lake in a controlled manner and evacuated residents.

### <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">August 2014 Sunkoshi River, Nepal</mark>

- Heavy precipitation triggered a huge landslide, forming a landslide lake across the Sunkoshi River.
- Lake was emptied using geo-engineering methods after 45 days.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Situation (as of Aug 28, 2026)</mark>

- Search and rescue operations near the Nepal-Tibet border were suspended due to risks from the barrier lake.
- Nepal Police issued an alert asking rescue personnel and the public to move to safe locations immediately.
- China mobilised an eight-member engineering team for aerial surveys and 3D modelling of the barrier lake.
- Engineer Chen Hanxiao (via CCTV) explained the mitigation approach: excavating a drainage channel at a lower point of the dam, with width/depth calculated based on inflow rate and lake-filling speed.
- Survey data: lake measured about 150 metres long, 40–50 metres deep, holding a reservoir of 1.5–2 million cubic metres of water.
- With the breach now occurring, Nepal is likely to face a second round of flooding.
