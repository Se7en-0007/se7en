## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Verdict</mark>

A nine-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court delivered a fractured verdict (five separate opinions) on what constitutes an "industry" under Indian labour law. Despite differing interpretations, all judges agreed that pending cases under the repealed Industrial Disputes Act, 1947 will continue to be decided using the 1978 "triple test," while the new Industrial Relations Code, 2020 must be interpreted independently on its own text.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of the Dispute</mark>

The dispute centered on Section 2(j) of the erstwhile Industrial Disputes Act, 1947, which defined "industry" in broad terms.

- Ambiguity led to decades of debate over whether hospitals, universities, charitable institutions, and government departments fell within the law's scope.
    
- This directly affected whether employees of such institutions were entitled to statutory labour protections.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The 1978 Triple Test (Bangalore Water Supply Case)</mark>

A seven-judge bench in Bangalore Water Supply and Sewerage Board vs A Rajappa (1978) established the governing precedent via Justice V R Krishna Iyer's leading opinion.

**Triple Test criteria** - an undertaking qualifies as an "industry" if it involves:

- Systematic activity
    
- Organised cooperation between employer and employee
    
- Production or distribution of goods/services calculated to satisfy human wants and wishes
    

Key features of the 1978 ruling:

- Absence of profit motive was held irrelevant.
    
- A "dominant nature" test was developed for establishments with multiple activities.
    
- A narrow exception was carved out for genuinely sovereign functions.
    
- The verdict itself was not unanimous - effective majority was 4:3 (two concurring opinions), with dissenting judges wanting an additional "commercial lines" requirement.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Post-1978 Legal Turbulence</mark>

|Event|Development|
|---|---|
|1982|Industrial Disputes (Amendment) Act sought to exclude hospitals, educational/research institutions, charitable bodies, and certain government activities - but was never notified, so never took legal effect|
|1996|Three-judge bench held Social Forestry Department could be an "industry"|
|Later|A two-judge bench took a narrower view regarding a forest department, creating inconsistency|
|2005|Five-judge bench in State of UP vs Jai Bir Singh expressed reservations about Bangalore Water Supply and referred the matter to a larger bench|
|2017|Seven-judge bench directed the matter be placed before the present nine-judge bench|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Validity of the Reference (6:3 Split)</mark>

Judges disagreed on whether the 2005 referral itself was even valid.

- CJI Surya Kant (with Justices Satish Chandra Sharma, Alok Aradhe, Vipul M Pancholi), plus Justices P S Narasimha and Joymalya Bagchi, upheld the validity of the reference.
    
- Justice B V Nagarathna noted the 2005 reference wrongly assumed a conflict existed between two smaller bench decisions on the forest department.
    
- Justice Dipankar Datta called it a "faltering reference," criticising the 2005 bench for wrongly treating separate concurring opinions in the 1978 judgment as evidence of non-unanimity. He held that settled precedent from a seven-judge bench, followed for decades, should only be revisited on exacting tests like manifest error or public mischief - a threshold the 2005 bench failed to meet.
    
- Net result: effectively a 6:3 split on whether the reference was valid at all.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Divergent Views on the Triple Test Itself</mark>

This is where the bench split most sharply.

- **CJI Kant's view**: The triple test needs "calibration" - an activity should show a discernible commercial character analogous to trade or business to qualify as an "industry." Profit motive stays irrelevant, but purely charitable initiatives and core sovereign functions would be excluded. However, he did not make this the governing test for pending cases.
    
- **Justices Nagarathna, Datta, and Bagchi**: Defended the 1978 approach, stressing labour law's social-welfare purpose. They argued a commercial requirement could strip workers in hospitals/schools of protection simply because their employer isn't profit-driven. Justice Bagchi called the triple test one of "rare felicity in judicial interpretation"; Justice Nagarathna stressed its importance amid privatisation.
    
- **Justice Narasimha's view**: Considered the entire debate over modifying the triple test "unnecessary" since the Industrial Disputes Act itself has been repealed.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Common Ground: Treatment of Pending Cases</mark>

Despite deep disagreement on doctrine, all opinions converged on the practical outcome for pending litigation.

- The Court declined to retrospectively apply any newly formulated, narrower test to old cases.
    
- Pending proceedings under Section 2(j) of the old Act will continue to be decided per the 1978 Bangalore Water Supply test.
    
- All opinions clarified the judgment has no bearing on interpreting the new Industrial Relations Code.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The New Industrial Relations Code, 2020</mark>

The Code takes a materially different, more restrictive approach to defining "industry."

- Defines "industry" as any systematic activity involving cooperation between employer and workers to produce/supply goods and services, regardless of capital investment or profit motive.
    
- Unlike the old Act, it explicitly excludes: charitable and philanthropic institutions, domestic services, and sovereign government functions.
    
- The Supreme Court held that the Code's provisions must be interpreted independently of this judgment and of Bangalore Water Supply.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Sovereign Functions and Government Welfare Activities</mark>

The judgment also clarified the scope of the "sovereign function" exception.

- Broad principle: an activity does not become "sovereign" merely because it is performed by the State or connected to a welfare objective.
    
- The exception is confined strictly to core, inalienable governmental functions.
    
- This approach is consistent with the original 1978 Bangalore Water Supply reasoning.
