## Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand Policies as Model

- The **recently notified treated wastewater reuse policies for Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand** offer an instructive example of **contextualised policymaking**.
- Developed through an **extensive consultative process**, these policies do **not apply a one-size-fits-all template**.
- They account for the **stark geographical diversity within each State**, and the **distinct needs of hill communities versus densely populated plains**.
- They embed key principles:
    
    - **Fit-for-purpose reuse approaches**
    - **Integration with urban planning and river rejuvenation**
    - **Community participation**
    - **Blended finance and public-private partnership pathways**
    - **Digital monitoring systems** that make reuse **accountable**

## The Recurring Water Crisis

- Such examples remain the **exception rather than the rule**.
- **Every summer, Indian States make headlines grappling with water scarcity**, yet the story never changes.
- Recurring issues include **rampant groundwater extraction** and the **relentless tug-of-war between agriculture, industry, and urban demand**.
- The real question is **not whether India needs to rewrite this narrative, but how much longer it can afford not to**.

## Hidden Opportunity

- **Treated wastewater is an untapped resource** waiting to be mainstreamed.
- For **agriculture, industry, urban landscaping, and ecological restoration**, it offers a **reliable, drought-proof supply**.
- **India's water future may hinge less on discovering new freshwater reserves and more on the intelligence with which the country recycles what it already has**.
- The **Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation** has done **commendable work in scaling sewage treatment infrastructure**, but **pipes and plants alone do not close the loop**.
- **A treatment facility that discharges into a drain rather than feeding a farm or a factory is infrastructure without purpose**.

### What India Needs

An ecosystem that converts capacity into practice, comprising:

- **Institutional coordination across departments**
- **Pricing signals that make reuse economically rational**
- **Quality standards that inspire confidence in end-users**
- **City-level roadmaps that translate national ambition into local action**

### National Framework on Safe Reuse of Treated Water (SRTW), 2022

- Offers the **canvas India needs to navigate this transition**.
- Represents a **policy signal that reuse is no longer peripheral - it is a national priority**.
- **Mandates States to develop their own reuse policies**, acknowledging that **water solutions must ultimately be local, even when the vision is national**.
- **Several States have already responded**, putting in place policies that **set reuse targets** and chart a path toward **measurable milestones**.

## Treated Wastewater as Economic Opportunity

- Treated wastewater, **reframed correctly, is not a waste management problem; it is an economic opportunity**.
- Its case rests on **hard, computable value**, including:
    
    - **Building climate resilience**
    - **Drought-proofing cities**
    - **Reducing costly dependence on freshwater**
    - **Enabling industrial and urban growth without straining existing supplies**
    - **Supporting low-carbon urban development at scale**
- Until this **economic case is made clearly and credibly to State governments and planners**, the **policy will remain an aspiration**.
- **Reuse succeeds only when utilities, urban departments, irrigation agencies, and industries stop working in silos and start working in an integrated manner**.
- The **financial momentum needed to hit reuse targets is rarely found in new budgetary allocations alone**; it is **unlocked by identifying synergies with existing schemes and convergence opportunities across programmes that are already funded and moving**.

## Need for Awareness

- The **public** is a constituency that **cannot be overlooked**.
- **Misconceptions about the safety of treated water run deep**.
- Both the **National SRTW Framework** and the **reuse policies for Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand** have chosen to call treated water **"Apna Jal" (our water)**.
- This naming signals that **the shift India needs is not just infrastructural but psychological**.
- **Experts working with State governments are witnessing a quiet shift in thinking** - that the **safe reuse of treated water is not just a policy consideration but a strategic imperative**.
- Yet, **several States remain on the sidelines**.
- **States that have not yet adopted a clear plan for water security through reuse are not just falling behind, they are running out of time to catch up**.
