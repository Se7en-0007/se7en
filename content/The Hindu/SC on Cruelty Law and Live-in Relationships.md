## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Ruling</mark>

The Supreme Court has expanded the protection against domestic cruelty under criminal law to cover women in live-in relationships, provided the couple shares an "intent to marry." This judgment was delivered on Monday (August 2026) by a bench of Justices Sanjay Karol and N Kotiswar Singh. Since Section 498A of the erstwhile IPC dealt with cruelty by a "husband," and the IPC has now been replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), the ruling will likely apply to the corresponding provision — Section 85 of the BNS.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background of The Case</mark>

- Section 498A of the IPC penalised a husband or his relatives for subjecting a woman to cruelty; its wording restricted application to legally valid marriages.
    
- Over time, the SC extended this to men who induced a woman into a void or voidable marriage (e.g., by concealing a prior subsisting marriage).
    
- In the present case, the accused had already been married to someone else and argued his relationship with the complainant was not a valid marriage, so Section 498A should not apply.
    
- The Karnataka High Court rejected this, holding that a man who induces a woman to believe she is lawfully married to him cannot escape criminal responsibility just because no valid marriage existed in law.
    
- The accused then appealed to the Supreme Court.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why The Court Went Beyond Void Marriages</mark>

- Instead of deciding only on the void-marriage precedent, the bench addressed the wider question of whether a man in a live-in relationship can be prosecuted under Section 498A at all.
    
- The judgment, authored by Justice Karol, used a "purposive interpretation," reasoning that the law's original objective was to deter domestic cruelty and must adapt to social realities.
    
- The Court observed that cruelty "does not check at the door," regardless of whether the house belongs to a married or unmarried woman.
    
- Denying protection to cohabiting women while granting it to married women was held to have "no rational nexus" with the law's purpose, making such a distinction offensive to Article 14 (Right to Equality).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why the Domestic Violence Act Was Not Enough</mark>

|Aspect|Section 498A (Criminal law)|DV Act, 2005 (Civil law)|
|---|---|---|
|Nature|Criminal provision|Primarily civil legislation|
|Consequences|Backed by criminal deterrence|Almost singularly civil and monetary|
|Remedies|Prosecution/punishment|Maintenance, protection orders|
|Coverage|Traditionally "husband" only, now extended|Already covers relationships "in the nature of marriage"|

- The petitioner and the Union government had argued that unmarried women are already protected under the DV Act since it explicitly covers those in a "relationship in the nature of marriage."
    
- The Court rejected this, calling it a "misstatement of law" to equate civil and criminal remedies, since their consequences and purposes differ fundamentally.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Intent to Marry" Test</mark>

- The Court borrowed the concept of "relationship in the nature of marriage" from the DV Act, describing it as a narrower subset of a general live-in relationship.
    
- Drawing on a 2013 precedent, it reiterated the features of such a relationship: pooling financial resources, sharing domestic arrangements, having a sexual relationship, and holding themselves out to society as spouses.
    
- The new judgment adds a further requirement: the couple must also have an "intent to marry" for Section 498A protection to apply.
    
- Reasoning: many couples may satisfy all criteria of a "relationship in the nature of marriage" yet never intend to marry; without this intent, the relationship should not be equated with marriage for penal purposes given the serious consequences involved.
    
- The Court held that only relationships that are both "in the nature of marriage" and carry "intent to marry" are close enough to marriage to warrant Section 498A protection.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Burden of Proof</mark>

- The initial burden to establish "intent to marry" lies on the female partner seeking protection under the law.
    
- The judgment does not lay down specific parameters for how this internal "intent" is to be legally proven or recognised, leaving this to case-by-case judicial determination.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Terms</mark>

- Section 498A (IPC) / Section 85 (BNS): Provision penalising cruelty by a husband or his relatives toward a wife.
    
- Relationship in the nature of marriage: A legal concept from the DV Act describing cohabiting couples who function as spouses.
    
- Article 14: Constitutional guarantee of equality before law, used here to strike down the married/unmarried distinction.
    
- Purposive interpretation: A method of interpreting law based on its underlying objective rather than literal wording.
