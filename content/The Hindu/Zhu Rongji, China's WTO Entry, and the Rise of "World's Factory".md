## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context of the News</mark>

- China's former Premier Zhu Rongji died on August 12, 2026, prompting global tributes.
    
- He is best remembered as a "tough taskmaster" and the key architect of China's entry into the World Trade Organisation (WTO) in 2001.
    
- The New York Times noted he contributed more to liberalising and internationalising the Chinese economy than any recent leader except Deng Xiaoping.
    
- Two milestones frame China's economic rise: 1978 (Deng's liberalisation) and 2001 (WTO accession under Zhu).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: Why China Sought WTO Membership</mark>

- WTO membership acted as a "buffer" for the Chinese economy during a period of major structural change.
    
- Zhu, serving under President Jiang Zemin, supported dismantling many State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs) as part of an efficiency drive - this caused significant job losses domestically.
    
- China's accession was the culmination of a long-standing battle among Chinese elites over economic direction: Deng Xiaoping favoured opening up, while conservative leaders viewed it as "surrender to capitalism."
    
- The push came soon after the 1989 Tiananmen Square protests, which were partly fuelled by economic distress following liberalisation; reformist leaders ultimately prevailed.
    
- WTO accession was seen as the "last frontier" of China's economic opening - joining a Western liberal economic institution.
    
- According to author Martin Jacques (When China Rules the World, 2009), China's goal was never Westernisation but restoring the Communist Party's legitimacy after Mao through economic growth.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why the US and West Supported China's Entry</mark>

- Western nations expected commercial benefits: access to low-cost labour and inputs from China.
    
- There was a political expectation that WTO integration would push China toward market-oriented institutions and rules.
    
- Washington believed accession would create a large Chinese middle class, which would eventually demand political rights and reform - an assumption that has been widely debated since.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The Accession Process</mark>

- WTO negotiations took 15 years to conclude, reflecting deep mutual suspicion between China and the US.
    
- China's entry terms were based on the "most detailed agreement ever made with any country" - far more stringent than terms given to India a few years earlier.
    
- Key structural changes triggered by accession include:
    

|Change|Description|
|---|---|
|Import tariffs|Large cuts across sectors|
|Trading licenses|Rules loosened to allow domestic private and foreign competition|
|Market forces|Greater overall role in the economy|
|SOE reform|Idling/closure of many state-owned manufacturing enterprises|
|Capital and labour reallocation|Shift from smaller, less productive SOEs to private manufacturing, raising productivity|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Economic Transformation Post-WTO</mark>

- China's share of trade in GDP rose significantly after 2001.
    
- Exports of goods and services grew sharply, reaching approximately $3.11 trillion in 2025 (World Bank data).
    
- China leveraged its enormous labour force and domestic infrastructure to build a comprehensive manufacturing ecosystem - earning the title "world's factory."
    
- Structural imbalances associated with China today - extremely high savings, high investment, and suppressed consumption - are described as direct products of the post-WTO economic model.
    
- Companies worldwide poured investment into China, while Chinese exporters gained deep access to major global markets, embedding China into global supply chains.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "China Shock" Phenomenon</mark>

- Coined in a influential 2016 paper by David H. Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon H. Hanson titled "The China Shock: Learning from Labor-Market Adjustment to Large Changes in Trade."
    
- Key argument: WTO-driven reallocation of capital and labour raised Chinese productivity but had uneven global costs.
    
- In the US, cheaper Chinese goods benefited consumers but caused concentrated job losses in manufacturing-heavy communities.
    
- This has fed into political concerns in the US (including from President Donald Trump) about deindustrialisation and trade imbalances.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Present-Day Fallout and Criticism</mark>

- Zhu himself, at a 1999 joint press conference with US President Bill Clinton, argued WTO competition would promote "rapid and healthy" development of China's economy - the "rapid" part is undisputed, but "healthy" remains debated.
    
- Key criticisms today include:
    
- Persistent trade imbalances with major economies, including the United States and India.
    
- Overproduction: China's high investment levels result in producing more than domestic consumption can absorb, due to subdued domestic demand.
    
- Structural issues compounding today: youth unemployment, a real estate crisis, and weak domestic demand - making Chinese export behaviour contentious for industries in the US, Europe, and elsewhere.
    
- China's ability to mass-produce and export cheaply has "exposed limits" in WTO rules, which were originally designed for more conventional market economies.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Assessment of Zhu Rongji's Legacy</mark>

- Zhu is described as a formidable figure who used WTO accession to force competition within China's economy, but he was not a full liberaliser in the Western sense.
    
- Analysts note that today's trade asymmetries are not simply a failure of Zhu's specific policies - they also reflect political and economic choices made by subsequent Chinese leadership in the decades following accession.
    
- His legacy is thus complex: he laid the structural foundation for China's manufacturing dominance, but the long-term consequences (imbalances, overcapacity, trade friction) continue to unfold today.
