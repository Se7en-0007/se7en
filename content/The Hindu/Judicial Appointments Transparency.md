The collegium system for appointing judges has grown less transparent over time, raising constitutional questions of accountability, equality, and institutional legitimacy — while the judiciary demands transparency from other organs of governance.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context / Trigger</mark>

- Justice Ujjal Bhuyan (SC) recently stated greater openness in the collegium process would boost public confidence and keep merit as the guiding principle.
    
- Debate reframed: earlier it was "executive vs judiciary" (1990s); now it is "opacity vs transparency" (this decade).
    
- Central argument: insulation from political interference should not become insulation from constitutional accountability.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Evolution of the Collegium System</mark>

The collegium is a judge-made construct, not a constitutional provision, built through three landmark cases.

|Case|Year|Key Shift|
|---|---|---|
|First Judges Case|1981|Executive given primacy in appointments|
|Second Judges Case (1993, 4 SCC 441)|1993|Primacy shifted to collective opinion of CJI + senior judges|
|Third Judges Case (1998, 7 SCC 739)|1998|Expanded into present five-member collegium|

- Even Fali Nariman, seen as an architect of the collegium, later criticised it as "not receptive at all" to the Bar.
    
- Nearly three decades on, there is: no notification of vacancies, no eligibility matrix, no known methodology for evaluating candidates.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Declining Disclosure — Timeline</mark>

- October 2017: Collegium began publishing resolutions with brief reasons for elevation — hailed as a transparency milestone.
    
- Practice steadily weakened over subsequent years.
    
- November 28, 2024: Resolutions stopped naming participating members or disclosing any reasoning.
    
- November 2025: CJI B.R. Gavai confirmed the collegium had formally decided to stop publishing detailed reasons, citing concern over harming candidates' career prospects.
    
- Contradiction highlighted: MediaOne judgment (2023) held sealed-cover secrecy is "antithetical to a transparent and accountable system" — yet the same standard is not applied to the collegium's own functioning.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Uncle Judges" Controversy</mark>

- Allegation: Opacity enables relatives of sitting/retired judges easier access to the Bench compared to first-generation lawyers.
    
- Former CJI R.M. Lodha remarked nearly one in three High Court judges could be an "uncle."
    
- 2018: Union government flagged 11 of 33 Allahabad HC collegium recommendations as relatives of sitting/retired judges.
    
- 2025 assessment: ~30% of Supreme Court judges (roughly 10 of 33) had familial links to former judges.
    
- January 2025: Collegium discussed barring judges' kith and kin from consideration; agreed in principle to higher scrutiny for relatives, but no formal legal bar was created.
    
- Significance: Absence of a public selection matrix means there is no objective way to separate meritorious relatives from beneficiaries of lineage.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Constitutional and Legal Angles</mark>

- Articles 14 and 16: Guarantee equality and equal opportunity in public employment.
    
- Secretary, State of Karnataka v. Umadevi (2006, 4 SCC 1): Public office appointments cannot use backdoor methods; must follow transparent, open procedures.
    
- Argument extended: If constitutional equality demands openness for recruiting clerks/engineers, why should the highest constitutional offices be exempt?
    
- Caveat acknowledged: Judicial appointments can't be reduced to a competitive exam — qualities like independence and temperament resist quantification; but qualitative judgment doesn't require total absence of procedure.
    
- Irony noted: CPIO v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019, 3 SCC 1) held the CJI's office falls under the RTI Act — yet this logic was never extended to the appointments process itself.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Comparative Practices — Other Democracies</mark>

|Country|Institution|Practice|
|---|---|---|
|United Kingdom|Judicial Appointments Commission|Publicly advertises vacancies, conducts structured interviews|
|South Africa|Judicial Service Commission|Publicly invites nominations, conducts televised interviews|

- Key takeaway: These systems aren't flawless, but rest on the premise that judicial independence is strengthened, not weakened, by public understanding of the selection process.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Theoretical & Social Context</mark>

- Jürgen Habermas: Democratic legitimacy rests on the "public sphere" — institutions must justify themselves before informed citizens.
    
- Real-world relevance: Every judicial appointment now faces rapid social media scrutiny.
    
- Example cited: A CJI's remark comparing youngsters to "cockroaches" spread from Jantar Mantar placards to trending hashtags within hours.
    
- Warning: Contempt jurisdiction (designed for newsrooms/courtrooms) cannot manage scrutiny of this scale and anonymity — signals a system still keeping appointments closed while resisting constructive criticism.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Suggested Reforms ("Reform, not Retreat")</mark>

- Does not require dismantling the collegium — only modernising it.
    
- Publish anticipated vacancies in advance.
    
- Invite applications from eligible advocates.
    
- Disclose objective eligibility criteria.
    
- Record reasoned recommendations explaining candidate preference.
    
- Confidential integrity-related inputs can remain protected — transparency needed only about process, criteria, and reasoning, not every file.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Constitutional Tension</mark>

- Not a judiciary vs. executive contest, nor an attempt to weaken judicial independence.
    
- Question: Whether one constitutional value (independence) is allowed to eclipse others (equality, fairness, reasoned decision-making).
    
- Conclusion of article: These values are complementary, not adversarial — independence without accountability breeds the very distrust the system was meant to prevent.
