The Supreme Court has ruled that hanging as a method of execution does not violate the right to dignity under Article 21, dismissing a PIL challenging the practice on scientific and constitutional grounds.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Case Overview</mark>

- Bench: Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta
    
- Date of judgment: Tuesday, August 18, 2026
    
- Nature of case: Public Interest Litigation (PIL) challenging constitutional validity of hanging as mode of execution
    
- Core question: Can a method of execution devised in colonial India still satisfy the Constitution's promise of dignity?
    
- Outcome: PIL dismissed; hanging upheld as constitutional
    
- Key observation by Court: Constitutional protection "does not cease to exist at the gallows"; it applies equally to prisoners on death row
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Two Hurdles Faced by the Challenge</mark>

- Precedent: The 1983 ruling in Deena vs UOI, where a three-judge bench had already upheld hanging as constitutional
    
- Legislative reaffirmation: Parliament's conscious decision to retain hanging while enacting the BNSS (Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita)
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">How Hanging Works (Technical Basis)</mark>

- Method relies on a calculated "drop" based on the convict's height and weight
    
- Intended effect: fracture-dislocation at the C2-C3 vertebrae (cervical spine)
    
- Goal: near-instant unconsciousness and death
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Grounds of Challenge by Petitioners</mark>

- Argued Article 21 includes a right to die through a "dignified procedure"
    
- Claimed hanging causes "intense physical torture and pain"
    
- Argued the method is inherently unpredictable: too short a drop causes death by strangulation; too long a drop can cause decapitation
    
- Relied on scientific evidence and evolving constitutional doctrine since 1983
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scientific Evidence Cited</mark>

- A 1992 study examined skeletal remains of 34 people hanged in England (1882–1945)
    
- Findings: Fractures at C2-C3 were common, but the "hangman's fracture" (assumed to cause near-instant death) occurred in only 3 of 34 cases
    
- 6 of the 34 died of asphyxiation instead of instant unconsciousness
    
- Petitioners argued this proves hanging is neither quick nor controllable, making outcomes unpredictable and the pre-execution wait psychologically traumatic
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Government's (Union's) Counter-Arguments</mark>

- Only 8 executions have taken place in India since 2003
    
- No botched execution has ever been officially recorded in India
    
- Contrasted this with the United States, where lethal injection has a well-documented history of failures
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Legal Framework</mark>

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Constitutional Basis</mark>

- Right to die with dignity flows from Article 21 (Right to Life)
    
- Landmark precedent: Gian Kaur v State of Punjab (1996) - held that right to life includes the right to live and consequently to die with dignity
    

## <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Statutory Provision</mark>

- Section 393(5) of the BNSS: mandates that a person sentenced to death "shall be hanged by the neck till he is dead"
    
- Historical continuity: This clause originated in the Code of Criminal Procedure (CrPC) of 1861 and has survived multiple amendments, now carried forward into the BNSS

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Reasoning Behind the Supreme Court's Verdict</mark>

- Found no compelling reason to revisit or overturn the Deena vs UOI precedent
    
- Scientific material presented by petitioners was held insufficient to displace the basis on which Deena was originally decided
    
- Petitioners failed to establish that alternative methods (lethal injection, electrocution, lethal gas, shooting) offer any clear advantage over hanging
    
- Reaffirmed Deena's reasoning: the long-drop method causes cervical fracture leading to near-immediate unconsciousness
    
- Court held the existing method satisfies the State's obligation to execute death sentences with "decency and decorum," avoiding "degradation or brutality"
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Important Nuance: Door Left Open</mark>

- The Court did not treat the matter as permanently settled
    
- It acknowledged that constitutional law evolves over time
    
- Future challenges may succeed if new scientific, medical, or empirical evidence fundamentally undermines the assumptions behind Deena
    
- The Union government retains the option to examine and adopt alternative execution methods if it so chooses
