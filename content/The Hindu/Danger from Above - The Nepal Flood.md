## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Event Overview</mark>

- A flash flood struck Nepal's Himalayan region and foothills, marking a major Himalayan disaster.
- Death toll: at least 389, including several foreign tourists; around 300 Indians remained missing.
- Affected corridors: Bhote Koshi and Trishuli river valleys — settlements washed away, hydropower and monitoring infrastructure damaged.
- Infrastructure damage: 35 motorable bridges, 45 suspension bridges, and about 40 km of roads destroyed.
- Secondary flooding risk persists from debris blockages formed upstream.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Cause of the Disaster</mark>

- Initial suspicion: earthquake — not supported by subsequent evidence.
- Satellite imagery (analysed by global agencies including ISRO) suggests a glacier collapse or ice-rock avalanche in Tibet.
- Probable mechanism: collapse led to a temporary river blockage, which then failed suddenly, releasing a massive flood wave.
- Context: monsoon season with a powerful Western Disturbance active over north India and the Himalayas — extratropical storms known to cause clouds colliding with mountains and sudden heavy rainfall — making a rainfall-triggered flood a plausible but ultimately less-supported explanation than the glacier-collapse theory.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Concept: GLOF (Glacial Lake Outburst Flood)</mark>

- Definition: sudden release of a large volume of water, debris, and rock when a glacial lake or ice dam fails.
- Precedent case: October 2023 South Lhonak Lake disaster in Sikkim — a Himalayan glacial lake gave way, releasing millions of tonnes of water and debris, destroying Sikkim's largest hydropower project.
- Significance: the Sikkim GLOF was found to have been "years in the making," showing these disasters build up slowly before sudden failure.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Scale of Himalayan Glacial Risk in India</mark>

- India's Himalayan region has nearly 7,500 glacial lakes and about 15,000 glaciers.
- Some lakes/glaciers are monitored via remote sensing (satellites).
- Limitation: full condition assessment requires on-site expeditions, which are difficult due to inhospitable terrain and a narrow feasible window (only July to September).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Challenges in Forecasting and Response</mark>

- Even with satellite monitoring, accurately predicting a glacier collapse remains extremely difficult.
- Risk of false alarms: premature warnings can be counterproductive, potentially triggering panic without residents actually relocating (due to habit or lack of alternatives).
- Vulnerable infrastructure: dams and hydropower projects in such terrain are typically the first casualties of these outbursts.
- Trend: such natural outbursts are increasing in frequency amid global warming.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Policy and Governance Recommendations</mark>

- Beyond immediate relief and rescue operations, governments should:
- Enforce strict construction codes in vulnerable Himalayan zones.
- Discourage settlements and large infrastructure projects in known high-risk areas.
- Invest in improved, continuous tracking of glacier health.
- Integrate monitoring data into long-term disaster planning and development programmes.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Editorial's Core Message</mark>

- The article frames such tragedies as products of passive neglect rather than any deliberate failure — implying that neglect (of monitoring, zoning, and construction regulation) is an addressable, correctable problem.
- Core takeaway: fragile Himalayan terrain combined with climate change is amplifying the frequency and unpredictability of natural outbursts, demanding proactive, science-based disaster governance rather than reactive relief measures.
