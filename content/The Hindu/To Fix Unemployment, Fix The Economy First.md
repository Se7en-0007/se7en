## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Argument</mark>

The article, by Arun Maira (author of "Reimagining India's Economy"), argues that India's unemployment crisis cannot be solved by fixing education alone - the deeper structural problems of the economy itself must be reformed. It contends that "ease of doing business" reforms must now be made subordinate to "ease of living and earning" for ordinary citizens.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context: Why This Matters Now</mark>

- The piece is triggered by the "Cockroach Janta Party's" movement, which has spotlighted the political urgency of employment reform for India's youth.
    
- India and China are the world's only two countries with populations exceeding one billion, making meaningful employment their biggest shared economic challenge.
    
- Comparative performance gap: China's per capita income has grown roughly eight times faster than India's over the last 30 years.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Three Conflicting Trends in India's Policy Approach</mark>

- Ease of doing business reforms — diluting workers' right to organise and weakening environmental protections.
    
- Widespread AI adoption — promoted to boost "productivity" (more output, less human input) across agriculture, industry, health, and education.
    
- Employment elasticity concerns — recognition of the need to raise employment elasticity (jobs created per unit of GDP growth), where India has lagged since the 1990s liberalisation reforms.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Concept: Employment Elasticity</mark>

Employment elasticity measures the amount of meaningful employment generated per unit of GDP growth. Given India has the world's largest youth population, its growth model should ideally have the highest employment elasticity — but rising AI adoption threatens to worsen this instead.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The China Model — AI, Jobs, and State Response</mark>

|Aspect|Detail|

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">China's Governing Philosophy</mark>

China's leadership follows a "people-first" ideology, prioritising the needs of ordinary citizens and social stability over pure market deregulation demands — particularly regarding labour, food, education, health, and housing. Despite being authoritarian, the state remains highly attentive to online public sentiment, driven by historical fear that discontent among educated youth, workers, and peasants could trigger unrest.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recommendations for India</mark>

- Rapidly raise income levels across agriculture, manufacturing, and services.
    
- Government's "Viksit Bharat by 2047" vision now includes "ease of living" alongside "ease of doing business" — but needs stronger enforcement.
    
- Employers should be required to train workers rather than freely laying them off.
    
- Labour codes (India's four labour codes) currently favour ease of doing business; this must be rebalanced toward ease of living/earning.
    
- Policy should prioritise workers' rights over capital owners' rights, following China's example.
    
- Voices of workers and small entrepreneurs need greater weight relative to financial investors.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Prescription</mark>

Maira's central thesis: India's leaders should learn from China's proactive, worker-protective response to AI-driven disruption, ensuring that technological productivity gains do not come at the cost of employment and livelihoods — especially for India's large youth cohort.
