## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Recent Trigger Event (August 2026)</mark>

Heavy rain on August 1, 2026 caused fresh landslides, mudslides and flooding across Kerala, killing at least six people, coming two years after the devastating 2024 Wayanad landslides. IMD issued a red alert for nine districts (Idukki, Kottayam, Pathanamthitta, Thrissur, Malappuram, Kozhikode, Wayanad, Kannur and Kasaragod) warning of extremely heavy rainfall, thunderstorms and gusty winds (40–50 kmph expected on Sunday).

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Meteorological Causes</mark>

- Offshore trough extending from south Gujarat coast to Kerala
    
- Strong southwesterly winds across the Arabian Sea
    
- Strong north-south pressure gradient along the west coast
    
- Combined effect: strengthened monsoon flow and widespread rainfall (not localized)
    
- Of 109 IMD stations, 53 recorded more than 7 cm rainfall; some crossed the "extremely heavy" threshold (over 20.4 cm in 24 hours)
    
- Landslides resulted from cumulative saturation from preceding rainfall plus a fresh intense burst, not a single rain event
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">What Causes a Landslide (General Concept)</mark>

A landslide is the sudden downward movement of rock, boulders, earth or debris along a slope. Landslides occur due to two categories of factors:

- Conditioning factors: soil type, topography, rock structure, geomorphology, and slope angle — these determine baseline vulnerability of a region
    
- Triggering factors: intense rainfall and human activities such as haphazard land use, unscientific construction, and deforestation
    
- Other natural triggers (besides rain): earthquakes, snow-melting, undercutting of slopes due to flooding
    
- Other anthropogenic triggers: excavation, hill/tree cutting, excessive infrastructure development, overgrazing
    

Most landslides in India specifically are rain-induced events.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Why Kerala's Western Ghats Are Vulnerable</mark>

Landslide risk in Kerala is concentrated along the Western Ghats, particularly in central and northern districts (Idukki, Pathanamthitta, Kottayam, Wayanad, Kozhikode and Malappuram) due to steep slopes, geology and changing land use.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Geological Mechanism</mark>

- The terrain has two distinct layers: loose soil sitting atop hard bedrock
    
- During intense/prolonged rain, water infiltrates the soil and reaches the soil-rock interface
    
- This weakens the bond between soil and rock, increasing the likelihood of slope failure (explained by KS Sajinkumar, Professor of Geology, University of Kerala, post-2024 Wayanad landslides)
    
- Preceding rainfall progressively saturates slopes; a subsequent heavy burst then adds large volumes of water quickly, increasing runoff and failure risk
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Landslide Atlas Data (ISRO-NRSC, 2023)</mark>

- Based on landslide events recorded 1998–2022
    
- Landslides occur primarily along the Himalayas and the Western Ghats
    
- Western Ghats recorded fewer landslide events than the Himalayan region overall
    
- However, Kerala accounted for a disproportionately high share of landslide fatalities
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Role of Human Intervention</mark>

Land-use changes have significantly increased slope fragility in the region.

- Quarrying, road construction, and plantation expansion alter slope stability and natural drainage
    
- Most landslides occur around plantation areas — a key triggering factor identified by S Abhilash (Director, Advanced Centre for Atmospheric Radar Research, CUSAT)
    
- Monocropping practice: large native trees (which anchor topsoil to bedrock with deep roots) are cut and replaced with plantation crops like tea and coffee, which have shallow root systems and provide weaker soil-binding
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Policy Debate: Gadgil vs Kasturirangan Committees</mark>

|Aspect|Gadgil Panel (2011)|Kasturirangan Committee|
|---|---|---|
|Chair|Ecologist Madhav Gadgil|Space scientist K Kasturirangan|
|Context|Western Ghats Ecology Expert Panel|Constituted after states objected to Gadgil report|
|Ecologically Sensitive Area (ESA)|Larger ESA proposed|Smaller ESA proposed|
|Regulatory stance|Strict regulation of mining, quarrying, polluting industries, large construction in sensitive zones|Less stringent regulatory framework|
|Outcome|Rejected by several states|Formed basis of subsequent, more limited protections|

This unresolved tension between development and ecological protection in the Western Ghats has been repeatedly revived after successive landslide disasters in Kerala, including the 2024 Wayanad tragedy and the current 2026 events.
