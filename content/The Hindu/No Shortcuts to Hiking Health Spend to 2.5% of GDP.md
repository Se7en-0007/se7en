## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Argument</mark>

- The Standing Committee's benchmarking of hospital rooms against three-star hotels is conceptually flawed since healthcare is a constitutional right (Article 21) with no real "choice" for patients, unlike hotel guests.
    
- The 176th Standing Committee report will have limited practical impact since its recommendations are non-binding on government.
    
- The real issue is the failure to raise public health spending to 2.5% of GDP, a target set in the 2017 National Health Policy but still unmet.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Numbers to Remember</mark>

- Current government health spend: around 1.4% of GDP.
    
- Target set by National Health Policy 2017: 2.5% of GDP.
    
- India's total health spend (public + private): 3.2-3.4% of GDP.
    
- Land acquisition cost cited in the report: ₹50 crore to ₹250 crore for five acres in Tier-I/Tier-II cities (NCR region) for institutional healthcare land.
    
- PMJAY coverage limit: ₹5 lakh per family (additional ₹5 lakh for senior citizens above 70 years).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Private Sector Dependence and Its Consequences</mark>

- Since private spending exceeds public spending on health, private players are structurally incentivized to maximize profits.
    
- Gaps in public investment are increasingly filled by private equity funds seeking high returns, which the author says comes at "grave costs to patients."
    
- The Clinical Establishments (CE) Act, 2010 is described as a weak regulatory tool; the author suggests government could regulate more effectively as a "bulk buyer" rather than a top-down regulatory authority.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Supreme Court Battle</mark>

- Petitions both for and against greater private-sector regulation are pending before the Supreme Court.
    
- The government defended the CE Act rules (framed 2012) but invoked "health is a State subject" to avoid direct implementation responsibility.
    
- States that adopted the CE Act are willing to enforce rate display but have raised objections about determining a "range of rates."
    
- This position emerged from five State/UT-level consultations held between March 2024 and March 2026, conducted per Supreme Court directions.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Historical Context of the CE Act</mark>

- The CE Act, 2010's statement of objects and reasons flagged the private healthcare sector as "largely unregulated and uncontrolled," citing inadequate treatment, excessive use of technology, wasted resources, and malpractice.
    
- The Standing Committee report is seen as reiterating these same longstanding concerns rather than offering new solutions.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Influx of Private Equity - The Deeper Problem</mark>

- Foreign funding influx means clinical decisions are increasingly made in corporate boardrooms (cited examples: London, New York) rather than by treating doctors.
    
- Reference to Ameerr Shahul's book "The Silent Syndicate," which exposes this trend.
    
- PE investors often pursue quick exits for profit rather than long-term commitment; original doctor-promoters are frequently reduced to minority shareholders.
    
- Industry jargon like EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortisation) and ARPOB (average revenue per occupied bed) reportedly dominate hospital management discourse over patient outcomes.
    
- High institutional land costs combined with absence of priority-sector lending push healthcare providers toward costly commercial financing or private equity, escalating patient costs.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Government Schemes - Limited Impact</mark>

|Scheme|Issue Highlighted|
|---|---|
|Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PMJAY)|Lacks public awareness; private hospitals reluctant to participate due to CGHS bill payment delays|
|Jan Aushadhi Yojana|Cited by government but has not achieved transformative private-sector participation|

- Excessive focus on cost control and limiting health benefit packages has kept PMJAY at a "well begun" stage rather than achieving full impact.
    
- Poor scrutiny of outcomes (money spent versus health results achieved) is flagged as a systemic gap.
    
- Concern raised that PE-driven corporate hospitals could quickly exhaust the ₹5 lakh PMJAY limit without delivering real patient benefit.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">The "Catch-22"</mark>

- More regulatory oversight requires more government spending (non-Plan funds), which further strains an already inadequate health budget.
    
- Less oversight allows private/PE exploitation to continue unchecked.
    
- The author frames this tension as an unresolved paradox in Indian healthcare policy.
