## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Core Thesis</mark>

The article argues that India's national freedom and sovereignty have always been fundamentally tied to command of the seas, not just territorial control of land. It draws on the 2007 Indian Navy doctrine "Freedom to use the seas: India's maritime military strategy," whose founding premise is "the freedom to use the seas for our national purposes, under all circumstances."

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Context: Why This Matters Now</mark>

- Recent global events (Strait of Hormuz closure by Israeli/U.S. strikes in February 2026, Houthi blockade against Saudi Arabia declared July 20, 2026) have brought naval terminology like chokepoints, sea lanes, and freedom of navigation into mainstream discourse.
    
- These terms were already embedded in Indian naval doctrine since 2007, decades before global events popularized them.
    
- The piece is written on the occasion of India's 80th Independence Day (2026).
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">India as a Maritime Nation - Historical Argument</mark>

- K.M. Panikkar's _India and the Indian Ocean_ (1945) argued India's subjugation was not primarily a land story but a loss of command over the seas.
    
- Colonial conquest began not with land invasion but with ships arriving at ports India could no longer control.
    
- The article suggests India's modern prosperity stems from holding the sea, not just the land.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Ancient and Medieval Maritime Power</mark>

- Satavahanas: built prosperity through Indo-Roman trade via Malabar ports.
    
- Cholas: under Rajendra Chola (11th century), launched a naval expedition against Srivijaya (Southeast Asia) to keep sea lanes free of piracy, not for territorial conquest.
    
- Chinese Song dynasty chronicles recorded embassies from Chola kings, indicating recognition of Indian sea power in East Asian courts.
    
- By the 12th century, roughly a quarter of world economic output is estimated to have flowed through Indian shores.
    
- Decline factors: reduced state patronage for shipbuilding, and religious injunctions against ocean crossing in parts of the subcontinent - creating a vacuum filled by Arab, Chinese, and later European traders.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Resistance Movements - "Doctrine Without the Word"</mark>

These figures practiced what the article calls "asymmetric resistance" to deny adversaries free sea use - the same logic later codified in modern naval doctrine.

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Post-Independence Strategic Evolution</mark>

- Independent India's strategic culture remained land-centric for its first four decades, largely due to the 1962 and 1971 wars being fought on northern/western land borders.
    
- Key naval thinkers who kept the maritime argument alive:
    
    - **Rear Admiral Raja Menon** - pushed through the 1990s–2000s for codified maritime strategy, sea denial, and force structure thinking.
        
    - **Admiral Arun Prakash** - as Chief of Naval Staff and after, advocated indigenous shipbuilding, naval modernisation, and framed maritime power as essential to strategic autonomy.
        

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Timeline of Indian Naval Doctrine</mark>

- Indian Maritime Doctrine - 2004
    
- Freedom to Use the Seas - 2007
    
- Ensuring Secure Seas - 2015
    
- MAHASAGAR vision and Indian Navy Maritime Security Strategy - 2026
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Chokepoints and Global Trade Significance</mark>

- The Indian Ocean has nine major chokepoints.
    
- Over 60% of world maritime trade and about 80% of world oil transit through these chokepoints.
    
- The 2026 Hormuz disruption caused shipping delays and cost spikes, validating assumptions already built into Indian naval doctrine.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Current Naval Leadership Perspective</mark>

- Admiral Krishna Swaminathan, the 27th Chief of the Naval Staff (took charge May 2026), stated the Navy "stands vigilant to protect national interests wherever they are" amid a "challenging, complex, unpredictable, and uncertain" regional security environment.
    
- This statement is framed as a continuation of the 2007 doctrine's core premise rather than a new position.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Key Conceptual Takeaway</mark>

"Freedom to use the seas... under all circumstances" is presented not as a static achievement but as a continuous verb - something exercised and contested afresh every generation, from Kunjali Marakkar's era to the present day.
