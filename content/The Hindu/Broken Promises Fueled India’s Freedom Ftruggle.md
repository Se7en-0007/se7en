## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Background: India's Entry into WWI</mark>

- On August 4, 1914, Britain declared war on Germany, automatically drawing India into World War I as a colony.
    
- Over 1 million Indians fought overseas for Britain, with additional numbers serving within the subcontinent; many never returned.
    
- India possessed one of the largest voluntary armies in the world at the time; of 27,522 new recruits enlisted between August and December 1914, 13,400 came from Punjab alone.
    
- India's massive wartime contribution raised expectations among Indians for greater political rights and eventual self-rule.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Strategic Importance of India to Britain</mark>

- Britain depended heavily on the Indian Army for its campaign against the Ottoman Empire, particularly in Mesopotamia, close to India's frontiers.
    
- There was a risk that Muslim opinion in India could be alienated because Britain was fighting fellow Muslims in the Ottoman Empire.
    
- This military dependence gave India significant bargaining leverage, reinforcing Indian expectations of political reward after the war.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Congress Demands and Political Mood</mark>

- At its Lucknow session in 1916, the Indian National Congress passed a resolution urging Britain to declare its "aim and intention" to confer self-government on India at an early date.
    
- Britain ignored this resolution formally but could not ignore the growing unrest and aspirations behind it.
    
- In 1917, Madan Mohan Malaviya declared in the Imperial Legislative Council that the war had "put the clock... fifty years forward," reflecting widespread optimism about imminent reforms.
    
- Austen Chamberlain, then Secretary of State for India, argued India should be represented at the Imperial War Conference, given the scale of Indian sacrifice on distant battlefronts.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms</mark>

- Edwin Montagu succeeded Chamberlain as Secretary of State, and Lord Chelmsford became Viceroy; together they introduced the Montagu-Chelmsford reforms.
    
- Key feature: the central legislature became bicameral, comprising the Imperial Legislative Assembly (lower house) and the Council of State (upper house).
    
- Despite this structural change, the powers of members were severely limited, and the Viceroy retained extensive powers to suspend or override the legislature, rendering its actual role largely ineffective ("nugatory").
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Unequal Treatment in Commemoration</mark>

- Britain established the Imperial War Graves Commission with a principle of uniform headstones and individual recording of names for its own dead, regardless of rank.
    
- Indian soldiers were treated with less honour: at the Memorial of the Missing at Basra (commemorating the Mesopotamia campaign), all British soldiers (around 8,000) and all 665 Indian officers were recorded by name.
    
- In stark contrast, 33,222 other Indian ranks were recorded only as numbers, not by name — reflecting institutional racial discrimination even in death.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Post-War Repression: Rowlatt Act</mark>

- The end of the war brought neither prosperity nor political fulfillment; by 1919, India faced economic hardship and growing unrest, and Indianisation of the armed services remained extremely slow (by 1930, no Indian officer held a rank higher than captain).
    
- In March 1919 — just six months before the constitutional reforms were introduced — the emergency Defence of India Regulations were made permanent through the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, commonly called the Rowlatt Act.
    
- Basis: recommendations of a committee headed by Justice Sir Sidney Rowlatt.
    
- Key provisions: allowed the government to imprison or deport political activists without trial for up to two years; possessing a "seditious" newspaper could itself be treated as evidence of guilt; imposed strict press control and trial without jury.
    
- Mahatma Gandhi denounced the Rowlatt Act as the "Black Act."
    
- In response, Gandhi launched his first nationwide Satyagraha and called for a nationwide hartal on April 6, 1919.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Jallianwala Bagh Massacre</mark>

- A further, more severe blow to hopes attached to the reforms came with the massacre at Amritsar (Jallianwala Bagh) on April 13, 1919.
    
- This event, following closely after the Rowlatt Act, symbolized the brutal end of any illusions about British goodwill toward Indian political aspirations.
    

## <mark style="background: #CACFD9A6;">Overall Historical Significance</mark>

|Aspect|Wartime Promise|Post-War Reality|
|---|---|---|
|Political reform|Expectation of self-government (Congress, 1916 Lucknow resolution)|Montagu-Chelmsford reforms with limited, largely powerless legislature|
|Military recognition|High dependence on Indian troops, hopes of equal treatment|Rowlatt Act repression; discriminatory war memorials (unnamed Indian soldiers)|
|Public sentiment|Optimism ("clock put fifty years forward" — Malaviya, 1917)|Widespread disillusionment, mass Satyagraha, and Jallianwala Bagh massacre|

- The cumulative effect of unmet promises, repressive legislation, and colonial violence caused a profound erosion of Indian faith in British rule.
    
- This sequence (wartime sacrifice, limited reforms, the Rowlatt Act, and the Jallianwala Bagh massacre) is widely regarded as a decisive turning point that radicalised the Indian independence movement, shifting it from constitutional demands toward mass mobilisation under Gandhi's leadership.
